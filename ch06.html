Assoc. Prof. Junjun Zheng: who he is

When and how long: Saturday morning, 45 minutes.

What he works on, in plain words

  1. Availability and software rejuvenation. Software slowly gets worse as it runs (memory leaks and so on). This is called aging. Rejuvenation means restarting it on purpose before it fails. Checkpointing means saving its state so you lose less when it does fail. He builds probability models of this and finds the restart timing that keeps the system up the most. Many papers from 2012 to 2022, for example in IEEE Transactions on Reliability (2017), Reliability Engineering and System Safety (2018) and Software Quality Journal (2020).
  2. Markov regenerative processes (MRGP) and non-Markovian models. These are the maths behind point 1. A plain Markov model assumes every wait is "memoryless" (exponential). A fixed restart timer is not memoryless, so he needs MRGPs or "phase expansion", a trick that turns a non-exponential wait into a chain of small exponential steps. He also studies sensitivity: how much the answer (for example availability) moves when a parameter changes. Examples: ISSREW 2021, Stochastic Models 2022, Performance Evaluation 2024.
  3. Uncertainty in model answers. If the model's input numbers are only estimates, how unsure is the output? Several papers on "epistemic uncertainty propagation" (2020 to 2025).
  4. Software reliability models. NHPP models, Hawkes (self-exciting) models, kernel regression, bootstrap, boosting, metrics-based models. Mostly with Prof. Dohi, Dr. Wu and doctoral students. One of these is in your proposal: Qiu, Dohi, Wu, Zheng, Okamura (IJMEMS 2026), your reference [3].
  5. Security and networks. Intrusion-tolerant systems, malware classification with neural networks, 5G handover modelled with stochastic Petri nets (INFOCOM 2026).
  6. LLMs. The N-version programming paper with generative AI (your reference [1], he is first author) and a 2026 multi-agent LLM paper (he is a co-author). The lab's page says they now ask whether generative AI can make N-version programming stronger, and how several agents should work together.

What he will probably care about in the call

These are my guesses from his work, not things he said.

  1. Is the probability model sound? He builds Markov and non-Markov models for a living. Expect "what exactly is random here?", "what do you assume?", "why a Beta distribution?". Know your beta-binomial cold (see QUESTIONS.md, Q4 to Q6).
  2. How sure are your numbers? He has papers on sensitivity and on uncertainty in model outputs. Your answer: the bootstrap that resamples runs gives intervals for the correlation and for the vote failure chance, not just one number.
  3. The "best number" question. His rejuvenation papers find the best timing that maximises availability. Your plan finds the number of runs N that minimises cost plus expected failure cost. Same kind of question. You can say that, plainly.
  4. How your study extends his N-version paper. He varied temperature and conversation history reuse. Your plan varies the context file, the agent, the model and the temperature, on real tasks.
  5. Can you do it in two years, and is your maths ready? He teaches linear algebra and decision-making to undergraduates. Be honest that your only statistics course is BCS301.
  6. The application itself. The Consent Form, the research plan on the university form, and the oral exam.

3 papers to skim before the call

1. Can Generative AI Enhance the Effectiveness of N-Version Programming? (read this one first)

J. Zheng, H. Okamura, T. Dohi. SFPVV 2025, LNCS 16356, pp. 1-16, Springer, online 18 Feb 2026. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-95-6032-5_1

  • They had LLMs write several versions of the same program, then checked how correct and how different the versions were.
  • They compared a strong model (GPT-4o) with weaker ones, and tried two ways to get more variety: changing the temperature and reusing the conversation history.
  • Voting made the strong model's results more reliable but helped little with weak models, and more variety did not always mean more reliability.

Note: this is behind a paywall. Only the abstract has been read so far. Do not quote any number from it. It is fine to ask him for a copy (see ASK_HIM.md).

2. Availability Analysis of Software Systems with Rejuvenation and Checkpointing

J. Zheng, H. Okamura, T. Dohi. Mathematics 9(8), 846, 2021. Open access. https://doi.org/10.3390/math9080846

  • A system that ages gets restarted on a timer (rejuvenation) and saves its state (checkpointing), and the operator can make mistakes while checkpointing.
  • They model it with a stochastic Petri reward net. The result is not a standard semi-Markov or Markov regenerative model, so they solve it with phase expansion.
  • They find the restart timing that gives the highest long-run availability, and show that operator mistakes lower availability and clearly shift the best timing.

3. Toward Scalable LLM-Based Multi-Agent Collaboration: A Dynamic Task Graph Approach with Asynchronous Parallel Execution

J. Yu, Y. Ding, J. Dai, J. Zheng, J. Wu, H. Sato. Electronics 15(11), 2475, 2026. Open access. Zheng is 4th of 6 authors. https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15112475

  • DynTaskMAS lets many LLM agents work in parallel by keeping a task graph that is updated while the work runs, instead of a fixed step-by-step pipeline.
  • It has four parts: a task splitter, a scheduler that hands ready tasks to free agents, a shared-context layer, and a controller that adjusts priorities to the load.
  • Against step-by-step baselines it cut execution time by 21.3 to 33.0%, raised resource use from 65% to 88%, and gave 3.47 times the throughput with 16 agents.

Optional 4th, if you have time: On the sensitivity of stationary solutions of Markov regenerative processes

J. Zheng, H. Okamura, T. Dohi. Performance Evaluation 164, 102397, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.peva.2024.102397 (free preprint: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4459340) I could not open the abstract, so I only know the title. From the title: it asks how much the long-run behaviour of an MRGP changes when its parameters change. Skim the preprint's first page before you mention it at all.
