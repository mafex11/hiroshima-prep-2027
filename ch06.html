Part 2: Your 15-minute talk

Chapter 6 of 45

Full script: oral examination presentation

When and how long: Out loud, timed: three times on Sat 14 Nov, then once a day.

Hiroshima University, Informatics and Data Science Program, Master's, April 2027 (special selection for international students outside Japan). Online, on Teams or Zoom, on a day the Program sets between 24 November and 2 December 2026.

Deck: Sudhanshu_Pandit_Hiroshima_oral_exam.pdf (present this) and .pptx (same slides, with this script in the speaker notes).

How to use this script

  • Prof. Zheng said on 2 November: about 10 minutes on your research, then 5 minutes on your research proposal, then about 15 minutes of questions. You have no published research of your own, so "your research" here is what you have built and found so far. It is told as the path that led you to your research question.
  • The full version below is about 14 minutes with the greeting, and about 13:30 on the slides. That leaves a little slack inside 15.
  • Speak at a calm 115 to 120 words a minute. The panel are not native English speakers. Pause for one breath at every slide change.
  • Lines in > quote blocks are what you say. Lines in [square brackets] are actions. Do not say them.
  • Under each slide, the Cue line is enough to get going again if you lose your place.
  • Read it aloud until it feels like your own words. Then present from the slides, not from this page.

How the cuts are marked inside the slides:

  • [cut in 10]: skip this paragraph in the 10-minute version.
  • [cut in 5]: skip this paragraph in the 5-minute version.
  • [cut in 10 and 5]: skip it in both short versions.
  • [10 only], [5 only], [10 and 5 only]: say this short line instead, only in that version.
  • Slides 2 to 7 are not in the 5-minute version. From slide 1, jump straight to slide 8.

Where each version cuts, in one line:

  • 10-minute version: the plan (slides 8 to 13) stays whole. Slides 1 to 7 get shorter: a short opening, one line on the paper, one line for Magic Editor, a short Yuki 2.0, no migration story, and a short bridge.
  • 5-minute version: a short opening on slide 1, then slides 8 to 13 with small cuts: 387 to 131, the five rules, the picture and the vote line on slide 11, the open data line, and the "open to adjusting" line. The numbers you skip are still on the screen.

Slide 14 (references) is a backup. Do not present it. Open it only if someone asks where a number comes from.

Slide 4. BrandMemory (about 1:20 · running total 5:05) (not in the 5-minute version)

Cue: learns a company's voice; logs said applied, output showed nothing; traced the prompt; 3,200 limit cut the voice first; one A/B pair; now I check

The second is BrandMemory. It learns a company's writing voice from its website. Then it gives those writing rules to the model, so that Magic Editor writes like that company.

Then I saw a problem. The logs said the brand guidance was applied. But the output had no trace of the brand voice. With BrandMemory on and off, the texts looked almost the same.

So I traced what the model actually received. There was a limit of 3,200 characters. The brand voice section had the lowest priority, so it was the first part cut. The rules were written down, but the model never saw them.

[cut in 10] After the fix, I compared one pair of demos, with BrandMemory off and on. Contractions went from 6 to 0, and "you" and "your" from 15 to 1, as the rules asked. It is one pair, not a study.

Since then, I always check whether a model actually follows an instruction.

Part 2. Research plan

[10-minute mark in the full version is about here, at 8:40. The plan takes about 5:30.]

Slide 8. Background (about 1:10 · running total 10:05)

Cue: vote helps only if versions fail on different inputs; Zheng, Okamura and Dohi; 429 vs 115; 387 to 131; 0.43 and 0.27; no context file

In N-version programming, a vote helps only if the versions rarely fail on the same inputs.

Prof. Zheng, Prof. Okamura and Prof. Dohi asked whether generative AI can make N-version programming more effective. Voting made strong models, such as GPT-4o, more reliable, but helped little with weak ones. More diversity did not always mean higher reliability.

Two 2026 studies found that versions written by language models fail together more often than independence predicts. In one, two or more of 48 agent-written programs failed together on 429 of a million inputs, where independence predicts about 115.

[cut in 5] Voting over three still cut the mean failure count from 387 to 131.

In the other, voting over three gave only 0.43 of the gain that independence would allow.

None of these studies used a context file.

Slide 9. Two research questions (about 0:40 · running total 10:45)

Cue: RQ1 how much more often, which change lowers it; RQ2 one rule replaced, tests and beyond

So I ask two questions.

RQ1. When several agent runs solve one real task with the same context file, how much more often do they fail on the same inputs than independence predicts? And which change between runs lowers that most: the file, the agent, the model, or the temperature?

RQ2. If I replace one real rule with neutral text, does the reliability of the code change, on the pull request's tests and on generated inputs beyond them?

Slide 10. Method (about 1:00 · running total 11:45)

Cue: merged PRs, tests fail before and pass after; 138 tasks, 5 rules x 2 tasks; original or one rule replaced; 10 runs per setting; admitted if tests pass; 10,000 inputs vs merged code; 820 runs

Here is the method. I take real merged pull requests from Python projects, starting from 138 public tasks. Their tests fail before the change and pass after it.

[cut in 5] I pick about five rules that a script can check, with two tasks each.

The agent gets the original context file, or the file with one rule replaced. Each setting runs ten times: Claude Code, Codex with two models, and my own harness with an open-weight model at two temperatures. A run counts only if it passes the pull request's tests.

Then each version gets at least 10,000 random inputs, and I compare its answers with the merged code. That makes 820 runs in total, about 420 to 590 US dollars.

Slide 11. The model (about 1:10 · running total 12:55)

Cue: theta of x; p squared plus variance; independent only if every input equally hard; picture 4 vs 8 percent; Beta, beta-binomial, rho; vote and number of runs

[Point at the formula when you say "p squared, plus the variance of theta". See PRESENTATION_GUIDE.md, section 6.]

How do I measure "fail together"? Let theta of x be the chance that a run fails on input x, and p its mean. Two runs both fail with probability p squared, plus the variance of theta. So the runs are independent only if every input is equally hard. This is Eckhardt and Lee's result.

[cut in 5] In the picture, p is 0.2 in both cases. If every input is equally hard, two runs both fail 4 percent of the time. If half are easy and half are hard, it is 8 percent.

I model theta with a Beta distribution. The number of failing runs on one input is then beta-binomial, and one number, rho, measures the dependence. Zero means independent.

[cut in 5] The model also gives the failure chance of a two-out-of-three vote, and the best number of runs.

Slide 12. Schedule and expected results (about 0:45 · running total 13:40)

Cue: courses, harness, pilot; main runs and RQ2; model and first paper; thesis; two results; open data

In the first half of Year 1, I take probability, statistics and reliability courses, build the harness, and run a pilot. Then come the main runs and RQ2. In Year 2, I fit the shared-failure model, write a first paper, and then the thesis.

I expect two results. First, a fitted model, so a team can choose how many runs to vote over at a given cost. Second, an answer on whether one real rule changes reliability beyond the tests.

[cut in 5] All tasks, code and data will be public.

Slide 13. What I want to learn (about 0:40 · running total 14:20)

Cue: RQ1 from the N-version study; RQ2 uses the boundary value method; field data with pysrat; I build agents, I want to learn reliability models; thank you

To close. RQ1 takes this lab's N-version study to real repository tasks. RQ2 uses the lab's boundary value method as a stress test. I can build and run the agents. What I cannot do yet is fit and test reliability models on their failures. That is what I want to learn in this lab.

[cut in 5] I am also open to adjusting the topic to fit the lab's research.

Thank you very much for listening. I am happy to take your questions.
