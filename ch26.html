5. LLMs and coding agents

Q15. What does temperature do mathematically, and why can two runs at temperature 0 still differ?

The model gives a score z_i to every possible next token and turns the scores into probabilities with a softmax, p_i = exp(z_i/T) / Σ_j exp(z_j/T). A low T makes the top token more likely, a high T flattens the choice, and as T goes to 0 the choice becomes the single most likely token.

Why he asks: your plan varies temperature and treats runs as random draws, so he will ask where the randomness comes from. On paper: two tokens with scores 2 and 1. At T = 1 the top token gets 1/(1 + e^(−1)) ≈ 0.73. At T = 0.5 it gets 1/(1 + e^(−2)) ≈ 0.88. At T = 2 it gets 1/(1 + e^(−0.5)) ≈ 0.62. If he pushes ("Why can T = 0 still differ?"): "As far as I know, GPU arithmetic done in a different order or batch can change the scores very slightly and flip a near-tie, and a provider can update the model behind the same name. For an agent, the tool results can also differ between runs, so my plan treats each setting as a distribution of runs, not one fixed output." If he pushes ("Can you set it in Claude Code or Codex?"): "As far as I know, neither CLI documents a temperature setting, so I vary temperature only in my own harness." Note for you: [CHECK] before you say anything specific about one provider's sampling.

6. CS basics

Q17. Problem: 10 tasks x 7 settings x 10 versions x 10,000 inputs. How many function calls is that, how would you run them fast and safely (timeouts, infinite loops, sandboxing), and how would you store and compare the outputs?

With one entry point per task it is 10 × 7 × 10 × 10,000 = 7,000,000 calls, if all 10 runs in every setting are admitted, plus 100,000 calls of the merged code as the reference. I would run each version in its task's Docker container with no network and fixed CPU and memory limits, load it once in a worker process, and give every call a timeout, because an infinite loop can't be detected in general, only stopped. Why he asks: the exam tests "application capability, and problem-solving abilities" (guideline VI.2). 7. Probability and statistics

Q18. You studied hypothesis tests and ANOVA in BCS301, and your plan cites "not significant, p = 0.21". What is a p-value, what are Type I and Type II errors, why is "not significant" not the same as "no effect", and how do you handle about 70 fits? What would a one-way ANOVA test here, and why use a mixed model with task as a random effect instead?

A p-value is the chance, if the null hypothesis is true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one we saw. It is not the chance that the null is true. A Type I error is rejecting a true null, a false alarm, at rate α, and a Type II error is missing a real effect, at rate β, so power is 1 − β. So "not significant, p = 0.21" for the 2.4% gain in Gloaguen et al. means the data cannot rule out zero, but cannot rule out a small real gain either, so I would look at the size of the effect and its interval.

Why he asks: your plan names these topics as your preparation, so he will test exactly them. If he pushes ("And about 70 fits?"): "10 tasks times 7 settings is 70 fits. At α = 0.05, about 3 or 4 tests would look significant by chance alone even if nothing were going on, so I would correct with Holm or Benjamini-Hochberg, or better, pool the tasks in one model and report intervals." If he pushes ("One-way ANOVA? Why a mixed model?"): "A one-way ANOVA would test whether the mean failure probability is the same across groups, for example the 7 settings, assuming independent observations, normal errors and equal variance. But runs on the same task are not independent, because tasks differ a lot in difficulty, and I want to say something beyond these 10 tasks. So I treat task as a random effect, which models the spread between tasks, and since the outcome is a proportion I would probably use a logistic mixed model." [CONFIRM: add "I have not fitted one myself yet" unless you have.] If he pushes ("How would you test the beta-binomial's fit?"): "I would group the inputs by how many of the k runs failed, 0 to k, and compare the observed counts with the fitted model's expected counts in a chi-square goodness-of-fit test, merging small cells. Q19. Bayes' theorem problem: 10% of runs break a rule, your checker flags 95% of real violations and wrongly flags 10% of compliant runs. What is P(real violation | flagged)?

About 0.51. P(flagged) = 0.1 × 0.95 + 0.9 × 0.10 = 0.095 + 0.09 = 0.185, so P(violation | flagged) = 0.095 / 0.185 ≈ 0.51. About half the flags are false alarms, because compliant runs are so much more common. That is why my plan checks each checker against my own hand labels and reports Cohen's kappa, instead of trusting its flags.

Why he asks: conditional probability is core exam material, and the example is your own checker. If he pushes ("And if it doesn't flag?"): "P(violation | not flagged) = (0.1 × 0.05) / (0.1 × 0.05 + 0.9 × 0.90) = 0.005 / 0.815 ≈ 0.006."

Q20. The Beta distribution is the conjugate prior of the binomial. If θ ~ Beta(a, b) and y of k runs fail on an input, what is the posterior, and what would a Bayesian view add to your plan?

The posterior is Beta(a + y, b + k − y), with mean (a + y)/(a + b + k). That mean is a weighted average of the prior mean a/(a + b) and the data y/k, with weights a + b and k. In my plan, the Beta fitted over all inputs can act as the prior, so each input's θ is pulled towards the overall mean, and 0 failures in 10 runs no longer has to mean θ = 0. A hierarchical Bayesian model could also share information across tasks and settings, and give intervals for ρ and for the vote failure chance.

On paper: prior ∝ θ^(a−1)(1 − θ)^(b−1), likelihood ∝ θ^y (1 − θ)^(k−y), product ∝ θ^(a+y−1)(1 − θ)^(b+k−y−1), which is Beta(a + y, b + k − y). Example: Beta(1, 9) has mean 0.1. With y = 3 of k = 10, the posterior is Beta(4, 16), mean 0.2, between the prior 0.1 and the data 0.3. If he pushes ("How does this relate to your beta-binomial?"): "The beta-binomial is exactly the distribution of y when θ is drawn from this Beta prior."

Q21. Derive a maximum likelihood estimate on paper: p from y failures in n trials, and λ from n exponential lifetimes. Is each unbiased, and what is the variance of p-hat?

For y failures in n trials, log L(p) = y log p + (n − y) log(1 − p) plus a constant, and setting the derivative y/p − (n − y)/(1 − p) to 0 gives p̂ = y/n. It is unbiased, since E[Y] = np, and Var(p̂) = p(1 − p)/n. For n exponential lifetimes, log L(λ) = n log λ − λ Σ t_i, so λ̂ = n / Σ t_i = 1/t̄. It is biased upwards: Σ t_i has a Gamma(n, λ) distribution, so E[λ̂] = nλ/(n − 1), and (n − 1)/Σ t_i is the unbiased version.

On paper: both second derivatives are negative (−y/p² − (n − y)/(1 − p)² and −n/λ²), so both are maxima. Var(p̂) = Var(Y)/n² = np(1 − p)/n². If he pushes ("What about the mean lifetime?"): "The MLE of 1/λ is t̄, and t̄ is unbiased for 1/λ, so the bias is only in λ̂."

Q22. You see 10 failures in 10,000 inputs. Give a 95% confidence interval for the failure probability, and say when the normal approximation breaks.

p̂ = 10/10,000 = 0.001, and the standard error is sqrt(0.001 × 0.999 / 10,000) ≈ 0.00032, so the normal interval is 0.001 ± 1.96 × 0.00032 ≈ 0.001 ± 0.00062, about 0.00038 to 0.00162. The normal approximation works for large counts because of the central limit theorem, but with only 10 failures it is borderline, and with 0 failures it gives the useless interval from 0 to 0. Then I would use a Wilson or Clopper-Pearson interval, or the rule of three, 3/n, as an upper bound when there are no failures.

Why he asks: it is the classroom version of "how sure are your numbers". On paper: Wilson gives about 0.00054 to 0.00184, and Clopper-Pearson about 0.00048 to 0.00184. 8. Markov chains and reliability basics

Q23. A two-state up/down system has failure rate λ and repair rate μ. Write the generator matrix, solve πQ = 0 and derive the availability. Also find the stationary distribution of a 2x2 discrete-time chain as an eigenvector.

With state 0 up and state 1 down, Q = [[−λ, λ], [μ, −μ]]. πQ = 0 gives λπ0 = μπ1, and with π0 + π1 = 1 that gives π0 = μ/(λ + μ). So the steady-state availability is A = μ/(λ + μ), which is MTTF/(MTTF + MTTR) with MTTF = 1/λ and MTTR = 1/μ.

Why he asks: Prof. Zheng works on availability and teaches Basic Linear Algebra. On paper: λ = 0.001 per hour and μ = 0.1 per hour give A = 0.1/0.101 ≈ 0.990, the same as MTTF 1,000 hours and MTTR 10 hours in the primer. Starting from up, A(t) = μ/(λ + μ) + λ/(λ + μ) e^(−(λ + μ)t). If he pushes ("Now a discrete-time chain"): "For P = [[1 − a, a], [b, 1 − b]], the stationary distribution π solves πP = π, so it is a left eigenvector of P for eigenvalue 1, scaled to sum to 1. The balance a π0 = b π1 gives π = (b/(a + b), a/(a + b)). With a = 0.1 and b = 0.5 that is (5/6, 1/6), about (0.833, 0.167). The other eigenvalue is 1 − a − b = 0.4, which sets how fast the chain forgets where it started."

Q24. Define the reliability function R(t), the density f(t), the hazard h(t) and MTTF = ∫R(t)dt. Show that the exponential has constant hazard and is memoryless. What does the Weibull shape parameter do, what is the bathtub curve, and why doesn't software wear out? Why does a fixed rejuvenation timer break the Markov assumption?

R(t) = P(T > t) is the chance of surviving past time t, f(t) = −R′(t) is the density of the failure time, and the hazard h(t) = f(t)/R(t) is the failure rate at t given survival so far, with MTTF = E[T] = ∫₀^∞ R(t) dt. For the exponential, R(t) = e^(−λt) and f(t) = λe^(−λt), so h(t) = λ is constant. It is memoryless because P(T > s + t | T > s) = e^(−λ(s + t)) / e^(−λs) = e^(−λt) = P(T > t).

If he pushes ("Weibull and the bathtub curve?"): "For the Weibull, R(t) = exp(−(t/η)^β) and h(t) = (β/η)(t/η)^(β−1). If β < 1 the hazard falls, which fits early failures, β = 1 is the exponential, and β > 1 means wear-out. The bathtub curve puts these together for hardware: many early failures, then a flat period, then wear-out." If he pushes ("Why doesn't software wear out?"): "The code does not change by itself. Its failures come from design faults that certain inputs trigger, and its reliability changes only when the code changes, which is why reliability growth models describe it as faults are fixed. But a long-running process can age, through things like memory leaks, and that is why rejuvenation exists." If he pushes ("Why does a fixed timer break the Markov assumption?"): "A continuous-time Markov chain needs every waiting time to be exponential, so the future depends only on the current state. A fixed timer is not memoryless, because the time left depends on how long you have already waited. As far as I know, your work handles this with Markov regenerative processes, or with phase expansion, which replaces the fixed time with a chain of exponential steps. For example, k steps each with rate k/δ give mean δ and variance δ²/k, which goes to 0 as k grows." Note for you: you know only the idea of this work, not the details (09 Q17 says you know only the title of his MRGP sensitivity paper). Say so if he goes deeper.

Q25. Triple modular redundancy with exponential units and a perfect voter: R_TMR = 3R² − 2R³. Compute its MTTF and compare it with one unit. When is TMR worse? Also give the reliability of series and parallel systems.

MTTF = ∫₀^∞ (3e^(−2λt) − 2e^(−3λt)) dt = 3/(2λ) − 2/(3λ) = 5/(6λ), which is less than 1/λ for one unit. TMR is better only while R > 0.5, because 3R² − 2R³ > R reduces to (2R − 1)(R − 1) < 0, which means t < ln 2/λ ≈ 0.69/λ. So it helps for short missions but is worse over long ones, and it is also worse if the units share a cause of failure or the voter can fail. In series R = Π R_i, so with exponential units the rates add, and in parallel R = 1 − Π(1 − R_i).

Why he asks: it is the same 2-out-of-3 formula as in your plan, in time form. On paper: with a voter that can fail, R_sys = R_voter × (3R² − 2R³). Two identical exponential units in parallel have MTTF = 2/λ − 1/(2λ) = 3/(2λ). If he pushes ("How is this different from your vote?"): "Here the units fail independently over time, and R is the same for every input. In my plan θ changes from input to input, so the runs fail together more often, and the vote gains less than this formula promises."

Q26. Define fault, error and failure, and the attributes of dependability. What is the difference between fault tolerance and fault avoidance, between N-version programming and recovery blocks, and between reliability and availability?

A fault is the cause, for example a bug in the code. An error is a wrong internal state that the fault produces when it is triggered, and a failure is when the service the user gets differs from the correct service. Dependability usually covers availability, reliability, safety, integrity and maintainability, with confidentiality added for security. Fault avoidance tries to keep faults out through good design, reviews and testing, while fault tolerance accepts that some faults stay in and uses redundancy to keep the service correct anyway.

Why he asks: the lab's research page says "Dependable denotes a generalized notion of high reliability that unifies fault tolerance and fault avoidance", and the official supervisor list includes fault-tolerant and dependable computing. If he pushes ("N-version programming versus recovery blocks?"): "N-version programming runs N versions together and takes a majority vote, with no acceptance test. Recovery blocks run one version, check its result with an acceptance test, and if the test fails, go back to a checkpoint and try an alternate, one after another. My plan has a bit of both: a run is admitted only if it passes the pull request's tests, like an acceptance test, and then the admitted runs vote." If he pushes ("Reliability versus availability?"): "Reliability is the chance of no failure over a time interval, R(t). Availability is the chance the system is up at a given time, or the long-run share of up-time. A system that fails often but is repaired fast can have high availability and low reliability." Note for you: [CHECK] these definitions against a dependability textbook before the exam. They follow the usual fault, error and failure terms, but no source for them is in the kit.
