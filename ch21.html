Hard questions from other professors on your plan

When and how long: Thu 19 Nov: H1 to H25. Fri 20 Nov: H26 to H47.

What this file is: 47 new questions about your research plan, the kind a professor from outside the lab, or a skeptic, would ask. They are different from the 55 questions in 07_questions_on_my_proposal.md (Q1 to Q55); where one builds on an old question, it says so. Each has a short spoken answer in your voice and the one point the professor wants to hear.

How to use it:

  • Read the "What the professor wants to hear" line first. If your own words hit that point, your answer is fine, even if it isn't the model answer.
  • Say the first sentence, then stop. These professors want to see how you think under pressure, not a speech.
  • If you don't know: "I don't know yet. I'd find out by ..." is always a good answer here.
  • Facts come from the paper texts in /Users/mafex/code/personal/masters-research/2027-refresh/hiroshima-application/qbank-2026-11-01/paper_text/ (checked 12 Nov 2026 and again on 13 Nov; see 04_My_Proposal/04_every_number_in_my_plan.md). [YOU: ...] marks something only you can check.

Who asks what: the professors in the meeting will likely include Prof. Zheng, and may include Prof. Okamura, Prof. Dohi and faculty from other labs in the programme. The roles below are guesses, to help you practise the tone. Professors from outside the lab ask the "so what" and "why should I believe it" questions. Lab members ask the modelling ones.

A. Novelty and contribution

H1. (Another professor) In one sentence: what will we know after your thesis that we don't know now?

How strongly repeated coding-agent runs on real repository tasks fail together, as a size with an interval, and which change between runs lowers it most. From that, how many runs are worth voting over at a given cost.

What the professor wants to hear: a measured quantity and a decision, not "I will study X".

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You say 'a size with an interval'. Ron et al. already report z = 29.2. Why isn't that the size?"
    You: "Because z is a test statistic. It says how surprising the joint failures are under independence, and it grows with the number of inputs: with twice the inputs and the same failure pattern, z grows by the square root of two. ρ is a size between 0 and 1 that doesn't grow with the inputs; more inputs only narrow its interval, so I can compare it across tasks and settings."
  2. Prof: "Compare it across tasks? ρ depends on the inputs you chose to generate. Change the input distribution and ρ changes. So it isn't a property of the agent at all, is it?"
    You: "You're right, ρ belongs to one task, one setting and one input distribution, just like a failure probability. So I write the distribution down before the runs, and what I compare is the ranking: whether changing the agent lowers ρ more than changing the temperature. I'd check that ranking again under a second, narrower distribution, for example values close to the ones in the task's tests."
  3. Prof: "Then which input distribution should it be, in reliability terms?"
    You: "Ideally the operational profile, how the function is really called in use, which Eckhardt and Lee call the usage distribution Q. I don't have usage data for these functions, so my numbers are failure probabilities under a stated test profile, not field reliability, and I'll call them that. Logging real calls in a project would give a real profile, but that's beyond this plan."

H2. (Another professor) Take the context file out of your title. What's left, and is it still a thesis?

RQ1 is still there: coincident failures of agent runs on real tasks, ten runs per setting, and a fitted model of how often they fail together. The file is what makes it specific: it's the part of the specification every run shares, and RQ2 tests one rule directly. Without the file it's a careful follow-up of Ron et al. on real tasks. With it, it's a question I haven't seen asked, as far as I know.

What the professor wants to hear: knows which part is the core (the RQ1 model) and which part is the hook (the file).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Without the file you call it 'a careful follow-up of Ron et al. on real tasks'. They had a precise specification and a million inputs. Isn't one clean specification simply better science?"
    You: "It's cleaner, and that's their strength: one small program, a long written specification and a trusted reference. Real tasks are changes inside an existing code base, with a short task text and the project's own helpers, so shared failures can have other causes, for example an old helper that every run reuses. One clean specification can't show that."
  2. Prof: "If every run reuses the project's helper, the developer who merged the change probably used it too. Then your reference has the same bug, and you can't see it."
    You: "Yes, that's the limit I name in my plan: differential testing can't see a bug the merged code shares. I only see the cases where the developer did something different from the runs, so I undercount exactly this kind of shared failure."
  3. Prof: "Can you estimate how much you undercount?"
    You: "Not with differential testing alone. For a few tasks I could add a check that doesn't use the merged code: simple properties written from the task text, for example that a discount never changes the sign of a price. Where that check catches the merged code too, I get a rough size of the blind spot, but only for the properties I thought of."

H3. (Prof. Okamura) The beta-binomial is a textbook model. What is new in your modelling?

The model isn't new, and I don't claim it is. It's a standard model for counts that vary more than a binomial allows. What's new is using it as the distribution of Eckhardt and Lee's θ for agent runs, fitting it on real tasks, and turning it into a vote failure probability and a cost-optimal number of runs.

What the professor wants to hear: honesty that the tool is standard; the contribution is the application and the decision.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why should θ follow a Beta at all? I would expect most inputs never to fail."
    You: "It may not. I expect most inputs to have θ equal to zero and a few to be hard, and one Beta can struggle with a spike at zero plus a separate group of hard inputs. So I'd also fit a zero-inflated beta-binomial, where a share of inputs never fails, and compare the fits by AIC."
  2. Prof: "Does that choice change any conclusion, or only the fit? Show me a case where the Beta gets the vote wrong."
    You: "It can change the conclusion. A Beta with mean below one half always puts more weight below one half than at the same distance above it, which is the asymmetry condition of Eckhardt and Lee's Theorem 3, so it always predicts that a majority vote does no worse than one run. But if 2% of inputs have θ = 0.8 and the rest zero, one run fails with probability 0.016 and a 2-out-of-3 vote with about 0.018, so voting hurts. A Beta with the same mean and ρ predicts about 0.0156, so it would say voting helps."
  3. Prof: "So what would you fit instead, and how would you know it's right?"
    You: "A mixture: a share of inputs that never fail, plus one or two Beta groups, one of which may sit above one half. I wouldn't trust AIC alone: with ten runs, the counts give E[θ²] and E[θ³] directly, with no model, so I'd first check that the fitted model reproduces the observed vote failure before I use it for larger N."

H4. (Another professor) Everyone already knows LLM outputs are correlated. So what?

We know they're not independent. We don't know how strongly on real repository tasks, or what lowers it. And the size matters for decisions: in Nogueira et al., voting got 0.43 of the possible gain overall, but only 0.27 when all three versions came from one model. A team choosing between rerunning the same agent and switching agents needs that number for its own setting.

What the professor wants to hear: the difference between "not independent" and "how much, and what to do about it".

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "What exactly is 'the possible gain' behind that 0.43?"
    You: "Nogueira et al. call it redundancy effectiveness: the gain the vote really gave over the average single version, divided by the gain it would have given if failures were independent. So 0.43 means voting got a bit less than half of what independence promises. Overall reliability rose only from 0.88 to 0.90 with three versions."
  2. Prof: "From 0.88 to 0.90 is tiny. Wouldn't a team just conclude that voting is useless?"
    You: "For contest problems and strong models, maybe. Nogueira et al. even advise benchmarking the strongest single model first, because no combination of models beat it. But their versions were mostly correct already, so there was little room to gain, and on real repository tasks I can't assume the same, which is exactly what I want to measure."
  3. Prof: "Then why not just run the strongest agent once and skip voting?"
    You: "Sometimes that's the right answer, and my model can say when: if runs share most of their failures, the cost-optimal number of runs comes out as one. But a team doesn't know in advance whether its one run is a good one. When several runs agree on generated inputs, that's evidence about the patch, as long as they don't all share the same bug."

H5. (Prof. Dohi) Eckhardt and Lee already asked whether an optimal number of versions exists. Isn't your "best N" old?

Yes, the question is old. Their report has a figure on the existence of an optimal N, and a theorem on when N versions beat one on average. What's new is estimating the distribution of θ from real agent runs, and adding the cost of each run. Their N minimises the failure probability. Mine minimises run cost plus expected failure cost.

What the professor wants to hear: knows the classic result (NASA TM-86369, Theorem 3 and Figure 4, "Existence of optimal N") and states the difference plainly.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "In their Figure 4, why does the failure probability go up again after some N?"
    You: "In that example the best N was 7, and beyond it the failure probability rose. That can only happen when some inputs have θ above one half: there most versions are wrong, so a bigger vote makes a wrong majority more certain. As N grows, the failure probability tends to the probability of those inputs."
  2. Prof: "Once you add the run cost, can your best N ever be larger than theirs?"
    You: "No. Past their N, the run cost is higher and the failure probability is no lower, so the total cost can only be higher. So my best N is at most theirs, and smaller when runs are expensive compared with failures."
  3. Prof: "But nobody knows the cost of a failure. How is your N useful then?"
    You: "So I wouldn't give one N, but the cost ratio at which two extra runs start to pay. For example, with independent runs that fail with probability 0.1, going from one run to three cuts the failure probability from 0.1 to 0.028, so it pays when a failure costs more than about 28 runs. With ρ = 0.3 the vote still fails with about 0.073, and the break-even rises to about 74 runs."

H6. (Prof. Zheng) My group is already working on related questions. How do you avoid repeating our work?

I'd like to align the topic with you from the start. You said we might adjust it when I join. The part I can bring is the repository side: real tasks, context files, and running agents at scale and checking what they did. If your team's new results already answer part of RQ1, I'd build on them, not repeat them.

What the professor wants to hear: flexibility, not defensiveness; knows what only you can bring.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You say you bring 'running agents at scale and checking what they did'. What have you actually run at scale?"
    You: "Not at the scale of this plan, honestly. I use Claude Code and Codex every day and review what they change, and for my own agent, Yuki for Mac, I built a small evaluation set and grew it from 10 to 36 cases because the 10-case set was too noisy. I also saw how much repeated runs differ: a local 7B model finished one live task cleanly in only 3 of 5 runs."
  2. Prof: "Repeating runs on your own machine is easy. In your study, what could leak from one run to the next?"
    You: "Anything that survives between runs: a cache, files left in the working folder, or notes the agent saves for itself. Claude Code, for example, reads a personal CLAUDE.md in the home folder and by default saves its own memory notes there, so every run starts in a fresh container from one image, with nothing in the home folder. What I can't control is on the provider's side, so I record the CLI version and the model name for every run."
  3. Prof: "How would you check that a run really saw only the file you intended?"
    You: "With a small planted check before the main runs. I'd put a harmless made-up instruction in a test project's file, like adding a fixed word to a comment, and a different one in my own home-folder file on the host machine. If the runs follow the first and never the second, nothing from my machine leaks in, and I'd repeat the check whenever the CLI version changes."

H7. (Another professor) Two close papers came out this summer. What if someone publishes your exact study before April?

Then I'd read it carefully and build on it, and the pilot would change. I'll keep checking arXiv before I start. The parts least likely to be repeated are the fitted dependence model and the cost decision, because the empirical papers so far stop at a test of independence.

What the professor wants to hear: calm, and a plan, not "that won't happen".

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You say the empirical papers 'stop at a test of independence'. Is that fair? Nogueira et al. measure how much of the voting gain is realised. That's a size, not a test."
    You: "That's fair, and I should say it more carefully. Nogueira et al. measure a size with redundancy effectiveness, and Ron et al. report a pairwise correlation between versions. What neither paper does, as far as I've read, is fit a model of how the failure probability varies across inputs, and Nogueira et al. name as a limitation that their baseline treats every test as equally likely to fail."
  2. Prof: "Why does such a model matter in practice, if their number already says voting works badly?"
    You: "Because their number holds only for the sets of versions they built. With a fitted distribution of θ, I can predict the vote for more runs than I actually made, find the cheapest number of runs at a given cost, and see whether the shared failures sit on a few very hard inputs or spread thinly over many."
  3. Prof: "Predicting votes larger than the runs you have sounds risky. How would you check it?"
    You: "With ten runs I can estimate the vote for up to nine runs directly from the counts, with no model. So I'd fit the model on five runs only, predict the vote for seven or nine, and compare with the direct estimate from all ten. If they disagree, I don't trust the model beyond the data."

B. Feasibility in two years

H8. (Another professor) What is the single riskiest step, technically?

Input generation for real functions. Many functions in real repositories take objects, files or settings, not plain numbers and strings. If only a few entry points can be fed with generated inputs, I'll have few usable tasks. The pilot checks this first, before any main runs.

What the professor wants to hear: one concrete risk and an early check, not a list of everything.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Give me an example. What would you do with a function that takes a settings object?"
    You: "If the class is a dataclass with type hints, a property-based testing tool like Hypothesis can build it field by field from the hints, and a file can be a temporary file with generated content. If the function needs a network connection or a live database, I can't generate that honestly, so I skip that entry point and count it as skipped."
  2. Prof: "Random field values will mostly be nonsense that the function just rejects. Then what are your 10,000 inputs worth?"
    You: "That's a real danger: if most inputs fail the argument checks, every version raises the same error and I learn almost nothing. So I'd mix in the values the task's tests use, add constraints from the docstring and from how the code base calls the function, and report what share of inputs gets past the checks."
  3. Prof: "How would you measure that share?"
    You: "With coverage on the merged code. I'd run a sample of the inputs through it one by one with coverage.py and count how many reach the lines the pull request changed. If almost all of them stop at the first check, the generator needs constraints before the main runs."

H9. (Another professor) How long will it take you to prepare one task: the rule checker, the input generator, the hand checks?

I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. The pilot measures it on the first two tasks. If it's much slower than I hope, I cut the extra settings before I cut tasks.

What the professor wants to hear: no fake precision; measure first, then decide what to cut.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why cut the extra settings before tasks? They're half of your RQ1 comparison."
    You: "Because the main runs already vary two things, the file version and the agent, so RQ1 can still compare those two changes, and RQ2 and all ten tasks stay whole. I'd lose the model and temperature comparisons, or run them on fewer tasks, and I'd say so."
  2. Prof: "But keeping all ten tasks means preparing all ten. The checker, the input generator and the hand checks are per task. So cutting settings saves you no preparation time at all, does it?"
    You: "Not per task, you're right. It saves running time, money and the work on my own harness, but every task I keep still needs its checker, input generator and hand checks. If the pilot shows that preparing tasks is the slow part, the honest cut is fewer tasks, for example four rules with two tasks each instead of five."
  3. Prof: "With eight tasks instead of ten, what do you lose for RQ2?"
    You: "The file effect is estimated across tasks, so if the differences between tasks dominate, its interval widens by about the square root of ten over eight, roughly 12%, a bit more with so few tasks. The bigger loss is breadth: four kinds of rule behind the result instead of five."

H10. (Prof. Zheng) Suppose only RQ1 works, and on three tasks. Is that a master's thesis?

I think so, if it's careful: a fitted dependence model with intervals on real agent runs, compared with the Knight and Leveson z and Nogueira's pairwise score, and the vote failure and best N from it. Three tasks would limit what I can claim, and I'd say so clearly. I'd like your view on what is enough.

What the professor wants to hear: knows the minimum result that still stands, and asks the supervisor.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Concretely, what could you claim from three tasks, and what not?"
    You: "I could claim that on those three tasks, with those agents, the runs fail together by this much, with intervals, and how far that is from independence. I couldn't claim an average over tasks, or that one change between runs is better in general. Three tasks are three case studies."
  2. Prof: "Your intervals come from resampling only ten runs per setting. What if one run is very different, say it fails on a third of all inputs?"
    You: "Then that run makes the failures look less clumped: on many inputs exactly one run fails, which looks like independence, so the fitted ρ drops. In a made-up case where seven of nine runs fail together on the same 2% of inputs, adding a tenth run like that drops the fitted ρ from about 0.7 to below 0.1. So I'd also report ρ with each run left out, to show whether one run drives the result."
  3. Prof: "But that run passed the task's tests. Isn't it a legitimate version? Would you drop it?"
    You: "No, I'd keep it. The acceptance test admitted it, and in Eckhardt and Lee's terms it's one draw from the population of versions. The trouble is only that one unusual draw weighs a lot among ten, so I'd show the results with and without it and read that run's code to see what it did differently, for example whether it ignored the rule."

C. Cost and who pays

H11. (Another professor) Your prices are for early-2026 models. By 2027 Sonnet 4.5 and GPT-5.2 may be gone. Then what?

Then I use the versions that exist when I start, pin them, and measure the real cost in the pilot. Nothing in the method depends on one model. The cost estimate is a size check, not a promise, and newer models may cost more: Zhang et al. report about five dollars per task and condition with Claude Opus 4.6.

What the professor wants to hear: the method is model-independent, and the cost is measured again, not assumed.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Five dollars per task and condition. At that price, what do your 820 runs cost?"
    You: "If every run cost five dollars, 820 runs would be 4,100 dollars, about seven to ten times my estimate. But Zhang et al.'s own total is about 2,000 dollars for just over 5,000 runs, which is about 40 cents a run, so I'm not sure their five dollars means one run. That's why the pilot measures the real cost on my tasks before the main runs."
  2. Prof: "What drives the cost of one run? Can you control it?"
    You: "Mostly tokens: how many steps the agent takes and how much code it reads at each step. Gloaguen et al. found that developer context files add about 3.3 steps on average and up to 19% more cost. I can cap the steps or time per run, but a cap changes what the agent does, so I'd set it high and report how many runs hit it."
  3. Prof: "If a run hits the cap, is that a failure, or do you drop it?"
    You: "It counts as a run that produced no version, like a run that fails the task's tests: it lowers the pass rate but isn't admitted. If one file version makes runs hit the cap more often, that's a real effect of the file, so I'd report it next to the pass rate."

H12. (Another professor) If there is no budget at all, what is the smallest study that still answers RQ1?

Only my own harness with the open-weight model, at two temperatures. That keeps RQ1 for one agent with temperature as the change between runs, and RQ2 with the two file versions on that model. I'd lose the comparison across commercial agents, and I'd say that's a real loss. It also needs a GPU. I don't know yet what the lab has, so I'd ask, and whether a master's student can use the university's shared GPU servers.

What the professor wants to hear: a real fallback, and honesty about what it loses.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "What hardware does a 30-billion-parameter model need?"
    You: "It's a mixture of experts: 30.5 billion parameters in total, but each token uses only about 3.3 billion of them, so it's fast but still needs all the weights in memory. In 16-bit that's about 61 gigabytes, so an 80-gigabyte GPU. A 4-bit version is about 19 gigabytes, which fits a 24-gigabyte card if the sessions stay short, with some loss in quality. The university's AI and Data Innovation Center has shared servers with two 48-gigabyte A40 cards each, so the 4-bit version would fit on one card and the 16-bit one would need both cards of one server. What the lab itself has, I don't know yet."
  2. Prof: "A quantised model will pass the tests less often. With few admitted runs, what can you still estimate?"
    You: "With ten runs and, say, four admitted on a task, I still have six pairs and four triples, so I can estimate joint failure and the 2-out-of-3 vote directly, just with wide intervals. With only two admitted runs there are no triples, so the vote would rest entirely on the Beta's shape, and I'd report it as not estimable for that task instead of guessing."
  3. Prof: "Say you get those estimates at both temperatures. Do you expect the higher temperature to help the vote?"
    You: "I don't know, and that's the interesting part. A higher temperature may make runs differ more, which lowers ρ, but each run may also get worse, which raises p, so the vote can still fail more often. Prof. Zheng's N-version paper says in its abstract that adjusting the temperature increased diversity but didn't always improve reliability, so I'd report p and ρ separately, not only the vote."

D. Statistics, power and sample size

H13. (Prof. Okamura) How many runs per setting do you need to estimate ρ within plus or minus 0.1?

I haven't computed it yet. I'd do it by simulation before the pilot: draw θ from a Beta with plausible values of p and ρ, simulate k runs on many inputs, fit the model, bootstrap over runs, and see how wide the interval for ρ is at k = 10, 15 and 20. Then the pilot data replace my guesses.

What the professor wants to hear: power by simulation, not a number from nowhere.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You'd draw θ from a Beta for each input and then simulate the runs. Then every input is independent in your simulation. Is that what real runs look like?"
    You: "No, and it would make the intervals far too narrow. A real run is one program, so one bug fails on a whole region of inputs at once. So I'd simulate each run as a program: it gets each of a few bugs with some chance, and each bug fails on a fixed region of inputs."
  2. Prof: "Take the simplest case: one bug, which each run has with chance q. What is ρ, and how well do ten runs estimate it?"
    You: "If the bug's region is a small share of the inputs, ρ is almost exactly q, because on that region the share of failing runs is q and everywhere else it's zero. So with ten runs I'm really estimating a proportion from ten draws. At q = 0.5 the standard error is the square root of 0.25 over 10, about 0.16, so a 95% interval of about plus or minus 0.3."
  3. Prof: "Then how many runs would you need for plus or minus 0.1?"
    You: "About a hundred in that case, because the width shrinks only with the square root of the number of runs: 1.96 times the square root of 0.25 over k is 0.1 at k of about 96. That's ten times my runs per setting. So with ten runs I'd claim to tell ρ near zero from ρ near a half, not 0.3 from 0.4, and I'd pool tasks when I compare settings."

H14. (Prof. Zheng) What is your real sample size: 10,000 inputs, or 10 runs?

For ρ it's much closer to the runs, and to the number of different bugs. One buggy run fails on a whole region of inputs at once, so those inputs aren't independent pieces of information. That's why I bootstrap over runs, and why adding inputs alone won't make the interval for ρ much narrower.

What the professor wants to hear: understands clustering and effective sample size.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You bootstrap by resampling the ten runs with replacement, so some runs appear twice. What does a run paired with its own copy do to ρ?"
    You: "It pushes ρ up, because a run and its copy fail on exactly the same inputs, so they look perfectly dependent. In a resample of ten, on average one pair in ten is a run with its own copy. So the resampled ρ centres near ρ plus a tenth of one minus ρ, a bias of up to 0.1."
  2. Prof: "A bias of 0.1 is as big as the precision you want. How would you fix it?"
    You: "Inside each resample I'd count only pairs of different original runs, so a run never meets its own copy. Or I'd use the jackknife, leaving out one run at a time, which never duplicates anything. Either way, I'd check on simulated data with a known ρ that the interval really covers it about 95% of the time."
  3. Prof: "You'd check the coverage on simulated data. Simulated how? If you simulate from your fitted beta-binomial, every input is drawn on its own again."
    You: "Right, and then the check would miss exactly the clustering that makes ρ hard to estimate. So I'd simulate whole runs: each run gets a few bugs by chance, and each bug fails on a fixed region of inputs, which is a parametric bootstrap at the level of runs. Your group's fine-grained bootstrap paper says in its abstract that intuitive resampling creates ties, so I'd like to learn how you avoided them."

E. Why a Beta distribution

These build on Q16 and Q17 in the old file (fit and moments).

H18. (Prof. Okamura) Is θ really continuous? With only a few bugs, θ takes only a few values.

That's fair. Each input falls in some failure region, roughly one per bug, and θ is about the same inside it. So a finite mixture, a latent-class model, is the more honest picture, and the Beta is a smooth approximation of it. If the data show a few clear clusters, I'd fit the mixture and compare the two by AIC and by how well each predicts the observed vote failures.

What the professor wants to hear: understands where the variation in θ comes from, and has an alternative.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You'd compare them by AIC. But your likelihood multiplies over 10,000 inputs as if they were independent. Can you trust AIC with that?"
    You: "Not really. The log-likelihood grows with the number of inputs, but the real information grows with the runs, so AIC's penalty of two per parameter is far too small and it will favour the bigger model too easily. So I'd compare the models on runs they weren't fitted to: fit on five runs and see how well each predicts how often the other five fail together."
  2. Prof: "Fit on five runs. How many classes can five runs support at all?"
    You: "At most three. Five runs identify only the first five moments of θ, and c classes need 2c − 1 numbers, c values of θ and c − 1 weights, so 2c − 1 can't be more than five. That's the classic condition for mixtures of binomials, and with all ten runs it allows five classes."
  3. Prof: "And if reading the code shows seven different bugs?"
    You: "Then the classes group bugs by how often they appear, not by what they are. Two bugs that each show up in about the same share of runs give their inputs the same θ, and the vote depends only on θ, so for the vote that grouping costs nothing. If the seven shares are all different, five classes are an approximation, and I'd judge it by how well it predicts the vote."

H19. (Prof. Okamura) With k = 10 runs per input, what can you identify about the distribution of θ?

Its first ten moments. If Y of k runs fail on an input, then C(Y, j) divided by C(k, j) has expected value E[θ^j], for any j up to k. The Beta needs only the first two moments. A richer model needs more, which is one more reason for ten runs and not three.

What the professor wants to hear: knows that k limits what can be identified (factorial moments).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Show me why C(Y, j) over C(k, j) has expected value E[θ^j]. Take j = 2."
    You: "Given θ, Y is binomial with k trials, so E[Y(Y − 1)] = k(k − 1)θ². C(Y, 2) over C(k, 2) is exactly Y(Y − 1) over k(k − 1), so its expected value given θ is θ², and averaging over inputs gives E[θ²]. In words, it's the share of pairs of runs that both fail on that input."
  2. Prof: "Then the tenth moment is just the share of inputs where all ten runs fail. How well can you estimate moments that high?"
    You: "Badly. E[θ^10] uses only one combination, all ten runs together, so it depends on whether some bug happened to land in every one of them. The low moments average over many more combinations, 45 pairs and 120 triples of runs, so they're more stable, though they still rest on the same ten runs."
  3. Prof: "And a vote over eleven or more runs?"
    You: "That needs moments above the tenth, and the data can't pin those down: two distributions with the same first ten moments give exactly the same distribution of counts but can give different votes over eleven runs. So a best N above ten comes partly from the model's shape, not from the data. I'd label it that way and show how much it moves between models that fit equally well."

H20. (Prof. Dohi) Our lab has written about the Poisson binomial distribution. Your runs have different failure probabilities. Why beta-binomial and not Poisson binomial?

The Poisson binomial is for independent trials with different probabilities, so it models differences between runs. My question is dependence: runs failing together because they share an input that is hard for all of them. The beta-binomial models that, with one θ per input shared by every run. If runs also differ a lot from each other, a model with both parts could be a next step. Your article on it is in Japanese, so I've read its abstract in translation.

What the professor wants to hear: separates "independent but different" from "dependent through a shared input".

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "But your runs do differ: a buggy run fails far more often than a clean one. Doesn't the beta-binomial ignore exactly that?"
    You: "It averages over those differences, treating runs as draws from one setting. But if runs failed independently with different chances p_i, the count of failing runs on an input would be Poisson binomial, with variance the sum of p_i(1 − p_i), and that is never larger than the binomial variance with the same mean. So unequal runs alone can't make the extra spread I measure; it has to come from dependence."
  2. Prof: "Prove that it's never larger."
    You: "The sum of p_i(1 − p_i) is the sum of p_i minus the sum of p_i². The mean of the squares is at least the square of the mean, so the sum of p_i² is at least k times p̄², where p̄ is the average. So the variance is at most k p̄ minus k p̄², which is k p̄(1 − p̄), with equality only when all the p_i are the same."
  3. Prof: "So unequal runs make the count less spread out, not more. Then where does the Poisson binomial belong in your analysis?"
    You: "As the null model. The Knight and Leveson test that Ron et al. use is already a Poisson binomial calculation: under independence, the chance that two or more versions fail on an input is one minus the Poisson binomial chances of zero and of one failure. I'd compare the whole distribution of the count with the observed one, not only 'two or more', to see at which count the excess starts."

F. If the dependence is not what you expect

Q49 in the old file covers "ρ near zero". These are the other surprises.

H21. (Another professor) What if ρ is large, but voting still helps a lot, as in Ron et al.?

That's not a contradiction. Dependence lowers the gain from voting; it doesn't remove it. Ron et al. had strong dependence and still cut the mean failures to about a third. So I'd report both: ρ, and how much of the independent-case gain the vote really gets, like Nogueira's redundancy effectiveness.

What the professor wants to hear: dependence does not mean no benefit; report both numbers.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Put numbers on 'lowers the gain'. With p = 0.1 and ρ = 0.5 in your Beta, what does a 2-out-of-3 vote give?"
    You: "E[θ²] is p² plus ρ p(1 − p), so 0.01 plus 0.045, which is 0.055, and the Beta's third moment comes out at 0.0385. So the vote fails with 3 times 0.055 minus 2 times 0.0385, about 0.088, against 0.1 for one run and 0.028 if runs were independent. That's only about a sixth of the possible gain."
  2. Prof: "So under your own model a high ρ means voting barely helps. Then how did Ron et al. get strong dependence and still cut failures to a third?"
    You: "Because 'strong' there means a large z, not a large ρ. At their mean failure rate, about 4 in 10,000, independent triples would fail over 800 times less often than one version; theirs failed about 3 times less. That's far from independence, even if ρ itself is modest."
  3. Prof: "How modest? Can you bound ρ from their numbers?"
    You: "Roughly, yes. A 2-out-of-3 vote fails at least whenever two given versions both fail, so its failure probability is at least E[θ²], which is about ρ times p when p is small. Their triples failed at 0.34 of the single-version rate, so ρ is at most about 0.34, whatever the shape of the distribution."

H22. (Another professor) What if ρ is 0.9 on one task and near zero on the others?

Then the spread between tasks is the result, and I wouldn't average it away. I'd report each task, look at what the high one has that the others don't, for example a rule that forces one helper, and if I need one summary, use a hierarchical model that shows the spread between tasks.

What the professor wants to hear: treats heterogeneity as a finding, not noise.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You'd look for something like a rule that forces one helper. How would you show it's the rule, and not something else about that task?"
    You: "On that task I have an experiment built in: the same task with the rule replaced by neutral text. If ρ falls a lot without the rule, the rule is the likely cause there. If it stays near 0.9, it's something else, for example a task text that points every run to the same helper."
  2. Prof: "With ten runs per file version, how big must that fall be before you believe it?"
    You: "Big. In the simplest picture, one shared bug that each run has with some chance, a ρ from ten runs is uncertain by about plus or minus 0.2 near 0.9 and 0.3 near a half, so only a fall from 0.9 to below about a half would stand out. Reading the code is the stronger check: if the runs without the rule stop using the helper and the shared failures go with it, I see the cause directly."
  3. Prof: "That's still one task. Isn't a cause found on one task just an anecdote?"
    You: "On its own, yes. It's a case study, and I'd call it one. The second task with the same rule is a first check, and if it shows the same pattern, that's a lead for a later study with more tasks of that kind, not a general claim."

H23. (Prof. Zheng) Suppose your interval for ρ includes zero. What do you conclude?

That I can't rule out independence on that task, which isn't the same as showing independence. I'd report the upper end of the interval and what it means for the vote: for example, even at the upper end, how much worse the vote is than the independent formula says. If that's small, the practical answer is "voting works here", whatever the exact ρ is.

What the professor wants to hear: "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence", and a practical reading through the upper bound.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "In a beta-binomial ρ can't be negative. So what does your estimate do when the true ρ is zero?"
    You: "It piles up at zero: when the true ρ is zero, the estimate lands exactly on zero about half the time, and more often when the runs differ in quality. And the plain bootstrap is known to work badly at a boundary like that. So 'the interval includes zero' tells me little on its own, and I'd test independence separately and use the interval mainly for its upper end."
  2. Prof: "How would you test independence with only ten runs?"
    You: "With a permutation test. For each run I shuffle which inputs it fails on, which keeps each run's own failure rate but breaks any link between runs, and I compare the observed number of inputs where two or more runs fail with the shuffled versions. It asks the same question as the Knight and Leveson test, without the normal approximation."
  3. Prof: "Say the test doesn't reject, and the upper end of ρ is 0.2, with p = 0.05. What do you tell a team?"
    You: "At that upper end a 2-out-of-3 vote fails with about 0.027, nearly four times the 0.007 that independence promises, but still about half of one run's 0.05. So I'd tell them three runs cut failures roughly in half even at the upper end, and maybe by much more."

G. When the checker or the oracle is wrong

If it makes random mistakes in both file versions, the compliance difference looks smaller than it is, so I'd underestimate how much the rule changed behaviour, not overestimate it.   1. Prof: "You say the checker doesn't enter the failure probability. But if the checker shows no change, you replace the rule. So doesn't the checker decide which rules reach RQ2?"

H26. (Prof. Zheng) You drop inputs after looking at disagreements. Doesn't that let you shape the result?

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You drop inputs where the merged code contradicts its own docstring. Why does that matter so much? What would a wrong reference do to ρ?"
    You: "It would push ρ up a lot. If the merged code is wrong on an input, every correct run disagrees with it, so all runs 'fail' together there, which looks like perfect dependence. So these drops protect RQ1 from shared failures that are really the reference's own bug."
  2. Prof: "But all runs failing together on one input is exactly the shared bug you're looking for. How do you tell the two apart?"
    You: "Only by reading the input and both outputs against the task text, the docstring and the tests. If the merged code breaks what its own docstring or tests say, the reference is wrong; if the runs break it, it's a real shared failure.     You: "Rounding to cents. Say the runs use a helper that rounds floats with Python's round, and the merged code uses Decimal, Python's exact decimal type. H. Limits of differential testing

H27. (Another professor) Strictly, you measure disagreement with one person's code. Why call it a failure probability?

Strictly, it's the probability of disagreeing with the merged reference under my declared input distribution. I call a disagreement a failure only after the hand check, and in the thesis I'd name it plainly: failure relative to the merged code. That's a limit on what my measure means, and I'll state it.

What the professor wants to hear: precise naming (construct validity).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Then write it down. How does your disagreement rate relate to the run's true failure probability?"
    You: "A run disagrees with the merged code when only the run is wrong, when only the merged code is wrong, or when both are wrong in different ways. So the disagreement rate is the run's true failure probability, plus the share of inputs where only the merged code is wrong, minus the share where both are wrong in the same way. The hand checks catch the inputs where only the merged code is wrong; the ones where both are wrong the same way I can't see at all."
  2. Prof: "So you miss exactly the shared bugs. Which way does that push ρ?"
    You: "Mostly down. If a rule pushes the runs to a buggy helper, it probably pushed the developer there too, so the inputs where many runs fail together are the ones most likely to be hidden. A large ρ would still be convincing, but a small ρ could partly be this blind spot."
  3. Prof: "Then how would you tell a truly small ρ from a hidden one?"
    You: "Not with differential testing alone. For each task I'd read whether the merged code calls the same helper the rule names, because where it does, a bug in that helper is invisible to me. So I'd report ρ for those tasks separately and read a small value there as possibly too low."

H28. (Another professor) What about a version that's correct but a hundred times slower, or never stops?

A run that doesn't finish within a fixed timeout counts as a failure, because an infinite loop can only be stopped, not detected. I'd set the timeout from the merged code's own time on that input, with a wide margin. Plain slowness isn't in my failure definition, so I'd report it separately.

What the professor wants to hear: timeouts tied to the reference, and a clear scope for "failure".

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why can't you detect an infinite loop? Surely you can see that the program is stuck."
    You: "In general no program can decide, for every program and input, whether it will stop; that's Turing's halting problem. In practice, when the timer runs out, a loop that never ends and a very slow correct run look exactly the same."
  2. Prof: "So your timeout can turn a slow but correct version into a failure. How would you know your margin was wide enough?"
    You: "I'd fix it in advance: a thousand times the merged code's time on that input, with a floor of one second, so even your hundred-times-slower version finishes. Then I'd rerun every timeout with a limit ten times longer: if many of those finish with the right answer, the limit was too tight, and I'd raise it for all settings and report both counts."
  3. Prof: "You rerun timeouts. But if the machine stalls for a moment, many versions could time out at once. Couldn't timeouts themselves create shared failures?"
    You: "Yes. If I feed each input to all versions at the same time, a stall of a second or two makes them all time out on that one input together, which looks exactly like dependence. So a timeout counts only if it happens again when I rerun that version on that input alone, and I'd log when each timeout happened to see whether they bunch up in time."

H29. (Prof. Okamura) Every admitted version passes the same tests. Doesn't that acceptance step itself push the failures into the same untested places?

Yes, I think it does. If every admitted version passes the same tests, the failures that are left must sit where the tests don't look, so they cluster there and ρ goes up. Knight and Leveson's versions all passed one acceptance test too, so the same effect may be in their data. I can't remove it, but both file versions face the same tests, so the RQ2 comparison stays fair, and I report ρ as "given admission".

What the professor wants to hear: sees that the selection step itself can create dependence.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Can you show that with a small model, or is it only intuition?"
    You: "A toy one. Each run has, with chance 0.2, a bug that fails the whole tested half of the inputs, and independently, with chance 0.2, one that fails the whole untested half, so before admission θ is 0.2 everywhere and ρ is zero. Admission removes every run with a bug in the tested half, so θ drops to zero there and stays 0.2 on the other half: p becomes 0.1, the variance of θ 0.01, and ρ is 0.01 over 0.1 times 0.9, about 0.11."
  2. Prof: "Then a project with stronger tests would show a higher ρ? That sounds backwards."
    You: "In this toy, yes. If the tests cover 90% of the inputs instead of half, p falls from 0.1 to 0.02, but ρ rises from about 0.11 to about 0.18, and a 2-out-of-3 vote gets about half of the gain independence would give instead of two thirds. Better tests leave fewer failures, but the ones left are the ones every admitted run can share."
  3. Prof: "That's a toy. How would you check it on real data?"
    You: "A run that fails the task's tests still leaves code I can run on the generated inputs, so on the same task I can compute ρ for all runs and for the admitted runs only. If ρ is clearly higher among the admitted ones, the acceptance step is adding dependence. Ron et al. had little to compare: of their 21 rejected versions, 12 produced no program and 5 crashed straight away."

N. What "reliability" means here

Q3 in the old file asks whether this is reliability or software engineering. These ask what the word means in your plan.

H39. (Prof. Dohi) Define reliability in your study precisely. Over time, or over inputs?

Over inputs, per demand. The reliability of one admitted version is one minus its failure probability under my declared input distribution, and for the vote it's one minus the vote's failure probability. Only the field study is over time, where the NHPP counts the faults found by calendar time.

What the professor wants to hear: per-demand reliability, and awareness that classic reliability growth models are time-based.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Per demand. But a function in use is called again and again. How do you get from your number to reliability over time?"
    You: "If calls arrive at random, as a Poisson process with rate λ, and each call draws its input from the same distribution, then each call fails with probability p, independently of the others. Keeping each point of a Poisson process with probability p, called thinning, gives again a Poisson process, so failures arrive at rate λ times p. The chance of no failure over a time t is then e to the minus λ p t."
  2. Prof: "Put numbers in: p = 0.001 and 1,000 calls a day."
    You: "Then λ p is 1,000 times 0.001, so one failure a day on average, and the chance of a failure-free day is e to the minus 1, about 0.37. So even a one-in-a-thousand failure probability gives at least one failure on about 63% of days."
  3. Prof: "That assumes calls fail independently. Isn't independence exactly what your thesis questions?"
    You: "It's a different independence. For one fixed version, failure on an input is just yes or no, so independent inputs give independent failures; my thesis is about different versions failing on the same input. Where it does break is when users retry a failing input: the chance of a failure-free day stays the same, since the first failure always comes on a fresh input, but the failures after it come in bursts, more spread out than a Poisson count."

H40. (Another professor) Before anything is merged, a human reviews it. Isn't the reviewer the real safety net? Why measure before review?

The reviewer is a safety net, but a reviewer reads the diff and the tests. A bug on an input nobody tested is easy to miss, and Xia and Miller's numbers suggest some get through: agent lines got more bug fixes after merge. My measure would tell a reviewer how far to trust several runs that all agree.

What the professor wants to hear: the study supports review; it doesn't replace it.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Xia and Miller's numbers are about merged code in general. Do they say anything about review itself?"
    You: "Indirectly, yes. Across projects, merging more pull requests without review went with more maintenance burden on agent code: each 10 points more no-review merges, about 6% more on average. So review seems to catch something, but that's an association across projects, not a count of what reviewers catch."
  2. Prof: "So review catches something, but human review is slow. Why not let a second agent review every change?"
    You: "Because the reviewer would be the same kind of system as the author. Xia and Miller note that automating review hands the decision to the same kind of system that wrote the code, though they say their data can't show it reviews worse. In my terms, the reviewing agent's misses may be correlated with the writing agent's failures, the same covariance I measure between two settings, and then it tends to miss the very inputs the first one got wrong."
  3. Prof: "'May be correlated.' Could your design actually measure that?"
    You: "Partly, with data I'd already have, though it isn't in my plan. I know which admitted versions fail on generated inputs, so I could give each diff and its task to a reviewing agent, ask it to approve or reject, and see whether it rejects the failing versions more often than the clean ones. If a reviewer on the same model as the author catches fewer of them than a reviewer on a different model, that's the correlation showing up in review."

  2. Prof: "A test needs an expected answer, and a team has no merged code yet. What do they compare against?"

  3. Prof: "How much does comparing runs miss? Give me a formula."
    You: "Take two runs. Of the inputs where run one fails, run two also fails on a share E[θ²] over p, which in my model is p + ρ(1 - p). With p = 0.05 and ρ = 0.3 that's about a third. Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "0.44 against 0.41 looks like noise. Is that difference even real?"
    You: "They checked it with a paired Wilcoxon test over the problems, a test on ranks: prompting gave p = 0.043, against p below 0.001 for mixing models.   2. Prof: "Contest problems aren't repository tasks. Why should a 'be different' prompt be weak on yours too?"
    You: "It might not be, and their data can't tell me.   3. Prof: "And if those runs fail together less, but each one fails more often?"
    You: "Then I'd compare the vote, not ρ alone. In my Beta model, with p = 0.05 and ρ = 0.3 a vote of three fails about 3.4% of the time; if the prompt cuts ρ to 0.1 but raises p to 0.07, the vote improves to about 2.9%, but if p doubles to 0.10, it gets worse, about 4.7%. H43. (Prof. Zheng) My work includes sensitivity analysis. Is there a sensitivity question in your plan?

Yes. The second part of RQ1 is one: how much does the vote failure move when I change one factor between runs? In the model it's direct. Two runs both fail with p² + ρ p(1 - p), so the change per unit of ρ is p(1 - p). For the vote and for the cost-optimal N it's less simple, and that's something I'd like to learn to do properly here.

What the professor wants to hear: a concrete link to sensitivity, with an actual derivative.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You say for the vote it's less simple. Try it now: at independence, how fast does the 2-out-of-3 vote failure grow with ρ, holding p fixed?"
    You: "The vote fails with 3E[θ²] - 2E[θ³]. At ρ = 0, E[θ²] grows at p(1 - p) per unit of ρ, and for a Beta, E[θ³] grows at 3p²(1 - p), so the vote failure grows at 3p(1 - p)(1 - 2p). For small p that's about three times the rate for a pair, because three runs make three pairs that can fail together."
  2. Prof: "But you expect ρ well above zero. Is the vote as sensitive there?"
    You: "No, less. For p = 0.05 the slope falls from about 0.13 at ρ = 0 to about 0.06 at ρ = 0.3, because as ρ goes to 1 every run gives the same answer and the vote fails exactly as often as one run, so the curve flattens out at p. So the vote is most sensitive to dependence when runs are close to independent."
  3. Prof: "Your ρ will come with a wide interval. What does that slope do with it?"
    You: "It carries the uncertainty in ρ over to the vote, to first order: the standard error of the vote failure is about the slope times the standard error of ρ. At ρ = 0.3, a standard error of about 0.15 in ρ, which is roughly what ten runs give, makes the vote failure of about 0.034 uncertain by about 0.009, about a quarter of it. It's the first-order version of the moment-based uncertainty propagation in your group's chapter on epistemic uncertainty in Markovian reliability models, which I know from its abstract, though with ten runs I'd check it against the bootstrap."

H44. (Prof. Okamura) Our lab has proportional-intensity reliability models with covariates. How is your ω_j = L_j exp(β′z_j) related?

It's the same basic idea: covariates multiply a baseline through an exponential, like in a Cox model. In Li, Dohi and Okamura's Electronics 2022 paper, whose abstract and model section I've read, time-dependent development metrics such as test execution time enter the intensity through exp(xβ). I put each change's own features into how many faults it brings. pysrat's metrics-based models might already cover part of it, and I'd ask you.

What the professor wants to hear: recognises the exp(β′z) proportional structure and links it to the lab.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You say each change's features set how many faults it brings. Why is the size L_j outside the exponential, and not a covariate with its own coefficient?"
    You: "Outside, it says faults grow in proportion to the lines added, with the power fixed at 1, which statisticians call an offset. If I put log L_j inside with a coefficient γ, ω_j becomes L_j to the power γ times exp(β′z_j), so my form is the case γ = 1, and I can check it: γ below 1 would mean bigger changes bring fewer faults per line."
  2. Prof: "How would you check it?"
    You: "Fit both by maximum likelihood and use a likelihood ratio test: if γ = 1 is right, twice the gain in log-likelihood is roughly chi-squared with one degree of freedom, so a gain above about 1.92 rejects it at 5%. Xia and Miller made the γ = 1 choice in their static-analysis models, where the number of changed lines entered as a log offset."
  3. Prof: "Lines are a crude measure. A one-line change to a core function can be riskier than 200 lines of tests."
    You: "True. So I'd count only source lines in L_j, as Xia and Miller did for their static-analysis counts, and put the risk into z_j, for example how many other files import the changed code, and which agent wrote it. Xia and Miller also adjusted for each file's role, such as source or test, because different kinds of files get changed for different reasons."

H45. (Prof. Dohi) Our lab asked whether infinite-failure NHPP models are useful. In your field model, how would you choose between finite and infinite?

What the professor wants to hear: model choice by prediction, and knowing the queue form covers both cases.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "In that paper the infinite-failure models predicted better, especially early in testing. Your agent data will always be early. Doesn't that decide it before you start?"
    You: "It might. I've read the abstract, section 2.2 and the conclusion of your paper, and the conclusion says exactly that for grouped data, though the advantage faded as testing went on.   2. Prof: "Then in your own model, what decides whether it levels off?"
    You: "Mostly the merges, not the fit. While agent changes keep arriving, m(t) keeps rising, and if they stop, it levels off at the sum of the ω_j. Past merge times I observe directly, so apart from a forecast of future merges, what's left to choose is the delay distribution G."
  3. Prof: "Does the choice of G still matter for that question?"
    You: "Yes, through its tail.
