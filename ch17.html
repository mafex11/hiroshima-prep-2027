Chapter 17 of 28

How to address them, and basic etiquette

When and how long: Sunday, 10 minutes.

What this file is: what to call each professor, how to greet them, how to end a call, and the small habits that Japanese professors notice. Short and practical.

1. Names and titles

Person Name on official pages Title In English (call and email) In Japanese style
Your supervisor Junjun ZHENG (鄭 俊俊 on his Japanese page) Associate Professor (准教授) Prof. Zheng Zheng-sensei
Senior professor Tadashi DOHI (土肥 正) Professor (教授) Prof. Dohi Dohi-sensei
Senior professor Hiroyuki OKAMURA (岡村 寛之) Professor (教授) Prof. Okamura Okamura-sensei
Assistant professor Jingchi WU Specially Appointed Assistant Professor Dr. Wu Wu-sensei

Family names come first in capitals on official lists: "ZHENG, Junjun" means Zheng is the family name. Always use the family name: Prof. Zheng, never "Prof. Junjun" and never "Junjun".

"Prof." for an associate professor is fine in English. You have already written "Dear Prof. Zheng", so keep it. "Dr. Zheng" is also correct, but don't switch between the two.

Saying the names:

  • Zheng: close to "jung". If you are not sure, listen to how he or the others say it on the call and copy them.
  • Dohi: "DOH-hee", two short sounds.
  • Okamura: "oh-kah-moo-rah", all syllables even, no strong stress.
  • If you get a name wrong, don't make a fuss. Correct it once and carry on.

2. "Sensei"

  • In Japan, professors and teachers are called sensei. It goes after the family name: Zheng-sensei, Dohi-sensei, Okamura-sensei.
  • On Monday the call is in English, so "Prof. Zheng" is the default. You never go wrong with it.
  • Use "-sensei" only if it feels natural, for example if Prof. Zheng himself says "Okamura-sensei" when he talks about a colleague. Then "Okamura-sensei" from you is fine too.
  • Never use "-san" for a professor. "-san" is for equals and staff. It sounds too casual for your supervisor.
  • Never use "-sensei" for yourself, and don't add "Mr." to your own name.
  • Students in the lab will probably call him "Zheng-sensei". Once you are in the lab, you will too.

3. Greeting

Start of the call:

"Hello, Prof. Zheng. Can you hear me well?" "Thank you very much for making time for me today, and for agreeing to supervise my research."

  • It is late afternoon in Japan (17:00 JST). Skip "good morning" and "good evening". "Hello" is safe.
  • A small nod of the head when you say thank you is natural, even on video. It does not need to be a full bow.

If Prof. Dohi joins:

"Thank you for joining, Prof. Dohi, and thank you for introducing me to Prof. Zheng."

If Prof. Okamura joins:

"Good to meet you, Prof. Okamura. Thank you for joining."

Optional Japanese, only if you can say it smoothly. You do not need any Japanese for this call.

  • At the start: "Hajimemashite." (Nice to meet you.)
  • After the greeting: "Yoroshiku onegaishimasu." (Roughly: I am glad to be in your care, please treat me kindly.) Japanese people say this at the start of almost any new relationship. One sentence is enough. Then go back to English.

4. During the call

  • Let him lead. He decides the order. Don't jump to your questions while he is still on the research.
  • Don't interrupt. Wait a full second after he stops. Japanese speakers often pause in the middle of a thought. A pause is not your turn yet.
  • Short answers. One or two sentences, one example, then stop. If he wants more, he will ask.
  • Say "I don't know" plainly when you don't know. Then say how you would find out. This is respected far more than a guess.
  • Take advice without arguing. "Thank you, that's helpful. I'll think about how to add it." You can discuss it by email later.
  • Nod and say "yes" or "I see" while he talks, so he knows you are following. On a video call silence can feel like a frozen screen.
  • Don't talk about money, chances of passing or Prof. Dohi's retirement.
  • Use "prospective supervisor" in writing until you are admitted. In speech, "my supervisor" is fine once he has used the word.

5. Ending the call

Say back what you agreed (see 01_30min_meeting_game_plan.md, section 8). Then:

"Thank you very much for your time today. I look forward to working with you."

Optional: "Arigatou gozaimashita." (Thank you very much, for something that has finished.)

  • Let him end it. In Japan the junior person usually waits for the senior person to close the meeting. Smile, nod and wait. If nobody leaves, say "Thank you, goodbye" once more and leave.
  • Don't add new topics after the goodbye has started.

6. Email habits

  • Reply within a day, the same day if you can. Japanese professors notice slow replies from students.
  • Reply All in his thread, keep the subject, and keep everyone he copied. Don't add new people.
  • Start with "Dear Prof. Zheng," and end with "Kind regards," and your full name.
  • One topic per email where you can. Short paragraphs.
  • Don't send several follow-ups. If something has a deadline, one polite nudge after two working days is fine (template 8 in 04_email_templates.md).
  • He may reply late at night or at the weekend. That doesn't mean you must. But during this application, answer quickly.
  • Holidays in Japan in this period: Tue 3 Nov (Culture Day) and Mon 23 Nov (Labour Thanksgiving Day). Offices are closed. Professors may still read email.
  • Times: JST first, IST in brackets.

7. Small things that help

  • Be early. Join at 13:25 IST. Being late, even by two minutes, is noticed.
  • Be precise with dates and forms. Japanese universities follow procedure closely. Repeat dates back. Use the official names: "Informatics and Data Science Program", "Special Selection for International Students living outside Japan".
  • Thank people for small things. The Support Office answered you quickly on 29 Oct. Thank them in your next email.
  • No gifts, no favours. Nothing like that is expected before admission.
  • Don't compare labs or universities in front of him. If NAIST comes up, state the facts plainly and stop.
