Chapter 17 of 45

Mock oral exams: three full committee rounds

When and how long: Mock 1 on Sun 15 Nov, Mock 2 on Sat 21 Nov, Mock 3 on Sun 22 Nov. 30 minutes each.

Three complete 30-minute mock exams for the Hiroshima University oral examination (Informatics and Data Science Program, master's, April 2027 entry). Each mock is a different committee with different questions: run one per session, not all three at once.

For the friend: read this first

What you are doing. You play the exam committee for Sudhanshu, the candidate. The real exam is online, on Teams or Zoom, on one day between 24 November and 2 December 2026. It has two parts:

  1. About 15 minutes of presentation by the candidate: about 10 minutes on work so far, then about 5 minutes on the research plan.
  2. About 15 minutes of questions from the committee.

The supervisor, Assoc. Prof. Junjun Zheng, said most questions will be about the research plan, that it depends on the committee, and that they may ask "Why did you choose Japan?" and "Why Hiroshima University?". The mocks follow that: about 60% research plan, 20% the candidate's work, 10% motivation, and 10% quick basics asked on the spot.

The people you play. Each question says who asks it. Change your tone a little for each one.

  • Prof. Zheng: Assoc. Prof. Junjun Zheng, the supervisor. Works on software reliability, probability models, and whether voting over several AI-written program versions makes software more reliable. Friendly, but precise.
  • Prof. Okamura: Prof. Hiroyuki Okamura, same lab. Testing methods and reliability tools. Asks about method and maths details.
  • Prof. Dohi: Prof. Tadashi Dohi, the senior professor of the lab. Classic software reliability models. Short questions that go deep.
  • Committee member: a professor from another lab in the programme. Not an expert in this topic. Asks "so what?", "why should I believe it?", and the motivation questions.

You don't need to understand the answers. Read each question as written. Then listen.

  • Don't read the "Listen for" line out loud. It is for you: the points a good answer contains.
  • Push when an answer is vague, with one of these: "Why?", "Can you give an example?", "How would you check that?", "What if that's not true?"
  • Keep it moving. If one answer goes past about a minute, say "Thank you. Let's move on."
  • Write down every question where the candidate hesitated, guessed, or talked too long. Go through them together at the end, with the checklist.

How to run one mock (30 minutes).

  1. Use a video call. The candidate shares the screen for the presentation, then stops sharing for the questions.
  2. Read the opening lines (the lines in grey quote blocks are what you say out loud).
  3. Say "please start your presentation", and start a 15-minute timer.
  4. At 14 minutes, say "One minute left, please." At 15 minutes, if the talk is still going, ask the candidate to finish. At 16 minutes, stop the talk politely. Write down the finish time. Otherwise, don't interrupt the presentation.
  5. Ask the questions in order, for about 15 minutes. If time runs short, skip a research-plan question, but always ask the quick basics question and the last question, "Do you have any questions for us?"
  6. Read the closing lines. Then fill in the checklist at the end of this file.

If Sudhanshu practises alone. The study site has a play button on each numbered question and on each quote block. Press play, answer out loud, time yourself, then read the "Listen for" line and score yourself honestly. Keep this file's format if you edit it: each question is its own paragraph that starts with bold text like **12. (Prof. Zheng) ...?**, followed by a line that starts Listen for:. Spoken lines that are not numbered questions go in quote blocks.

Mock 1: a friendly committee (questions 1 to 15)

Prof. Zheng chairs. The questions are the most likely ones. Run this mock first.

Opening (about 1 minute)

Hello, Sudhanshu. Can you hear us well?

Thank you for joining. I'm Junjun Zheng. With me today are Prof. Okamura, Prof. Dohi and a colleague from our programme.

The examination has two parts. First, about fifteen minutes for your presentation: your work so far, and then your research plan. Then about fifteen minutes of questions from us.

When you're ready, please share your screen and start your presentation.

Presentation (15 minutes)

Start the timer now. Say nothing until 14 minutes.

One minute left, please.

If the talk is still going at 15 minutes:

Thank you. Could you finish your current slide, please?

When the talk ends:

Thank you, Sudhanshu. Please stop sharing your screen. We'll start the questions now.

Questions (about 15 minutes)

1. (Prof. Zheng) Thank you. In two or three sentences, what is the main question of your research plan? Listen for: repeated agent runs on one real task treated as N versions; the shared AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md as part of the specification every run shares; RQ1, how much more often runs fail together than independence predicts and which change between runs lowers it; RQ2, whether one real rule changes reliability beyond the tests; a model that tells a team how many runs to vote over. Short, not a second presentation. (Q1 in 03_Mock_Interview/05_question_bank_with_model_answers.md)

2. (Prof. Zheng) You model the failure chance θ with a Beta distribution. Why a Beta, and what will you do if it fits badly? Listen for: θ lies between 0 and 1 and changes from input to input; a simple two-parameter family; the count of failing runs on one input is then beta-binomial; ρ = 1/(a + b + 1), zero means independence; if most inputs never fail, try a zero-inflated model or a mixture, compared by AIC and by predicted vote failures. (Q16)

3. (Prof. Okamura) How will you use our boundary coverage distance method, and why only as a stress test? Listen for: 30 to 50 chosen inputs are not a random sample, so they cannot estimate a failure probability; boundary points taken from branch conditions that compare a parameter with a constant; says these are path-based boundaries, as Remark 2 of the paper warns; kill rate by execution path, also by output. (Q32, Q33)

4. (Committee member) You say two runs both fail with probability p squared plus the variance of θ. Can you explain that with a small example? Listen for: on one input both runs fail with θ squared; averaged over inputs that is E[θ²] = p² + Var θ, the definition of variance; an example such as half the inputs with θ = 0 and half with θ = 0.4, giving p = 0.2, p² = 0.04, but a real joint failure of 0.08; so independence needs every input equally hard. (Q6)

5. (Prof. Dohi) Eckhardt and Lee already asked whether there is an optimal number of versions. What is new in your "best N"? Listen for: agrees the question is old (their report has a figure on the existence of an optimal N); what is new is estimating the distribution of θ from real agent runs and adding the cost of each run; theirs minimises failure probability, this plan minimises run cost plus expected failure cost. (H5)

6. (Committee member) What is your real sample size for estimating ρ: the 10,000 inputs, or the 10 runs? Listen for: much closer to the runs and to the number of different bugs; one buggy run fails on a whole region of inputs, so inputs are not independent information; that is why the bootstrap resamples runs; more inputs alone won't narrow the interval for ρ. (H14)

7. (Prof. Zheng) What if the merged human code has the same bug as the agents? Listen for: names this as the main limit without being pushed; differential testing cannot see a bug the reference shares; it is exactly the shared-helper case the study is about, so those failures may be undercounted; the hand check catches the opposite case, where the merged code is wrong. (Q38)

8. (Committee member) Walk me through the 820 runs and what they cost. Listen for: 400 main runs (10 tasks x 2 file versions x 2 agents x 10 runs), 100 for the second Codex model, 200 for the own harness, 120 for screening; about 420 to 590 US dollars at the per-task costs in Gloaguen et al.; newer models may cost more; would ask whether it fits a lab budget, otherwise move runs to the open-weight model. Must not suggest the lab has to pay. (Q28)

9. (Committee member) You work at Layerpath. Tell us about one time an AI feature failed without anyone noticing, and how you found it. Listen for: BrandMemory; logs said the guidance was applied but the output had no trace of the brand voice; traced what the model really received; the brand voice section had the lowest priority and was cut first by a 3,200-character limit; what was fixed; one A/B pair (contractions 6 to 0), clearly called "not a study". (W17 to W19)

10. (Prof. Okamura) Your CV says Path AI reaches 85% accuracy. How was that measured? Listen for: either a real, specific method (what counts as correct, how many calls, who judged), or a plain "I'm not sure how that number was measured, so I won't defend it"; ideally adds what a proper measure would need: a definition of failure, a random sample, an interval. Red flag: defending the number without a method. (W7)

11. (Prof. Zheng) Your desktop agent's safety checker caught 9 out of 9 risky actions. How sure are you of that number? Listen for: calls it a small sample; with 9 out of 9, a 95% interval still goes down to about 66%; 0 false holds out of 36 still allows a false-hold rate up to about 10%; offline replays, often one run per task; "a good sign, not a proof". (W42)

12. (Committee member) Why do you want to study in Japan? Listen for: leads with the research: this lab builds probability models of how software fails, and the plan builds on Prof. Zheng's N-version paper; any personal reason is short and true; does not lead with "a booming industry" or anything generic; under about a minute. (09 file, Q5)

13. (Committee member) And why Hiroshima University in particular? Listen for: the Dependable Systems Laboratory is here and Prof. Zheng agreed to supervise; the lab's fields (software reliability, testing) are where the question sits; the programme joins informatics and data science; this special selection allows applying from India with an online exam. (09 file, Q9, Q10)

14. (Prof. Dohi) A quick one. What are the mean and the variance of a binomial distribution with n trials and probability p? Listen for: mean np, variance np(1 - p), said quickly. A bonus, not required: the beta-binomial variance is larger, np(1 - p)[1 + (n - 1)ρ].

15. (Prof. Zheng) Do you have any questions for us? Listen for: one or two real questions, for example what to read or learn before April, how the lab's weekly meeting works, or which probability and statistics courses are taught in English; short; nothing about money or scholarships for the lab to pay.

Closing

Thank you, Sudhanshu. That's all from the committee.

The results will be announced by the university. Thank you for your time, and you can leave the meeting now.

Mock 2: a harder committee (questions 16 to 30)

A professor from another lab chairs and pushes on novelty, statistics and honesty. Run this mock after Mock 1 feels easy.

Opening (about 1 minute)

Good afternoon. Can you see and hear us clearly?

I'm chairing today's committee. Prof. Zheng and Prof. Okamura are also with us.

You have about fifteen minutes to present. Please keep to the time. After that we'll ask questions for about fifteen minutes.

Please share your screen and start your presentation.

Presentation (15 minutes)

Start the timer now. Say nothing until 14 minutes.

One minute left, please.

If the talk is still going at 15 minutes:

We need to move on. Please finish in one sentence.

When the talk ends:

Thank you. Please stop sharing. We'll go to questions.

Questions (about 15 minutes)

16. (Committee member) Everyone already knows that LLM outputs are correlated. Why does your study matter? Listen for: "not independent" is known, "how much" is not, on real repository tasks with context files; the size matters for decisions, for example voting got 0.43 of the possible gain overall but 0.27 with one model (Nogueira et al.); a team must choose between rerunning the same agent and switching. (H4)

17. (Prof. Zheng) In RQ1 you compare four changes between runs. But your design is not fully crossed. What can you conclude, and what not? Listen for: admits it; temperature changes only in the own harness; the second model only inside Codex on the original file; Claude Code against Codex changes the agent and the model together; reports each comparison with its own baseline and claims no general ranking. (Q9)

18. (Prof. Okamura) Why does your bootstrap resample runs and not inputs? Listen for: the same runs meet all 10,000 or more inputs; one buggy run fails on many inputs at once; resampling inputs would make intervals far too narrow; admits the bootstrap is rough with about 10 runs and asks for advice, for example a parametric or hierarchical approach. (Q18)

19. (Committee member) Your rule checker is validated on only 20 examples. Suppose it is really only 85% accurate. What happens to RQ2? Listen for: the checker only confirms the manipulation, it is not part of the failure probability; random checker mistakes make the compliance difference look smaller, not bigger; the reliability numbers come from the generated inputs. (H24)

20. (Prof. Zheng) Every admitted version passes the same tests. Could that acceptance step itself make the failures cluster? Listen for: yes; remaining failures must sit where the tests don't look, so they cluster and ρ goes up; cannot remove it, but both file versions face the same tests so RQ2 stays fair; reports ρ "given admission". (H29)

21. (Committee member) How would you notice that a model copied the merged code from its training data? Listen for: compare each run's diff with the merged diff; near-identical diffs more often on old tasks than new ones; copying hides failures; reports old and new tasks separately. (H30)

22. (Prof. Okamura) With 10 runs per input, what can you identify about the distribution of θ, and why does it matter? Listen for: up to the first 10 moments; C(Y, j)/C(k, j) has expectation E[θ^j] for j up to k; the Beta needs only two moments; richer models need more, a reason for 10 runs rather than 3. A partial answer that says "k limits what I can learn about the shape" is fine. (H19)

23. (Prof. Zheng) Suppose only RQ1 works, and only on three tasks. Is that still a master's thesis? Listen for: yes, if careful: fitted model with intervals, compared with the Knight and Leveson z and Nogueira's score, plus vote failure and best N; claims limited to three tasks; asks the supervisor's view. (H10)

24. (Committee member) Your form says your 2023 paper won first position. Tell us about the paper and your part in it. Listen for: a review and concept paper in a special issue of IJIRAE, June 2023; second of four authors, a faculty member first; surveyed brain tumour segmentation, classification and robot guidance; no dataset, model or experiment of their own; the award was first position at ICRCCT 2023, held by the candidate's own department; neither inflates it nor calls it worthless; names an own part only if it is true. (W55, W56)

25. (Prof. Okamura) Most commits in your Yuki 2.0 repository carry "Co-Authored-By: Claude". What did you do yourself? Listen for: honest: coding agents were used as tools for parts of the work; the design decisions, the review and the testing were the candidate's; names concrete design rules (no screenshots, permission by whether an action can be undone, a checker that sees only the user's words); does not claim to have typed every line; offers to explain any part. (W45)

26. (Committee member) At BusinessRoom you set up monitoring, and feature adoption rose 15%. Did your monitoring cause that rise? Listen for: no causal claim; the logs guided product changes and adoption rose afterwards; without a control group the share due to the changes is unknown; does not defend a number without knowing how it was measured. (W25)

27. (Prof. Zheng) Why this laboratory, and why me as your supervisor? Listen for: the lab pairs each technique with probabilistic evaluation of how much quality improved; Prof. Zheng is first author of the N-version paper the plan builds on; the boundary value method and pysrat fit into the plan; the best-N question is like finding the best rejuvenation timing; the candidate brings the agent side and wants to learn the reliability modelling; says nothing about Prof. Dohi retiring. (09 file, Q13, Q16)

28. (Prof. Dohi) What does the mean value function m(t) of a non-homogeneous Poisson process mean? Give one example. Listen for: the expected number of faults found by time t; the count N(t) is Poisson with mean m(t); an example such as Goel-Okumoto, m(t) = ω(1 - e^(-bt)), where ω is the expected total number of faults and b the detection rate; the intensity is the derivative of m(t).

29. (Committee member) A lifetime is exponential with rate λ. Given that it has survived to time s, what is the chance it survives to s + t? Listen for: e^(-λt), the same as a new unit surviving t; the word "memoryless"; ideally the one-line reason, e^(-λ(s + t)) divided by e^(-λs).

30. (Committee member) Do you have any questions for us? Listen for: one or two real, short questions; nothing about money; not a repeat of something already answered.

Closing

Thank you. That's the end of the examination.

We'll let you know the result through the university. You can leave the meeting now.

Mock 3: methods, the lab's work and life plans (questions 31 to 45)

Prof. Zheng chairs again. More on the lab's own methods, the field study, reliability as a word, and the candidate's plans. Run this mock last, close to the exam day.

Opening (about 1 minute)

Good morning, Sudhanshu. Before we begin, can you check your camera and microphone? Can you hear all of us?

Thank you. Today's committee is Prof. Okamura, Prof. Dohi, a colleague from another laboratory and me.

As you know, please present for about fifteen minutes, first your work so far and then your research plan. Then we'll have about fifteen minutes of questions.

You can share your slides now. Please start your presentation.

Presentation (15 minutes)

Start the timer now. Say nothing until 14 minutes.

One minute left, please.

If the talk is still going at 15 minutes:

Thank you, we're at fifteen minutes. Please finish your last point.

When the talk ends:

Thank you. Please stop sharing your screen, and we'll begin the questions.

Questions (about 15 minutes)

31. (Prof. Zheng) In RQ2 you replace a rule with neutral text. Why not just delete the rule, and what counts as neutral? Listen for: deleting also makes the file shorter; Zhang et al. found random rules helped about as much as curated ones, so having rules at all seems to matter; neutral text is a sentence of about the same length with no instruction; the checker confirms the agents stopped following the rule. (Q10)

32. (Prof. Dohi) Define reliability in your study precisely. Is it over time, or over inputs? Listen for: over inputs, per demand; one version's reliability is one minus its failure probability under the declared input distribution; the vote's reliability likewise; only the field study is over calendar time, with an NHPP. (H39)

33. (Prof. Okamura) In your field-data part, why doesn't a finite-fault NHPP fit, and what do you use instead? Listen for: agent code keeps arriving, so the number of faults is not fixed; each merged change brings ω_j = L_j exp(β′z_j) expected faults, found after a delay with distribution G; m(t) is the sum of ω_j G(t - τ_j); with one change and an exponential G it reduces to Goel-Okumoto; it is an infinite-server queue, as in Dohi, Osaki and Trivedi (2004), honestly known only from the abstract. (Q34, Q35)

34. (Committee member) Strictly, you only measure disagreement with one person's merged code. Why do you call it a failure probability? Listen for: agrees it is strictly disagreement with the merged reference under a declared input distribution; called a failure only after the hand check; will name it plainly in the thesis as failure relative to the merged code. (H27)

35. (Prof. Zheng) Suppose your interval for ρ includes zero. What do you conclude? Listen for: cannot rule out independence, which is not the same as showing it; reports the upper end of the interval and what it means for the vote; if even the upper end changes the vote little, the practical answer is "voting works here". (H23)

36. (Committee member) Claude Code and Codex change every few weeks. How could anyone check your results two years from now? Listen for: releases every diff, input and pass-or-fail result, so the analysis can be redone exactly; pins and records every version; the open-weight harness can be rerun exactly; for hosted models the method is the reusable part; a bonus: mixed run order and a daily check task to catch silent model changes. (H36, H37)

37. (Prof. Zheng) My work includes sensitivity analysis. Is there a sensitivity question in your plan? Listen for: yes, the second part of RQ1, how much the vote failure moves when one factor between runs changes; a concrete example: two runs both fail with p² + ρ p(1 - p), so the change per unit of ρ is p(1 - p); honest that it gets harder for the vote and the best N. (H43)

38. (Committee member) A software team won't run 10,000 inputs. What would a team actually do differently after reading your thesis? Listen for: concrete actions, honestly conditioned on the results: switch agent or model instead of rerunning the same one when runs share a rule-driven design; check rules that force one helper; add cheap generated-input tests beyond the PR's tests. (H41)

39. (Prof. Okamura) Your Yuki agent keeps a confidence score of (S + 1) divided by (S + F + 2). What is that formula, statistically? Listen for: Laplace's rule of succession; the posterior mean of a success probability with a uniform Beta(1, 1) prior after S successes and F failures; links it to the Beta and binomial family in the research plan. (W44)

40. (Committee member) Take one bug from your own work and name the fault, the error and the failure. Listen for: a clean mapping, for example BrandMemory: the fault was the priority order plus the 3,200-character limit; the error was a prompt without the brand voice; the failure was a demo that didn't sound like the brand; and detection failed because the log said "applied". (W88)

41. (Prof. Zheng) Your plan says you can build agents but can't yet fit reliability models. What exactly can't you do yet? And what is your statistics background? Listen for: honest: one statistics course, BCS301 (probability distributions, Markov chains, hypothesis tests, ANOVA); can explain the models and do basics on paper; has not yet fitted a beta-binomial or an NHPP to real data and defended the intervals; that is why courses come first; no bluffing. (W91, weak points file item 1)

42. (Committee member) What do you want to do after your master's? Listen for: an honest answer, for example deciding about a PhD after the first real study; a wish to keep working on how far people can trust code that agents write; no promise made only to please the committee. (09 file, Q23)

43. (Prof. Zheng) Your Japanese is at beginner level. How will you manage daily life and lab life? Listen for: honest level, below N5; real study habits only; plans to take the university's Japanese classes; asks whether lab work can be in English at first; mentions the international lab without overselling. (W71, W73)

44. (Prof. Dohi) Quick question. Three identical units, each with reliability 0.9, and a perfect 2-out-of-3 voter. What is the system reliability? Listen for: 3R² - 2R³ = 3(0.81) - 2(0.729) = 2.43 - 1.458 = 0.972; assumes the units fail independently; a bonus: the vote helps only while R is above 0.5.

45. (Prof. Zheng) Do you have any questions for us? Listen for: one or two real questions not already asked in Mocks 1 and 2, for example what Prof. Zheng would like read first, or whether a short online meeting before April would help; short; nothing about money.

Closing

Thank you very much, Sudhanshu. That's all from us today.

The university will announce the results. Thank you, and you can leave the meeting.
