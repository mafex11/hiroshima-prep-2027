How the meeting with Prof. Zheng went (Mon 2 Nov 2026)

When and how long: Look back when needed.

Teams, 13:30 to 13:56 IST, about 26 minutes. Only Prof. Zheng was on the call. This summary is based on a machine speech-to-text record of the call, so his lines are rough; I read them for meaning.

In short

It went well. He had read your CV and proposal, said your topic is close to his own work, and said at 13:42: "I'm very happy, and we are very happy you will come to our lab." He praised your GPA ("8 of 10... very good, very high level, no problem") and your paper ("it's very great"). The call ended with practical next steps, not doubts.

What he said

About your research

  • When you explained that runs sharing one rules file can fail on the same inputs, he agreed: "If they all make the same mistake, only having several versions doesn't really help." He added: "I think that's actually one of the problems we are interested in." (13:37)
  • He said it's quite close to work his group is doing now, and called your proposal "a good starting point". (13:35, 13:45)
  • He described your topic in his words: how different AI models or different instructions affect the reliability of generated code, and the failure dependence between AI-generated programs. That is your RQ1. (13:46)
  • He said: "When you come to our lab, maybe we can adjust your research topic a little to align with the topic of our lab." (13:46)
  • About his N-version paper: they used GPT-4o and 4o-mini, which are old now. They varied the temperature. Newer models don't let you set the temperature, which is why they didn't compare new models. (13:41)
  • Since then they've tried GPT-5, Gemini and local models. Local models sometimes performed poorly. With ChatGPT they have to follow the company's policy. (13:48)
  • He said he'll show you his team's new results on your question later. (13:48)

About the lab

  • He studies how software systems fail, how to measure their reliability and how to make them more dependable, using probability and mathematical modelling. They're starting on AI topics too, and he said that's hard because AI is like a black box.
  • Students come from Kenya, Indonesia, Korea and China. It's a global lab.
  • One lab meeting a week, fixed on Tuesday, where students share progress and problems, plus one-to-one meetings when needed.
  • He doesn't expect you to know everything when you join. He does expect you to read papers, try things out and come up with ideas. One of his lines here was misheard, but that's the meaning.

About the application

  • Confirmed: April 2027 entry, through the admission route for international students living outside Japan.
  • Self-funded: "it's no problem". He also said the university has lots of scholarship opportunities and you can try to apply. You said you'd love to.
  • He'll send you the Consent Form after this call, and an electronic version is fine.
  • The schedule is very tight. He'll check it and "try to get back to you soon".
  • You asked if he'd look at your research plan form before you submit it. He said yes, send a copy to him; he also mentioned Prof. Dohi, but that line was garbled. You said you'd send it today.

About the next meeting (24 Nov to 2 Dec)

  • About 15 minutes of presentation: roughly 10 minutes on your work and 5 minutes on your research proposal.
  • Then about 15 minutes of Q&A, so about half an hour in total. The speech-to-text says "50 minutes", but you checked "so, like half an hour?" and he said "Yes".
  • Q&A questions depend on which professors join. He expects most to be on your research proposal, but they may ask "Why did you choose Japan?" and "Why Hiroshima University?". He said: "Don't worry, it's okay."

Your to-do list

  1. Today: send Prof. Zheng your research plan form (the university form) for review, as you promised.
  2. When his email arrives: sign the Consent Form and send it back. Electronic is fine.
  3. Tonight: finish the UCARO online application and pay the fee (from your plan).
  4. By 6 Nov: courier the paper documents. They must reach the Support Office by 13 Nov, 5 p.m. Japan time.
  5. After that: look at Hiroshima University scholarships (HU-PASP, JASSO), since he suggested applying.
  6. For the next meeting: prepare a 15-minute talk (about 10 minutes on your work, 5 on your proposal) and practise the Q&A, especially "Why Japan?" and "Why Hiroshima?".

How you did

Went well

  • Your research idea came across clearly, and he agreed with the core point.
  • You asked him a sharp question about his paper: does more diversity fail to help because versions still fail on the same hard inputs, or because a higher temperature makes each version worse? He engaged with it and offered to show new results.
  • You asked about the format of the next meeting and offered to send your plan for review. That's proactive and he liked it.
  • You knew the lab: dependable computing, probability models, pysrat.

To improve for the next meeting

  • "Why Japan?": you said Japan has a booming industry and opportunities, and that your brother in Singapore is close by. The professors will likely ask this. A stronger main reason is the lab: it builds probability models of how software fails, and your plan builds on Prof. Zheng's N-version paper. Keep the personal part short.
  • "Why a master's now?": you said you started working early and now want to study. Add the research reason too: tests only cover the cases someone thought of, and you want real numbers for how often agent code fails, which needs models you can't build yet.
  • Your 2023 paper: you called it "below average". Don't undersell it. Say it was a first-year review paper that taught you how to read and summarise research, and that your own experiments start here. If you mention the first prize, make sure you can say who gave it.
  • Lots of "like", "right?" and "I would say". That's normal in a live call, but practise the 15-minute talk out loud a few times.

How Natively did

  • 23 suggestions, each one about 4 to 7 seconds after the question, with no failures or stalls.
  • You read from it for:
    • how you got interested in the topic (#8, almost word for word);
    • what you know about the lab (#14);
    • why testing AI code is hard (#10, #11).
  • You answered the intro, why a master's, why Japan, Japanese and the paper in your own words.
  • Some suggestions fired on "Mm-hmm" or "Okay" and weren't needed:

    • 6 offered to ask about working in English;

    • 13 repeated #14;

    • 21 and #22 were for your own question.

  • Its content matched your facts. For example, #12 about his paper (GPT-4o, temperature) matched what he then said.
  • Speech-to-text struggled with his accent and names (Zain, Thorgy, Pais Rat). Natively still worked out the right questions most of the time.

There was also a short trial call at 11:37 this morning ("Hello, Sudanchu, I'm Jen Jen Jung..."). It wasn't part of the main call.
