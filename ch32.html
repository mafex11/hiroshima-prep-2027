Part 5: Maths

Chapter 32 of 62

Statistics

When and how long: Days 7 to 9.

Your plan fits models by maximum likelihood, takes intervals from a bootstrap, reports Cohen's kappa and uses task as a random effect. A professor who wants to ask about your statistics will start from those words. This file gives you the definitions in plain words and 25 worked problems.

For the "why" behind p values, effect sizes, power and multiple comparisons, from_NAIST/04_statistics_primer.md sections 6 to 11 are still good. Its plan numbers are from your NAIST plan, so do not quote them here.

How the answers were checked. Every numeric answer was recomputed on 12 Nov 2026 with Python (numpy, scipy), and again on 13 Nov 2026: the t and chi-square tests with scipy.stats, the regression with linregress, the Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg results by hand and with scipy, and S1 and S23 by simulation (and exact enumeration for S23). Items marked [CHECK] are ones I am not fully sure of.

Part 1. Statements to say out loud

1.1 Estimation

  • An estimator is a rule that turns data into a guess theta-hat. It is random, because the data are.
  • Bias = E[theta-hat] - theta. MSE = E[(theta-hat - theta)^2] = Var(theta-hat) + bias^2. A small bias can be worth it if it cuts the variance a lot (S2).
  • Consistent: theta-hat tends to theta as n grows.
  • Method of moments: set sample moments equal to model moments and solve. Quick, often used as a starting value.
  • Maximum likelihood (MLE): choose the parameter that makes the observed data most probable. L(theta) = prod_i f(x_i; theta); maximise log L = sum_i log f(x_i; theta). Usually solve d log L / d theta = 0 (the score equation), numerically if there is no closed form.
  • Standard MLEs: exponential lambda-hat = 1/x-bar (with censoring: failures / total time on test). Poisson lambda-hat = x-bar. Binomial p-hat = j/n. Normal mu-hat = x-bar, sigma^2-hat = (1/n) sum (x_i - x-bar)^2 (biased; s^2 divides by n - 1).
  • Fisher information I(theta) = -E[d^2 log f(X; theta) / d theta^2], the expected curvature of the log-likelihood. For large n, theta-hat is approximately Normal(theta, 1/(n I(theta))). Sharper peak means more information and a smaller variance.
  • Invariance: the MLE of g(theta) is g(theta-hat). Delta method: Var g(theta-hat) is about g'(theta)^2 Var theta-hat.
  • Say it: "Maximum likelihood picks the parameter that makes the data most probable. For large samples the estimate is roughly normal, with variance one over n times the Fisher information, which is the curvature of the log-likelihood."

1.2 Intervals

  • A 95% confidence interval comes from a method that covers the true value in 95% of repeated samples. It is not "a 95% chance that theta is in this one interval" (that is the Bayesian credible interval).
  • Known sigma: x-bar +- 1.96 sigma/sqrt(n). Unknown sigma: x-bar +- t_{n-1, 0.975} s/sqrt(n).
  • Proportion: Wald p-hat +- 1.96 sqrt(p-hat(1 - p-hat)/n), poor near 0 or 1. Prefer Wilson. Zero failures in n: upper 95% bound about 3/n.
  • Wald interval from an MLE: theta-hat +- 1.96 / sqrt(n I(theta-hat)).

1.3 Tests

  • H0 (null) against H1 (alternative). A test statistic measures how far the data are from H0.
  • p value: the probability, if H0 is true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not P(H0 is true), and it is not the size of the effect.
  • Type I error: reject a true H0 (rate alpha). Type II error: keep a false H0 (rate beta). Power = 1 - beta.
  • z test (known variance or large n), t test (estimated variance: one-sample, paired, two-sample Welch), chi-square (goodness of fit; independence in a table), F test / ANOVA (several group means).
  • Multiple testing: with m independent tests at alpha and all nulls true, P(at least one false positive) = 1 - (1 - alpha)^m (and at most m alpha in general). Bonferroni (alpha/m) and Holm control that family-wise error. Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false discovery rate (the expected share of false findings among the findings).
  • Likelihood ratio test: 2 (log L_big - log L_small) is roughly chi-square with df = number of extra parameters, if the small model is not on the edge of the big one.

1.4 Models

  • Least squares regression: choose beta to minimise sum (y_i - x_i' beta)^2. Normal equations X'X beta = X'y, so beta-hat = (X'X)^(-1) X'y. R^2 = share of the variance of y explained. With normal errors it is also the MLE.
  • Logistic regression: logit p = log(p/(1 - p)) = beta_0 + beta_1 x. exp(beta_1) is the odds ratio per unit of x. Fitted by MLE with Newton-Raphson (IRLS), no closed form.
  • Mixed (random-effects) models: some effects are fixed (chosen levels you want to estimate, like file version) and some random (levels sampled from a population, like tasks). A random intercept gives each task its own baseline. Results generalise to new tasks, and the standard errors respect the correlation of runs within a task.
  • Model choice: AIC = 2k - 2 log L. BIC = k ln n - 2 log L. Smaller is better. BIC penalises extra parameters more when n > 7 (ln n > 2).

1.5 Bootstrap

  • Resample the data with replacement, recompute the statistic, repeat B times (1,000 to 10,000). The spread of the B values estimates the standard error. The 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles give a percentile interval.
  • Nonparametric: resample the observed units. Parametric: simulate new data from the fitted model (the lab's NHPP papers do this, for example Wu, Dohi, Zheng and Okamura, DASC 2025 and Journal of Systems and Software 2026).
  • Resample the independent units. In your plan those are runs, not inputs (file 05, D11).

1.6 Agreement

  • Cohen's kappa: (p_o - p_e)/(1 - p_e). 1 = perfect, 0 = chance level, below 0 = worse than chance.

Part 2. 25 problems with full solutions

S1. Why divide by n - 1?

Show that E[X-bar] = mu and that E[(1/n) sum (X_i - X-bar)^2] = (n - 1) sigma^2 / n.

Solution. E[X-bar] = (1/n) sum E[X_i] = mu. For the variance, use the identity

sum (X_i - X-bar)^2 = sum (X_i - mu)^2 - n (X-bar - mu)^2
E[ ... ] = n sigma^2 - n (sigma^2 / n) = (n - 1) sigma^2

So dividing by n is biased low. Dividing by n - 1 gives an unbiased s^2. The reason: X-bar is fitted to the same data, so the deviations from it are a little too small.

Check. Simulation with n = 5 and sigma^2 = 4: the divide-by-n average was 3.20 (= 4 * 4/5) and the divide-by-(n - 1) average was 4.00.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the deviations from X-bar are a little too small. Why are the deviations from X-bar smaller than the deviations from the true mu?"
    You: "Because X-bar is the centre that makes the sum of squared deviations as small as possible, so any other centre, the true mu included, gives a bigger sum. The gap is exactly n times (X-bar minus mu) squared, and on average that is one sigma squared. So the sum loses one sigma squared, and that is what people mean when they say fitting the mean uses up one degree of freedom."
  2. Prof: "You said fitting the mean uses up one degree of freedom. What happens when you fit more than one parameter, say a straight line?"
    You: "Each fitted coefficient uses up one more. For a line, the residuals must sum to zero and also sum to zero when weighted by x, so the residual sum of squares has expectation (n minus 2) sigma squared, and I divide by n minus 2 to get an unbiased variance."
  3. Prof: "You keep choosing the divisor to remove the bias. Is an unbiased estimate always the best choice?"
    You: "Not always. For normal data, dividing the sum of squares by n gives a smaller mean squared error than dividing by n minus 1, even though it is biased, and dividing by n plus 1 gives the smallest. With n = 5 and sigma squared = 1 the three mean squared errors are 0.50, 0.36 and 0.33, so what matters is bias squared plus variance, not bias alone."

S2. Bias against variance

J failures in n = 10 runs. Compare p-hat = J/n with p-tilde = (J + 1)/(n + 2) by MSE, at p = 0.1 and at p = 0.5.

Solution.

p-hat:   unbiased, MSE = p(1 - p)/n
p-tilde: bias = (np + 1)/(n + 2) - p = (1 - 2p)/(n + 2),  Var = n p (1 - p)/(n + 2)^2

p = 0.1: MSE(p-hat) = 0.0090.  p-tilde: bias 0.0667, Var 0.00625, MSE 0.0107.  -> p-hat better
p = 0.5: MSE(p-hat) = 0.0250.  p-tilde: bias 0,      Var 0.0174,  MSE 0.0174.  -> p-tilde better

Neither wins everywhere. p-tilde is the posterior mean under a uniform prior; it shrinks toward 0.5.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said p-tilde is the posterior mean under a uniform prior. Can you show that?"
    You: "The uniform prior on p is a Beta(1, 1). With J failures in n runs the likelihood is p to the J times (1 minus p) to the (n minus J), so the posterior is Beta(J plus 1, n minus J plus 1). A Beta(a, b) has mean a over (a plus b), so the posterior mean is (J plus 1) over (n plus 2)."
  2. Prof: "Why a uniform prior? What changes with another Beta prior?"
    You: "The uniform prior is the simple choice: every p is equally likely, and it acts like one imaginary failure and one imaginary success. With a Beta(a, b) prior the posterior mean is (J plus a) over (n plus a plus b), so it shrinks toward a over (a plus b), and a plus b works like that many imaginary earlier runs. For failure probabilities, which are usually small, a prior centred near zero would make more sense than one centred at one half."
  3. Prof: "Where would such a prior come from in your plan, and what is the risk?"
    You: "In my plan the Beta already describes how the failure chance varies across inputs, and I fit a and b by maximum likelihood. Using it as the prior for one input gives (J plus a) over (n plus a plus b), which is called empirical Bayes. The risk is that a and b come from the same data, so I'd refit them in every bootstrap resample of the runs instead of treating them as known, or the intervals come out too narrow."

S3. Method of moments

(a) Lifetimes 2, 5, 3, 10. Moment estimate of an exponential rate? (b) theta has mean 0.1 and variance 0.01. Moment estimates of a Beta(a, b)?

Solution. (a) E[X] = 1/lambda, so lambda-hat = 1/x-bar = 1/5 = 0.2. (b) From Var = m(1 - m)/(a + b + 1): a + b = m(1 - m)/v - 1 = 0.09/0.01 - 1 = 8. Then a = m(a + b) = 0.8 and b = 7.2. These are good starting values for the beta-binomial MLE in your plan.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the moment estimates are good starting values for the MLE. Why not just stop at the moment estimates?"
    You: "They're quick, but they usually have a larger variance than the MLE for the same data, and they can fall outside the allowed range. For the beta-binomial, if the variance of the failure counts across inputs comes out smaller than the binomial variance, the moment formula gives a negative rho."
  2. Prof: "When would that happen, and does a negative rho mean anything?"
    You: "It happens by chance when the true rho is near zero and there are few inputs. A Beta mixture can't produce it, because the chance that two runs both fail is p squared plus Var theta, and a variance is never negative. So within this model I'd set rho to zero, which is the plain binomial."
  3. Prof: "Could two versions ever fail together less often than independence? Give me an example."
    You: "Yes, in principle, if what is hard for one version is easy for the other, say one tends to fail on empty inputs and the other on very large ones. Littlewood and Miller showed in 1989 that versions built with deliberately different methods, which they called forced diversity, can then do better than independence. Runs from one setting can't, by Eckhardt and Lee's argument, but runs from two settings can, which is why my plan looks at the covariance between settings."

S4. MLE for exponential lifetimes, with censoring

(a) Lifetimes 2, 5, 3, 10: MLE of lambda? (b) 5 units are tested for 100 h. Three fail at 20, 50 and 80 h. Two still work at 100 h. MLE of lambda and of the MTTF? What if you ignored the two survivors?

Solution. (a) log L = n log lambda - lambda sum x_i. Score: n/lambda - sum x_i = 0, so lambda-hat = 4/20 = 0.2 (here the same as the moment estimate). (b) A failure at t contributes f(t) = lambda e^(-lambda t). A survivor to 100 h contributes R(100) = e^(-100 lambda).

log L = 3 log lambda - lambda (20 + 50 + 80 + 100 + 100) = 3 log lambda - 350 lambda
lambda-hat = r / (total time on test) = 3/350 = 0.00857 per hour,  MTTF-hat = 116.7 h

Ignoring the survivors gives 3/150 = 0.02 and an MTTF of 50 h, which is far too pessimistic.

Say it. "With censoring, the MLE of an exponential rate is the number of failures over the total time on test. Units that survived still count, through their reliability."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Your MTTF of 116.7 hours comes from only three failures. How precise is it?"
    You: "Not very. The observed information is r over lambda squared, so the standard error of lambda-hat is lambda-hat over root 3, about 0.005, and a Wald interval for lambda would reach below zero. So I'd use a chi-square interval instead, which puts the MTTF somewhere between about 40 and 570 hours."
  2. Prof: "Where does that chi-square come from?"
    You: "If the test stops at the r-th failure, the total time on test is a sum of r exponential pieces, so 2 lambda times it is chi-square with 2r degrees of freedom. Here the test stopped at a fixed 100 hours, so for the upper bound on lambda the usual choice is 2r plus 2 degrees of freedom, which allows for a failure that might have come just after the stop."
  3. Prof: "Stopping at a fixed time is also how software testing data look. How does this connect to the NHPP models our lab fits?"
    You: "The five units are like five faults, each found after an exponential time, which is the Jelinski-Moranda model. If the number of faults is instead Poisson with mean omega, the failure times form an NHPP with mean value omega times (1 minus e to the minus lambda t), the Goel-Okumoto model. Both log-likelihoods are the sum of the log failure rate at each failure time minus the area under the failure rate up to the stop. Here that area is lambda times the 350 unit-hours, so I get 3 log lambda minus 350 lambda plus a constant."

S5. MLE for Poisson and binomial

(a) Daily failure counts 2, 0, 3, 1, 4. MLE of the Poisson mean? (b) 3 failures in 20 runs. MLE of p? Show it is a maximum.

Solution. (a) log L = (sum x_i) log lambda - n lambda - sum log x_i!. Score: sum x_i / lambda - n = 0, so lambda-hat = x-bar = 2. (b) log L = j log p + (n - j) log(1 - p). Score: j/p - (n - j)/(1 - p) = 0, so p-hat = j/n = 0.15. Second derivative: -j/p^2 - (n - j)/(1 - p)^2 < 0, so it is a maximum.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Do your daily counts 2, 0, 3, 1, 4 actually look Poisson? How would you check?"
    You: "For a Poisson the variance equals the mean, and here the mean is 2 and the sample variance is 2.5. The dispersion statistic, the sum of squared deviations over the mean, is 10 over 2, which is 5 on 4 degrees of freedom, with p about 0.29. So there's no evidence against the Poisson, though five days can't show much either way."
  2. Prof: "And if the variance were much bigger than the mean?"
    You: "That's overdispersion, and it usually means the rate itself changes from day to day. If the rate follows a gamma distribution, the counts are negative binomial, which is the same idea as my beta-binomial, a binomial whose p varies from input to input."
  3. Prof: "Has our lab compared those two kinds of model for software bugs?"
    You: "Yes. As I understand it from the abstract, Hagihara, Dohi and Okamura in 2024 compared a Poisson prior with a negative binomial prior for the number of bugs left, and on real bug count data the plain Poisson model predicted better. So extra flexibility doesn't automatically help, and I should check that my beta-binomial really beats the plain binomial before I rely on it."

S6. MLE for the normal

Data 4, 6, 5, 9. MLEs of mu and sigma^2? Compare with s^2.

Solution. mu-hat = x-bar = 6. sigma^2-hat = (4 + 0 + 1 + 9)/4 = 3.5 (MLE, divides by n). s^2 = 14/3 = 4.67 (unbiased).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why does the MLE of sigma squared divide by n and not n minus 1?"
    You: "Because the likelihood is maximised over mu and sigma squared together. With mu set to x-bar, setting the derivative in sigma squared to zero gives the average squared deviation, 14 over 4, which is 3.5. The likelihood doesn't care about bias, it just picks the value that makes the data most probable."
  2. Prof: "Is there a likelihood method that does give n minus 1?"
    You: "Yes, restricted maximum likelihood, or REML. It maximises the likelihood of the residuals after the mean is removed, which accounts for the degree of freedom that mu used up, and here it gives exactly s squared, 4.67."
  3. Prof: "Where would REML matter in your plan?"
    You: "In the RQ2 model with task as a random effect. With only 10 tasks, the maximum likelihood estimate of the between-task variance is biased low for the same reason as dividing by n, and REML largely corrects that. In R's lme4 package, lmer uses REML by default, but glmer, the version for pass or fail outcomes, has no REML option, so for a logistic model I'd keep that bias in mind."

S7. Fisher information and a Wald interval

(a) Find I(lambda) for one exponential observation and the approximate variance of lambda-hat. (b) n = 50 and lambda-hat = 0.2: a 95% Wald interval. (c) For the binomial in S5(b), the standard error of p-hat.

Solution. (a) log f = log lambda - lambda x. Second derivative: -1/lambda^2. So I(lambda) = 1/lambda^2 and Var(lambda-hat) is about lambda^2/n. (b) SE = 0.2/sqrt(50) = 0.0283. Interval 0.2 +- 1.96 (0.0283) = (0.145, 0.255). (c) I(p) = 1/(p(1 - p)), so SE = sqrt(p(1 - p)/n) = sqrt(0.15 * 0.85 / 20) = 0.080.

Say it. "Fisher information is the expected curvature of the log-likelihood. A sharp peak pins the parameter down, so the variance of the MLE is roughly one over n times the information."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Your Wald interval is symmetric around 0.2. Is that right for a rate?"
    You: "Not exactly, because lambda-hat is 1 over x-bar, which is skewed to the right, and a rate can't go below zero. Here there is an exact interval, since 2 lambda times the sum of the lifetimes is chi-square with 100 degrees of freedom, and it gives 0.148 to 0.259. So with 50 lifetimes the Wald interval is only off by about 0.004, but with a handful of failures the gap would be much bigger."
  2. Prof: "When there is no exact interval, how would you judge an interval method?"
    You: "By its coverage in a simulation: draw many samples from a known lambda, build the interval each time, and count how often it contains the truth and on which side it misses. For this case the Wald interval covers about 95 percent overall, but it lies entirely below the true rate in about 3 percent of samples and entirely above it in under 2 percent, so its misses are lopsided."
  3. Prof: "Would you run that coverage check on the bootstrap interval for rho in your plan?"
    You: "Yes, I'd want to. I'd simulate ten runs over many inputs from a Beta model with a known rho, do the whole fit and the run bootstrap, and repeat that many times to count how often the interval contains the true rho. With only ten runs I expect it to cover less than 95 percent, and the check would tell me by how much."

S8. Invariance and the delta method

With lambda-hat = 0.2 from n = 50 exponential lifetimes: MLE of the MTTF, its standard error, and the MLE of R(1) = P(T > 1).

Solution. MTTF = 1/lambda, so the MLE is 1/0.2 = 5 (invariance). Delta method: |g'(lambda)| = 1/lambda^2 = 25, so SE = 25 * 0.0283 = 0.71. (Directly: MTTF-hat = x-bar, SE = 5/sqrt(50) = 0.71.) R(1)-hat = e^(-0.2) = 0.819.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "What is the standard error of your estimate of R(1)?"
    You: "By the delta method, R is e to the minus lambda, so the size of its derivative is also e to the minus lambda, about 0.819. The standard error is 0.819 times 0.0283, about 0.023, so R(1) is about 0.82 plus or minus 0.045."
  2. Prof: "If the reliability were close to 1, could that kind of interval go above 1?"
    You: "Yes, a delta method interval can cross 1. The fix is to take the interval for lambda and push its end points through e to the minus lambda. Because that map is monotone, the new interval has exactly the coverage of the old one and stays between 0 and 1, and from the Wald interval for lambda it gives 0.775 to 0.865."
  3. Prof: "What if the quantity depends on two parameters, like the software reliability from an NHPP model?"
    You: "Then there's no single monotone map, so I'd use the multivariate delta method, the gradient times the inverse information matrix times the gradient, or a bootstrap. As I understand the abstracts, the recent papers by Wu, Dohi, Zheng and Okamura propose parametric bootstrap methods for exactly this, because analytical intervals for NHPP reliability measures are hard to get."

S9. A z interval

x-bar = 50, sigma = 10 (known), n = 25. 95% interval? What does it mean?

Solution. SE = 10/5 = 2. 50 +- 3.92 = (46.08, 53.92). If you repeated the study many times, 95% of intervals made this way would contain the true mean.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "So is there a 95 percent probability that the true mean is between 46.08 and 53.92?"
    You: "Not in the frequentist sense. The true mean is fixed, so this one interval either contains it or it doesn't, and the 95 percent describes the method over repeated samples. A probability statement about the mean itself needs a Bayesian credible interval, and that needs a prior."
  2. Prof: "With a flat prior, what would that credible interval be here?"
    You: "With sigma known and a flat prior on the mean, the posterior is normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 2. So the 95 percent credible interval is the same numbers, 46.08 to 53.92, and only the reading changes."
  3. Prof: "Then when do the two really differ, in your kind of data?"
    You: "When the data are thin and the prior carries real information. With zero failures in 20 runs, the exact upper bound is 0.139 and a uniform prior gives a Beta(1, 21) posterior with a 95 percent upper limit of 0.133, so they're still close. But a prior from earlier versions worth another 20 clean runs would give Beta(1, 41) and pull the bound down to about 0.07, and then the prior is driving the answer."

S10. A t interval

n = 9, x-bar = 12, s = 3. 95% interval?

Solution. SE = 3/3 = 1. t with 8 df at 0.975 = 2.306. 12 +- 2.306 = (9.69, 14.31). You use t because sigma is estimated, which adds uncertainty; t has heavier tails than the normal.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "How much wider is that than if sigma were known to be 3?"
    You: "With known sigma I'd use 1.96 instead of 2.306, so the half-width would be 1.96 instead of 2.31, about 15 percent narrower. That extra width is the price of estimating sigma from 9 points, and it shrinks as n grows, because t approaches the normal."
  2. Prof: "What does the t interval assume, and what if the data were skewed, like failure times?"
    You: "It assumes the data are roughly normal, though for larger n the central limit theorem makes it fairly robust. With 9 skewed, positive values like failure times, I'd rather fit a lifetime model such as the exponential or Weibull by maximum likelihood, or work on the log scale."
  3. Prof: "Say the 9 values were exponential lifetimes with an average of 12. What exact interval would you give for the true mean?"
    You: "The sum of the lifetimes is 108, and 2 times the sum divided by the mean is chi-square with 18 degrees of freedom. So the mean lies between 216 over 31.53 and 216 over 8.23, about 6.9 to 26.2. That's far wider than the t interval, and skewed to the right, because an exponential with mean 12 has a standard deviation of 12, not 3."

S11. Intervals for a proportion, and zero failures

(a) 30 of 400 inputs fail. Wald and Wilson 95% intervals? (b) 0 failures in 20 runs, and 0 in 10,000 inputs: 95% upper bounds?

Solution. (a) p-hat = 0.075, SE = sqrt(0.075 * 0.925 / 400) = 0.0132. Wald: (0.049, 0.101). Wilson: (0.053, 0.105). (b) The exact one-sided 95% upper bound solves (1 - p)^n = 0.05, so p = 1 - 0.05^(1/n). n = 20: 0.139 (rule of three: 3/20 = 0.15). n = 10,000: 0.0003.

Say it. "If nothing fails in n tries, I can still say the failure probability is probably below about 3 over n. Zero failures in 20 runs only tells me it's below about 14 percent."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why is the Wilson interval shifted up compared with Wald?"
    You: "Wilson comes from inverting the score test, so it uses the hypothesised p in the standard error instead of p-hat. S12. A z test for a proportion

S16. Type I and II errors, power and sample size

H0: mu = 0 against H1: mu = 0.5, sigma = 1, n = 25, one-sided alpha = 0.05. (a) The rejection rule. (b) Power at mu = 0.5. (c) n for 80% power.

Solution. (a) SE = 1/5 = 0.2. Reject if x-bar > 1.645 (0.2) = 0.329. (b) Power = P(x-bar > 0.329 | mu = 0.5) = P(Z > (0.329 - 0.5)/0.2) = P(Z > -0.855) = 0.80. Type II error 0.20. (c) n = ((z_alpha + z_beta) sigma / delta)^2 = ((1.645 + 0.842)/0.5)^2 = 24.7, so n = 25.

Say it. "A type I error is a false alarm, rejecting a true null, and alpha controls it. A type II error is a miss. Power is one minus the miss rate, and it grows with the effect size and the sample size."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You allow a 20 percent chance of a miss but only 5 percent for a false alarm. Why that imbalance?"
    You: "It's a convention from Cohen, who suggested 80 percent power and pointed out that it treats a false alarm as about four times as serious as a miss, 0.20 against 0.05. It isn't a law, and the balance should follow what each mistake costs in the problem."
  2. Prof: "In software reliability, which mistake costs more?"
    You: "Usually letting a bad program through. If H0 says the program meets its target and I keep H0 whenever the data don't clearly reject it, an unreliable program can pass just because I ran too few cases. So reliability demonstration turns it around: the program is declared good only when the data are strong enough."
  3. Prof: "Then how many failure-free runs do you need to claim a failure probability below 1 percent with 95 percent confidence?"
    You: "If the true probability were 1 percent, all n runs would pass with probability 0.99 to the n, and I need that to be at most 0.05. So n is at least ln 0.05 over ln 0.99, about 298.1, which means 299 runs, close to the rule of three, 3 over 0.01. If I allow one failure, I need 473 runs."

S17. Reading a p value

Gloaguen et al. [8] found that developer-written context files raised task success by 2.4% on average, with p = 0.21 (the paper writes "p = 21%"). What can you conclude, and what not?

Solution.

  • You can say: a difference at least this large would be fairly common (21% of the time) if the files had no effect. So the data are compatible with no effect.
  • You cannot say: "the files have no effect", or "there is a 21% chance the null is true". A large p value can mean "not enough data".
  • Better: report the effect with a confidence interval, which would show both no effect and a few points of gain as plausible.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said a confidence interval would show both no effect and a few points of gain as plausible. Can you get that interval from the p value alone?"
    You: "Roughly, if I treat it as a z test, though the paper actually used a two-sided Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test, which pools several 2 by 2 tables. A two-sided p of 0.21 means z is about 1.25, so the standard error is about 2.4 over 1.25, about 1.9. The 95 percent interval is then 2.4 plus or minus 3.8, roughly minus 1.4 to plus 6.2."
  2. Prof: "If the true gain really were 2.4, how often would a study of that size detect it?"
    You: "With a standard error of 1.9, the effect is about 1.25 standard errors, so the chance of getting past 1.96 is only about 24 percent. To reach 80 percent power, the standard error would have to fall to about 0.86, which takes about five times as much data."
  3. Prof: "But 2.4 is the observed effect. Isn't power at the observed effect just the p value in another form?"
    You: "Yes. Power computed at the observed effect is a fixed function of the p value, so it tells me nothing new about that study, and it's only useful for planning the next one. So for RQ2 I'd fix in advance the smallest change worth finding, work out what my 10 tasks can detect, and report the effect with its interval rather than lean on the p value."

S18. Multiple testing

(a) 20 independent tests, all nulls true, each at 0.05. P(at least one false positive)? (b) Four p values 0.01, 0.04, 0.03, 0.005 at alpha = 0.05. Which survive Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg?

Solution. (a) 1 - 0.95^20 = 0.64. Bonferroni threshold 0.05/20 = 0.0025. (b) Sort: 0.005, 0.01, 0.03, 0.04 (m = 4).

Bonferroni (each vs 0.0125):          0.005 yes, 0.01 yes, 0.03 no, 0.04 no      -> 2 rejected
Holm (vs 0.05/4, 0.05/3, 0.05/2, ...): 0.005 <= 0.0125 yes, 0.01 <= 0.0167 yes,
                                       0.03 <= 0.025 no -> stop                    -> 2 rejected
BH (vs i * 0.05/4 = 0.0125, 0.025, 0.0375, 0.05):
                                       largest i with p_(i) <= i q/m is i = 4      -> all 4 rejected

BH is less strict because it controls the share of false findings, not the chance of any.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You found 0.64 for 20 tests. Where would you face many tests in your own plan?"
    You: "In RQ2, if I test each of my roughly 5 rules separately. With 5 tests at 5 percent and no real effects, the chance of at least one false alarm is 1 minus 0.95 to the 5, about 0.23, and RQ1 adds a comparison for each change between runs."
  2. Prof: "Which correction would you use there, and why?"
    You: "Holm, if I want to claim that a particular rule matters, because it controls the chance of any false claim and always rejects at least as much as Bonferroni. S19. One-way ANOVA (your BCS301 topic)

SS_between = 3[(5-7)^2 + (7-7)^2 + (9-7)^2] = 24,  df = 2,  MS_b = 12
SS_within  = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6,                        df = 6,  MS_w = 1

S21. Logistic regression

A model says logit P(pass) = -2 + 0.7 x, where x is the number of helpful rules. (a) P(pass) at x = 0 and x = 2. (b) The odds ratio per rule. (c) Why not ordinary regression for pass/fail?

Solution. (a) x = 0: p = 1/(1 + e^2) = 0.119. x = 2: logit = -0.6, p = 1/(1 + e^0.6) = 0.354. (b) e^0.7 = 2.01: each rule doubles the odds (odds 0.135 at x = 0, 0.549 at x = 2 = 0.135 e^1.4). It does not double the probability. (c) A straight line can predict below 0 or above 1, and the variance of a 0/1 outcome depends on p. The logit maps (0, 1) to the whole real line. The fit is by maximum likelihood, solved numerically.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said one rule doubles the odds but not the probability. What does one rule do to the probability, starting from x = 0?"
    You: "The logit goes from minus 2 to minus 1.3, so p goes from 0.119 to 1 over (1 plus e to the 1.3), about 0.214, which is 1.8 times. That's close to double because when p is small, the odds p over (1 minus p) are nearly the same as p. Near one half the gap is large: doubling odds of 1 gives odds of 2, so p only goes from 0.5 to 0.67."
  2. Prof: "Your 1.8 times rests on the slope being exactly 0.7. How would you estimate it from data, and its standard error?"
    You: "I'd maximise the log-likelihood, the sum of y log p plus (1 minus y) log(1 minus p), with Newton-Raphson, since there's no closed form.   3. Prof: "When does that maximum likelihood fit break down?"

S22. Random effects in plain terms

S23. The bootstrap by hand

Taking my labels as the truth, it finds 20 of my 30 yeses, a sensitivity of 0.67, and 15 of my 20 noes, a specificity of 0.75. Kappa hides that direction, and McNemar's exact test on 10 against 5 gives p about 0.30, so 50 items can't show that the lean is real."
  2. Prof: "You took your own labels as the truth. Are they?"
    You: "Not necessarily, since I'm one person and I can be inconsistent. I could relabel a sample a week later to measure my own consistency, or ask a second person to label it and compute kappa between us. If two people only reach a kappa of 0.6, I can't expect the checker to do much better."
  3. Prof: "Whether it's you against a second person or the checker against you, how many items does kappa need to be precise?"
    You: "A simple large-sample formula gives the standard error of kappa as the square root of p_o times (1 minus p_o) over n, divided by (1 minus p_e). With 90 percent agreement and chance agreement of 0.545, which gives a kappa of 0.78, on 20 items that's about 0.15, so the true kappa could be anywhere from about 0.5 to 1. To get within plus or minus 0.1, I'd need about 170 items, so 20 items, my plan's minimum, can only rule out a poor checker."

S25. AIC, BIC and a likelihood ratio test on the boundary

On n = 200 inputs, a binomial (1 parameter) has max log L = -520.3 and a beta-binomial (2 parameters) has -510.1. Compare by AIC and BIC. Test H0: rho = 0.

Solution.

AIC: binomial 2(1) + 1040.6 = 1042.6.     beta-binomial 2(2) + 1020.2 = 1024.2
BIC: binomial ln(200) + 1040.6 = 1045.9.  beta-binomial 2 ln(200) + 1020.2 = 1030.8

Both choose the beta-binomial. Likelihood ratio: 2 (520.3 - 510.1) = 20.4. The binomial is the beta-binomial with rho = 0, which is on the edge of the allowed range (rho >= 0). On the boundary the null distribution is a 50:50 mix of 0 and chi-square with 1 df (Self and Liang, 1987), so p = 0.5 P(chi^2_1 > 20.4) = 3.1e-6 instead of 6.3e-6. Either way, reject rho = 0.

Follow-up. Which n goes into BIC? Here the number of inputs. With runs shared across inputs, the "number of independent units" is not clear, which is one more reason to rely on the run bootstrap. [CHECK: which n to use in BIC for crossed data like yours; ask Prof. Zheng]

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "The beta-binomial wins on AIC by 18.4. How strong is that in plain terms?"
    You: "One way is Akaike weights: each model gets e to the minus half its AIC gap from the best, scaled so the weights sum to one. The binomial's gap is 18.4, so its weight is e to the minus 9.2, about 0.0001, and the beta-binomial gets almost all of it. Burnham and Anderson's rough rule is that a gap above 10 means the worse model has essentially no support."
  2. Prof: "Where are Akaike weights useful beyond choosing one model?"
    You: "For model averaging: when several models are close, I can average their predictions with those weights instead of betting on one. As I understand it, Wu, Dohi and Okamura did this in 2024 for NHPP software reliability models, and on real bug data the averaged prediction was more stable than the single model with the smallest AIC."
  3. Prof: "But the model that fits best is not always the one that predicts best. How would you check prediction in your setting?"
    You: "By holding data out: I'd fit on some of the runs and check how well the model predicts the held-out runs, for example how often a 2-of-3 vote over them fails, against what actually happened. As I understand its abstract, a 2024 chapter by Wu, Dohi and Okamura makes the same point, that the model chosen by an information criterion is not guaranteed to predict future bug counts best."
