2. What we could find about calls like this

I searched in English and Japanese on 30 Oct 2026 (Hiroshima University pre-application meetings, the Informatics and Data Science Program, the online oral exam). No applicant has written up a meeting with this lab or this program. So the plan below is a best guess. It is built from his own email, the official guideline, and the way these meetings usually go in Japanese graduate schools.

How a 30-minute call with a Japanese associate professor who has already agreed usually goes (general pattern, not checked for this lab):

  • He leads. He sets the order. Often a short hello, then "please introduce yourself", then the research, then the practical steps. Some professors do the practical steps first. Follow him.
  • It is a working talk, not a grilling. Expect real questions on the plan, but in the tone of "how would you handle this?", not "prove it".
  • The questions follow his own work. He builds probability models of failure for a living. Expect "what is random here?", "why this distribution?", "how sure are your numbers?" (see section 6).
  • He may check two things quietly: is your English easy to follow, and is your maths ready for a statistics-heavy lab.
  • The end is practical. Forms, dates, who sends what. Japanese professors are usually careful with procedure, so be precise and repeat back what you agreed.
  • Time is respected. 17:00 JST is late afternoon in Japan. He gave 30 minutes. Do not run over.

4. When to offer the slides

The sentence, word for word:

"I prepared five short slides, about five minutes. May I share my screen?"

Offer them the moment he moves to the research, for example after "Could you explain your research plan?" or "So, about your plan...".

Do not offer them:

  • before he has asked about the research,
  • if he says "I have read your proposal, I have some questions". Then answer his questions. Offer one slide only if a question needs the figure: "May I show the design figure? It's one slide."
  • if fewer than 12 minutes are left. Say instead: "To save time, I'll explain it in one minute," and give the short version.

If he says yes:

  1. Click Share in Teams and pick the window with the slides PDF, not the whole screen. Your notes stay private.
  2. Ask: "Can you see the slides?"
  3. Five slides, about one minute each. Stop after each slide if he wants to ask something. A question in the middle is a good sign.
  4. After the last slide: stop sharing, and say "That's all from me. I'm happy to take any questions."

If he says no, or the share fails: do not fight Teams for more than 30 seconds. "Sorry, let me just explain it without slides." Then the 2-minute spoken version from 08_Presentation/FULL_SCRIPT.md.

6. The questions most likely in 30 minutes

Ranked by how likely they are. Full model answers: 03_Research_Plan_Defense/ (question bank) and the older list in /Users/mafex/code/personal/masters-research/2027-refresh/hiroshima-application/meeting-prep/QUESTIONS.md. These guesses come from his papers, not from anything he said.

About the plan (almost certain)

  1. "Explain your research in a few sentences." Repeated agent runs on one real task are N versions. RQ1: how much more often they fail together than independence predicts, and which change between runs lowers that most. RQ2: does replacing one real rule with neutral text change reliability beyond the tests.
  2. "How is this different from our N-version paper?" "I've only read the abstract, because the full text is behind the Springer paywall. Your study varied temperature and conversation history reuse. My plan takes the question to real repository tasks with coding agents, and adds the shared context file as a possible cause of common failures. Could I ask you for a copy?"
  3. "Why can't you assume the runs fail independently?" Eckhardt and Lee: two runs both fail with probability p^2 + Var θ. Inputs differ in difficulty, so Var θ is above zero.
  4. "Why a beta-binomial model?" θ is a chance between 0 and 1 that changes from input to input. Beta is a flexible model for that. Its intraclass correlation ρ = 1/(a + b + 1) is zero under independence.
  5. "How sure are your numbers?" A bootstrap that resamples runs gives intervals for ρ and for the vote failure chance. I report a range, not one number.
  6. "How do you find failures the tests miss?" Only runs that pass the PR's tests count as versions. Then at least 10,000 generated inputs per entry point, compared with the merged code. The honest limit: if the merged code has the same bug, this can't see it.

About you (likely)

  1. "What statistics have you studied?" Only BCS301 in the third semester: distributions, Markov chains, hypothesis tests, ANOVA. That is why courses come first in the plan.
  2. "Can you do it in two years? What about the cost?" 820 runs, about 420 to 590 US dollars at the prices in [8]. RQ1 is the core. The field data part can be dropped.
  3. "How will you pay for your studies?" "I'll fund my studies myself." (Confirmed 29 Oct.)
  4. "Do you speak Japanese?" "I'm a beginner, below N5." [CONFIRM: add how you are studying only if it is true.]
  5. "Are you applying anywhere else?" See section 7.

9. Traps to avoid

  • Talking too long. A 3-minute answer is a tenth of the call. Short answer, one example, stop.
  • Saying you read his N-version paper. You have read the abstract only, unless you get the paper before Monday.
  • Saying the lab works on coding agents or context files. It does not, as far as we found. That part is what you bring.
  • "Most of my code is written by agents." Never. Say: "I use Claude Code and Codex every day. I review and test their changes."
  • "No one has done this", "first", "novel". Say "as far as I know".
  • More than one BrandMemory comparison. It was one A/B comparison.
  • Burnt. Do not mention it.
  • Mentioning Prof. Dohi's retirement. Never.
  • Asking if you will pass. He cannot answer, and it puts him in a bad spot.
  • Agreeing to a date you cannot keep. Check against the 5 Nov and 6 Nov dates before you say yes.

Right after the call: write down every question he asked and every paper he named, within 30 minutes. Questions from a meeting like this can come back in the oral exam. Then send the thank-you email and the Consent Form (04_email_templates.md).
