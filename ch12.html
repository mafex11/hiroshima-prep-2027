Part 1: Before Monday's meeting (30 Oct to 2 Nov)

Chapter 12 of 28

Questions on why Japan, why Hiroshima and why this lab

When and how long: Sunday: fill the [FILL] lines first, then say each answer once.

This file covers the motivation questions Prof. Zheng may ask on 2 Nov, and the oral exam panel may ask between 24 Nov and 2 Dec: why a master's, why now, why Japan, why Hiroshima, why this lab and why Prof. Zheng, what you bring and want to learn, plans after the master's, NAIST, Japanese and life in Higashi-Hiroshima. The parts marked [FILL] and [CONFIRM] are yours to complete. Say them only once they are true, and if one is not true for you, drop it.

At the oral exam, change "you" and "your" to "Prof. Zheng" and "Prof. Zheng's", and skip Q2's push line and the questions you ask him in Q22 and Q30.

1. Why a master's, and why now

Q1. Why do you want a master's degree? Why not keep working?

At work I review and test the changes coding agents make every day, but tests only cover the cases someone thought of. I want real numbers for how often this code fails and how much voting helps, and that needs probability models I can't build yet. I want to learn that properly, full time, with people who build these models.

Why he asks: he wants to know you will not leave halfway for a job. If he pushes ("You could learn statistics online"): "I could learn the basics online. But fitting a model to real failure data and defending it is something I want to learn from people who do it every day."

Q2. Why now? You only finished your B.E. this year, and you have a good job.

These tools are new, so the questions are open now. Two studies this year found that code written by LLMs fails together more often than independence predicts, and neither used a context file like the ones I maintain at work. After about a year of building LLM systems at Layerpath, I keep meeting questions that engineering alone can't answer.

If he pushes ("Why not work two more years first?"): "This selection is for April 2027, and you have agreed to supervise me for it. I would rather start while the question is still open."

Q3. You are an LLM engineer. Why reliability modelling?

Because building agents is the part I can already do. What I can't do yet is say how reliable their output is, with a number and an interval. I have seen an instruction to a model have no effect at all while our logs said it was applied, so "the tests passed" doesn't feel like the full answer to me.

If he pushes ("Give an example"): "In BrandMemory, the brand voice section had the lowest priority, so a 3,200-character limit cut it first. After the fix, one A/B comparison showed contractions falling from 6 to 0. One comparison is not a study, but since then I check whether a model really follows an instruction."

Q4. Are you sure you want to leave your job?

Yes. I will leave Layerpath before I enrol and study full time, and I'll hand over my work properly before I go. [FILL: one true sentence on why you are ready to leave, for example "I have built the AI features I joined to build, and now I want to learn how to measure them" / "I want two years on one question, and a startup job doesn't give me that" / "I've talked it over with my family and we have planned for it".]

[CONFIRM: add "My CEO is writing one of my recommendation letters, so the company knows I'm applying" only once he has agreed. Do not say his name.]

2. Why Japan

Q5. Why do you want to study in Japan?

I was looking at research master's programmes in Japan, and when I found your lab, it fit my question best. Your lab builds probability models of how software fails, and my plan builds on your N-version paper. [FILL: your own reason for looking at Japan, only if it is true, for example "In Japan I apply to one supervisor with a research plan, and the master's ends in a thesis. That is the kind of master's I want." / a real personal link to Japan: a place, a person, or something you have followed for years.]

Why he asks: he wants to see whether you came for the research or only for the country. Note for you: your September search notes put Japan first, before you found any lab. So if he asks "Why were you looking at Japan at all?", the [FILL] needs a true answer. The NAIST interview transcript has no line on why Japan, so there is nothing to reuse from it.

Q6. Why not study in India?

Among the labs I looked at, yours is the closest to my reliability question. [FILL: one true sentence about India, for example "I want to study away from my job so research gets my full time" / "I want to see how research is done in another country" / "I didn't find a group in India working on N-version programming with LLMs, as far as I looked" (only if you really looked).]

Note for you: never say anything bad about Indian universities.

Q7. Why not the US or Europe?

I chose Japan first, and of the labs I found in Japan, yours fits my question best. [FILL: your true reason for Japan, the same one as in Q5, or another true reason, for example "I want a two-year research master's with a thesis" / "I didn't apply to the US or Europe this year".]

If he pushes ("Did you apply outside Japan?"): [FILL: the true answer. Your notes list Singapore, Germany and Australia as options you looked at, but no confirmed application.] Note for you: do not compare fees with other countries. You have no checked numbers for them.

Q8. Have you been to Japan before?

[FILL: the true answer. If no: "No, not yet. This would be my first time in Japan." If yes: when, where and why, in one sentence.]

3. Why Hiroshima University and this programme

Q9. Why Hiroshima University?

Because your lab is here, and you have agreed to supervise me. On the programme's list of supervisors, the Dependable Systems group has fields like software reliability engineering and software testing, which is where my question sits. This selection also lets me apply from India, with an online oral exam.

Q10. Why the Informatics and Data Science Program?

The programme wants students with "the will to work as a high-level engineer or researcher in areas of the informatics and data science". I'm an engineer who wants to learn research, and my question sits between software engineering and statistics: agents write the code, and probability models judge how reliable it is. This programme has both sides, so it fits my plan.

Q11. How did you find our lab?

Your group's 2026 paper on whether generative AI can make N-version programming more effective caught my attention, because my own question is about how reliable the code from AI coding agents is. [CONFIRM: your notes show you found the lab on the programme's list of supervisors and then its N-version paper. Say that if it is how you remember it.] So I wrote to Prof. Dohi on 20 October with a one-page plan and my CV.

Why he asks: a normal opening question. He wants to see that you chose the lab and did not mass-mail. If he pushes ("Did you write to other professors?"): "Yes, I wrote to a few other professors in Japan, and I'm applying to NAIST." [CONFIRM: true as written; name them only if he asks.]

Q12. Your first email had a different plan. Why did it change?

My first one-page plan asked which instructions in AGENTS.md and CLAUDE.md agents follow, and how they affect reliability. When I looked more closely at your lab's work, I saw that the stronger question for this lab is whether runs that share one file fail together, and what that means for voting. So I rebuilt the plan around coincident failures, your N-version question and the boundary value method.

Why he asks: Prof. Dohi got both versions. Prof. Zheng was copied only from 29 Oct, so he may not have seen the one-page plan, but he may have read your first email in the quoted thread.

4. Why this lab, and why Prof. Zheng

Q13. Why the Dependable Systems Laboratory?

Your lab's page says, "In every case we pair the technique with conventional probabilistic evaluation, because what matters is showing quantitatively how much the quality actually improved." My question needs that kind of answer. My plan takes the question of your N-version paper to real repository tasks, and the boundary value method and pysrat fit straight into it. I can bring the agent side, and the reliability modelling is what I want to learn here.

Q14. What do you know about the lab's history and work?

I know it only from the outside, from your website and paper abstracts. The lab works on six areas: software reliability models, support for software development such as test generation, stochastic models, optimal maintenance, dependability and performance, and security. Your page says the lab was among the first to ask when software rejuvenation should be done, and more recently it has turned to large language models, with the N-version work and work on how several agents should cooperate.

If he pushes ("Anything older?"): "In 2004 Prof. Dohi, with Osaki and Trivedi, used an infinite-server queue to unify finite and infinite fault NHPP models, and my optional field study uses the same idea. [CONFIRM: read the abstract of Dohi, Osaki, Trivedi, APSEC 2004 before saying this] I have only read that paper's abstract." If he pushes ("Who is in the lab, and what tools does it use?"): "There are four faculty: Prof. Dohi, Prof. Okamura, you and Dr. Wu, with 3 doctoral, 10 master's, 7 undergraduate and 2 research students. The tools include pysrat in Python and Rsrat in R. The older SRATS Excel add-ins are no longer maintained."

Q15. Our lab does not work on coding agents. Why come here?

That is the reason. As far as I know, the lab has no paper on coding agents or context files, so that part is what I bring. I already use agents every day, so the part I need to learn is the reliability side: fitting a model to failures and saying how sure the answer is.

Q16. Why do you want me as your supervisor?

Prof. Dohi told me my topic fits your work better, and when I looked, I agreed. You are the first author of the N-version paper, which my plan builds on. Your work on finding the best rejuvenation timing is also close to one part of my plan, where I look for the number of runs that gives the lowest total cost.

Note for you: never mention Prof. Dohi's retirement. Do not bring up Prof. Zheng's own path as an international student.

Q17. What do you know about my other work, outside the N-version paper?

In the 2021 Mathematics paper with Prof. Okamura and Prof. Dohi, you found the rejuvenation timing that maximises availability, and showed that operator mistakes during checkpointing lower availability and change the best timing. I also saw you are a co-author of the 2026 DynTaskMAS paper, where LLM agents work in parallel on a task graph that changes at runtime.

[CONFIRM: look at his researcher page (https://seeds.office.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/profile/en.c1b1491e563a3c40520e17560c007669.html) and read both abstracts before you say this. Both papers are open access and in 04_Papers/pdfs/.] If he pushes ("What about my Markov regenerative process work?"): "I know only the title of the Performance Evaluation paper on sensitivity, so I'd rather not guess. I'd be glad to read it."

Q18. Which of our papers and tools connect to your plan?

Five of them. RQ1 takes the question of your N-version paper to real repository tasks, and RQ2 uses the boundary coverage distance method of Guo, Prof. Okamura and Prof. Dohi as a stress test. The optional field study would build fix data from GitHub, as Qiu and colleagues did in the Hawkes paper. Its model is the infinite-server queue that Prof. Dohi, Osaki and Trivedi used in 2004, with Prof. Okamura's pysrat for the delay distribution.

If he pushes on the boundary method: "I use it only as a stress test, because 30 to 50 inputs per program can't estimate a failure probability."

5. What you bring and what you want to learn

Q19. What will you bring to the lab?

The agent side. I use Claude Code and Codex every day, I review and test their changes, and I maintain the CLAUDE.md and AGENTS.md files they read. I can write a harness in plain Python, as I did for my desktop agent Yuki 2.0, and I have used the GitHub API in my own tools. I'll release all tasks, code and data, so others can reuse them.

If he pushes ("Could you help with the lab's LLM work?"): "I'd be glad to, if it's useful. Running agents and checking what they actually did is what I do every day."

Q20. What do you want to learn here?

Mainly how to fit and test reliability models on real failure data: maximum likelihood, the bootstrap, NHPP models, and how to check whether a model fits. I also want to learn to write a research paper properly, because I haven't published research of my own yet. And I'd like to learn the lab's tools, such as pysrat, and Julia or R, which your page says students use.

Q21. What will be hardest for you in this lab?

Statistics. My only statistics course so far is BCS301 in my third semester, which covered probability distributions, Markov chains, hypothesis tests and ANOVA. That's why probability, statistics and reliability courses come first in my plan. Japanese will also take time, because I'm a beginner.

Q22. How will you prepare before April?

[CONFIRM: say only what you are really doing.] I'm revising probability and statistics for the oral exam, and I'll keep going after it. I also study Japanese every day. I'd like to ask what you would recommend I read before April.

[CONFIRM: add "I installed pysrat and ran its example" only if you did.]

7. Hiroshima and NAIST

Q26. Are you applying anywhere else?

Yes. I'm also applying to NAIST for October 2027, to Prof. Iida's lab. Their official application is in February and the exam is on 10 March. If I'm admitted here, I'll tell you my decision quickly and honestly, so you don't hold a place for me that I won't take.

[FILL: name any other place where you have actually applied.]

Q27. Your NAIST plan is also about context files. Why apply to both?

Both come from the same question: can we trust the code agents write when every run reads the same context file? At NAIST the plan asks which rules agents follow. Here it asks whether the runs fail together and how much voting helps, and that needs the reliability models your lab builds.

Note for you: state the facts and stop. Do not compare the two labs or say which is better.

Q28. If both universities admit you, which will you choose?

Honestly, I haven't decided that yet. NAIST's exam and results are in March. I don't want to promise something I haven't decided, so I'll tell you my decision as soon as I make it.

[CONFIRM: say this only if it is still true on the day. If you have decided, say so plainly.] Note for you: the 282,000 yen enrolment fee is never refunded, and may be due in early March [CONFIRM in the admission guide after 14 Dec; only a sister guideline says early March], before NAIST's results on 15 March. Never call Hiroshima your first choice unless it is.

Q29. Prof. Dohi said some admitted students cancel when they don't get MEXT. Would that happen with you?

Not because of a scholarship. I plan to study as a self-funded student, so a MEXT result won't change anything. I've planned for the enrolment fee of 282,000 yen and tuition of 535,800 yen a year. The only open point is NAIST, and I'll tell you my decision as soon as I make it. [CONFIRM: "The money comes from my savings from work and my family's support."]

If he pushes ("So you will definitely come?"): stay consistent with Q28. "A scholarship won't decide it. The only open point is NAIST, and I'll tell you quickly." If he pushes ("Will you apply for a tuition exemption?"): "If there is a tuition exemption I can apply for, I would, but I'm not relying on it." Never suggest the lab should pay anything.

8. Japanese, Higashi-Hiroshima and lab life

Q30. How is your Japanese, and what is your plan?

I'm a beginner, below N5. [CONFIRM: "I study with the Genki I textbook and an app, about 30 minutes a day."] I want to reach N5 before I arrive, and then take the university's Japanese classes for international students. Could I work in English in the lab at first?

If he pushes ("Why so little Japanese?"): [CONFIRM: "I started late. I know it matters for daily life and for talking with other students, so I'm taking it seriously."] Note for you: the university's Japanese language education page (Morito Institute of Global Higher Education) says its Japanese units are allowed "to all international students (including visiting researchers)".

Q31. Do you know where the campus is? How will you live in Higashi-Hiroshima?

The lab is on the Higashi-Hiroshima campus, in Faculty of Engineering Building A1, on the 7th floor. The nearest train station is Saijo, about 40 minutes by train from Hiroshima, and then about 15 minutes by bus. I haven't priced rent and daily costs in detail yet, so I'll ask the international office and the lab's students. [FILL: anything true you know or like about the area.]

If he pushes ("Is there anything you need from us?"): "Is there housing for new international students, like a university dormitory, and when should I apply?" (question 10 in 02_Meeting_Strategy/02_questions_to_ask_him.md)

Q32. Life in a Japanese lab can be different from a startup. Are you ready for it?

Yes. I'm happy to follow how the lab works: regular seminars, reporting my progress, and asking before I change direction. I don't know yet how your lab runs its seminars, so I'd like to ask. At Layerpath I work in a small team and own the features I build, so I'm used to being responsible for my part.

Q33. Some students in the lab may not be comfortable in English. How will you work with them?

I'll learn enough Japanese for daily lab life, and I'll keep my English simple and clear. I'd also like to learn from the senior students how the lab does things. [FILL: one true example of working with people who speak a different first language, if you have one.]
