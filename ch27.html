Practice meetings: three full rounds

When and how long: Practice meeting 1 on Sun 15 Nov, 2 on Sat 21 Nov, 3 on Sun 22 Nov. 30 minutes each.

Three complete 30-minute practice meetings to get ready for the meeting with the professors at Hiroshima University (Informatics and Data Science Program, master's, April 2027 entry). Each practice meeting is set up differently and has different questions: run one per session, not all three at once.

For the friend: read this first

What you are doing. You play the professors for Sudhanshu. The real meeting is online, on Teams or Zoom, on one day between 24 November and 2 December 2026. It has two parts:

  1. About 15 minutes of presentation by Sudhanshu: about 10 minutes on work so far, then about 5 minutes on the research plan.
  2. About 15 minutes of questions from the professors.

The supervisor, Assoc. Prof. Junjun Zheng, said most questions will be about the research plan, that it depends on which professors join, and that they may ask "Why did you choose Japan?" and "Why Hiroshima University?". The practice meetings follow that: about 60% research plan, 20% Sudhanshu's work, 10% motivation, and 10% quick basics asked on the spot.

The people you play. Each question says who asks it. Change your tone a little for each one.

  • Prof. Zheng: Assoc. Prof. Junjun Zheng, the supervisor. Works on software reliability, probability models, and whether voting over several AI-written program versions makes software more reliable. Friendly, but precise.
  • Prof. Okamura: Prof. Hiroyuki Okamura, same lab. Testing methods and reliability tools. Asks about method and maths details.
  • Prof. Dohi: Prof. Tadashi Dohi, the senior professor of the lab. Classic software reliability models. Short questions that go deep.
  • Another professor: a professor from another lab in the programme. Not an expert in this topic. Asks "so what?", "why should I believe it?", and the motivation questions.

You don't need to understand the answers. Read each question as written. Then listen.

  • Don't read the "Listen for" line out loud. It is for you: the points a good answer contains.
  • Push when an answer is vague, with one of these: "Why?", "Can you give an example?", "How would you check that?", "What if that's not true?"
  • Keep it moving. If one answer goes past about a minute, say "Thank you. Let's move on."
  • Write down every question where Sudhanshu hesitated, guessed, or talked too long. Go through them together at the end, with the checklist.

How to run one practice meeting (30 minutes).

  1. Use a video call. Sudhanshu shares the screen for the presentation, then stops sharing for the questions.
  2. Read the opening lines (the lines in grey quote blocks are what you say out loud).
  3. Say "please start your presentation", and start a 15-minute timer.
  4. At 14 minutes, say "One minute left, please." At 15 minutes, if the talk is still going, ask Sudhanshu to finish. At 16 minutes, stop the talk politely. Write down the finish time. Otherwise, don't interrupt the presentation.
  5. Ask the questions in order, for about 15 minutes. If time runs short, skip a research-plan question, but always ask the quick basics question and the last question, "Do you have any questions for us?"
  6. Read the closing lines. Then fill in the checklist at the end of this file.

If Sudhanshu practises alone. The study site has a play button on each numbered question and on each quote block. Press play, answer out loud, time yourself, then read the "Listen for" line and compare it with your answer honestly. Practice meeting 1: a friendly meeting (questions 1 to 15)

Prof. Zheng leads the meeting. The questions are the most likely ones. Run this practice meeting first.

Opening (about 1 minute)

Hello, Sudhanshu. Can you hear us well?

Thank you for joining. I'm Junjun Zheng. With me today are Prof. Okamura, Prof. Dohi and a colleague from our programme.

The meeting has two parts. First, about fifteen minutes for your presentation: your work so far, and then your research plan. Then about fifteen minutes of questions from us.

When you're ready, please share your screen and start your presentation.

Presentation (15 minutes)

Start the timer now. Say nothing until 14 minutes.

One minute left, please.

If the talk is still going at 15 minutes:

Thank you. Could you finish your current slide, please?

When the talk ends:

Thank you, Sudhanshu. Please stop sharing your screen. We'll start the questions now.

Questions (about 15 minutes)

1. (Prof. Zheng) Thank you. In two or three sentences, what is the main question of your research plan? Listen for: repeated agent runs on one real task treated as N versions; the shared AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md as part of the specification every run shares; RQ1, how much more often runs fail together than independence predicts and which change between runs lowers it; RQ2, whether one real rule changes reliability beyond the tests; a model that tells a team how many runs to vote over. Short, not a second presentation. (Q1 in 03_Practice_Meetings/05_question_bank_with_model_answers.md)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Zheng) In classic N-version programming the versions come from independent teams. Is one more run of the same agent really a new version? You: "In Eckhardt and Lee's model, yes: even independent teams are random draws from one development process, and because some inputs are hard for every team, their versions still fail together. One more run in a clean container is one more draw from the agent's process. Changing the agent or the model between runs is closer to changing the process, and RQ1 compares that too."
  • (Prof. Zheng) How would you know the runs aren't just giving you the same patch again and again? You: "I'd check by behaviour, not by text: I run every admitted patch on the same generated inputs and count how many different behaviours there are. Runs do vary: Zhang et al. ran Claude Code three times on each of 500 SWE-bench Verified tasks, and 58 tasks were solved only once or twice. How much the passing patches differ on my tasks is what the pilot has to show."
  • (Prof. Zheng) Suppose your 10 runs collapse into two behaviours, seven runs one way and three the other. What does a vote over all ten give you? You: "The majority then always takes the answer of the group of seven, so the vote is only as good as that one behaviour run once. On inputs where it is wrong, seven runs fail together, which is exactly the clustering my ρ measures, so the model would say extra runs of this setting don't pay. The fix is to change something between runs, like the agent, so that their mistakes land on different inputs."

2. (Prof. Zheng) You model the failure chance θ with a Beta distribution. Why a Beta, and what will you do if it fits badly? Listen for: θ lies between 0 and 1 and changes from input to input; a simple two-parameter family; the count of failing runs on one input is then beta-binomial; ρ = 1/(a + b + 1), zero means independence; if most inputs never fail, try a zero-inflated model or a mixture, compared by AIC and by predicted vote failures. (Q16)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Zheng) You say ρ = 1/(a + b + 1). Where does that come from? You: "For two runs on a random input, the correlation between their pass-or-fail results is Var θ divided by p(1 - p). For a Beta(a, b), p is a over a + b, and Var θ works out to p(1 - p) divided by a + b + 1. Dividing the two gives 1/(a + b + 1)."
  • (Prof. Zheng) So what does a + b mean, and what happens at the two extremes? You: "a + b says how tightly θ sits around p, a bit like a prior sample size. As a + b grows, Var θ goes to zero, every input is equally hard, ρ goes to zero, and the beta-binomial becomes a plain binomial, the independent case. As a + b goes to zero, θ piles up at 0 and 1, so on each input either every run fails or none does, and ρ goes to 1."
  • (Prof. Zheng) Take a case in between, p = 0.01 and ρ = 0.2. What does a 2-out-of-3 vote fail with? You: "ρ = 0.2 means a + b = 4, so a = 0.04 and b = 3.96. Then the mean of θ squared is p² plus ρp(1 - p), which is 0.00208, and the mean of θ cubed is about 0.00071. So the vote fails with 3 times 0.00208 minus 2 times 0.00071, about 0.0048. That only halves the single-run 0.01, while independent runs would give about 0.0003, sixteen times less."

3. (Prof. Okamura) How will you use our boundary coverage distance method, and why only as a stress test? Listen for: 30 to 50 chosen inputs are not a random sample, so they cannot estimate a failure probability; boundary points taken from branch conditions that compare a parameter with a constant; says these are path-based boundaries, while the paper split the inputs by output (its Remark 2 notes that path-based and output-based partitions can differ); kill rate by execution path, as in the paper, also by output. (Q32, Q33)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Okamura) Couldn't you still weight the boundary inputs to get a failure probability, as in importance sampling? You: "Only if I knew, for each chosen input, both its probability under my input distribution and the probability that the method picks it. Your BCD algorithms move random starting inputs toward the boundaries by a random search, so the chance of picking each input isn't known. With only 30 to 50 inputs the weights would also be very unstable, so I keep the two apart: random inputs for the estimate, boundary inputs to find bugs."
  • (Prof. Okamura) Suppose a boundary input finds a bug that your 10,000 random inputs missed. What does that say about the failure probability? You: "That the failing region is small under my input distribution. If none of 10,000 random inputs hit it, the rule of three gives a 95% upper bound of about 3 in 10,000 for the chance of hitting it. So the bug is real but adds little to p as I define it, and I'd report it separately as a boundary bug."
  • (Prof. Okamura) But real users may hit that boundary far more often than your generator does. Isn't the input distribution the weak point? You: "Yes. p only means something for a given input distribution, the operational profile, meaning how often real use sends each kind of input. Mine is only a guess from type hints and the values in the task's tests, so I'd report p under it and also show how p changes when I put more weight near the boundaries."

4. (Another professor) You say two runs both fail with probability p squared plus the variance of θ. Can you explain that with a small example? Listen for: on one input both runs fail with θ squared; averaged over inputs that is E[θ²] = p² + Var θ, the definition of variance; an example such as half the inputs with θ = 0 and half with θ = 0.4, giving p = 0.2, p² = 0.04, but a real joint failure of 0.08; so independence needs every input equally hard. (Q6)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Another professor) In your example some inputs have θ = 0.4. What would such an input look like in real code? You: "An edge case. Imagine a discount function: on normal prices every run is right, so θ is 0. But on a negative price, a refund, about four in ten runs forget to handle the sign, so θ is 0.4 there, and hard inputs like that are what make runs fail together."
  • (Another professor) With 10 runs, how precisely can you know θ on that one input? You: "Not precisely at all: if I see 4 failing runs out of 10, an exact 95% interval for θ goes from about 0.12 to 0.74. That's why I don't estimate θ input by input. I fit one distribution of θ across thousands of inputs, which is far more stable."
  • (Another professor) How would you know that one distribution really fits all those inputs? You: "I'd compare what the model predicts with what I see: the share of inputs with 0, 1, 2 and up to 10 failing runs. If the model says 2% of inputs should have 5 failing runs and I see almost none, it doesn't fit. Then I'd try a mixture, for example a group of easy inputs plus a separate group of hard ones."

5. (Prof. Dohi) Eckhardt and Lee already asked whether there is an optimal number of versions. What is new in your "best N"? Listen for: agrees the question is old (their report has a figure on the existence of an optimal N); what is new is estimating the distribution of θ from real agent runs and adding the cost of each run; theirs minimises failure probability, this plan minimises run cost plus expected failure cost. (H5)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Dohi) Write down your cost function. And why only odd N? You: "The total cost is the cost of one run times N, plus the cost of a failure times the probability that a majority of the N runs fails. I use odd N so there is always a majority. With even N, a five-to-five split needs a tie rule, one more choice to justify. Given θ, the runs fail independently, so that probability is a binomial tail, and I average it over the fitted Beta."
  • (Prof. Dohi) Take independent runs with p = 0.1. Compute it for N = 1, 3 and 5. You: "For N = 1 it is 0.1. For N = 3 it is 3p² - 2p³, which is 0.03 minus 0.002, so 0.028. For N = 5 it is the chance that 3 or more of 5 fail, 10p³(1 - p)² + 5p⁴(1 - p) + p⁵, which is 0.0081 plus 0.00045 plus 0.00001, about 0.0086."
  • (Prof. Dohi) So when do the two extra runs, from 3 to 5, pay for themselves? You: "They cost two runs and cut the failure probability by 0.028 minus 0.0086, about 0.019. So they pay when a failure costs more than 2 divided by 0.019, about 100 runs. If the runs are correlated with ρ = 0.2, the cut drops to about 0.013, and the break-even rises to about 150 runs. That's exactly where the fitted ρ changes the answer."

6. (Another professor) What is your real sample size for estimating ρ: the 10,000 inputs, or the 10 runs? Listen for: much closer to the runs and to the number of different bugs; one buggy run fails on a whole region of inputs, so inputs are not independent information; that is why the bootstrap resamples runs; more inputs alone won't narrow the interval for ρ. (H14)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Another professor) Why does one bug make a whole region of inputs fail? Give me a picture. You: "Say one run's patch forgets that a price can be negative. Then every generated input with a negative price fails for that run, maybe a thousand of them, but it's still one mistake. Counting them as a thousand separate pieces of evidence would be like counting one person's opinion a thousand times."
  • (Another professor) So what is the real unit of evidence, the run or the mistake? You: "For how often runs fail together, it's the runs and the mistakes they share: what I learn is whether other runs make the same mistake on the same inputs. Ten runs where only two share a mistake give me little evidence, however many inputs I generate. The inputs still matter for something else: they measure how big each failure region is, which is the failure probability."
  • (Another professor) You say runs make 'the same mistake'. If two runs fail on one input but give different wrong answers, is that the same? You: "Not for a vote: if two of three runs give the same wrong output, the vote picks it without any warning. If their wrong outputs differ, there's no majority, so the system can at least flag that input. My formula counts both as a vote failure, the cautious case, and I'd also report how often the wrong outputs match."

7. (Prof. Zheng) What if the merged human code has the same bug as the agents? Listen for: names this as the main limit without being pushed; differential testing cannot see a bug the reference shares; it is exactly the shared-helper case the study is about, so those failures may be undercounted; the hand check catches the opposite case, where the merged code is wrong. (Q38)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Zheng) You check only 20 disagreements per task by hand. What can 20 tell you? You: "They bound how often the merged code is wrong. If none of the 20 shows it wrong, the rule of three gives a 95% upper bound of 3 in 20, so 15%. So 20 checks catch merged code that is often wrong, not merged code that is rarely wrong. If I do find cases, I drop those inputs and check more for that task."
  • (Prof. Zheng) When the merged code and a run disagree, who decides which one is right? You: "I do, by checking both against the specification, which here means the task text, the tests, the docstrings and the code around the change. If those don't settle it, the input is ambiguous, and I mark it as ambiguous instead of calling either side wrong."
  • (Prof. Zheng) But ambiguity is often where versions fail together. Aren't you throwing away the interesting cases? You: "Partly, yes. Ron et al. traced most of the errors in their study back to weaknesses in the specification, so ambiguous inputs may be exactly where runs fail together. So I'd keep them as a separate count and report how the runs split on them, because a task with many ambiguous inputs is itself a finding about its task text or context file."

8. (Another professor) Walk me through the 820 runs and what they cost. Listen for: 400 main runs (10 tasks x 2 file versions x 2 agents x 10 runs), 100 for the second Codex model, 200 for his own harness, 120 for screening; about 420 to 590 US dollars at the per-task costs in Gloaguen et al.; newer models may cost more; would ask whether it fits a lab budget (the line in the proposal he sent the lab on 28 Oct), otherwise move runs to the open-weight model. Must not suggest the lab has to pay. (Q28)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Another professor) Where does the range of 420 to 590 dollars come from, and which part is the biggest? You: "I took the lowest and highest per-task cost of each model in Table 2 of Gloaguen et al. and multiplied by its number of runs. Claude Code is most of it: 260 runs at 1.15 to 1.51 dollars each, about 300 to 390 dollars. Codex with GPT-5.2 adds about 80 to 150, and the smaller Codex model and the open-weight model about 40 to 50 together."
  • (Another professor) If Claude Code is most of the cost, why not drop it and keep only Codex and the open model? You: "Because the main runs compare two agents on both file versions, and my own harness runs only the original file. With one agent, I couldn't tell whether what I see is about the context file or just about that agent, and Claude Code is the one that reads the file as CLAUDE.md. If the cost had to come down, I'd first move runs to the open-weight model or cut tasks, before I cut the second agent."
  • (Another professor) How few tasks could you go down to before the results stop meaning anything? You: "RQ1 still works with few tasks, because each task gives its own estimate of ρ, but the claims cover fewer tasks. RQ2 suffers more, because it estimates one effect of the rule across tasks, and with very few tasks I can't separate that effect from the differences between tasks. I'd find the number in the pilot, by simulating how many tasks give a useful interval, rather than guess it now."

9. (Another professor) You work at Layerpath. Tell us about one time an AI feature failed without anyone noticing, and how you found it. Listen for: BrandMemory; logs said the guidance was applied but the output had no trace of the brand voice; traced what the model really received; the brand voice section had the lowest priority and was cut first by a 3,200-character limit; what was fixed; one A/B pair (contractions 6 to 0), clearly called "not a study". (W17 to W19)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Another professor) You call it one A/B pair, not a study. What would it take to make it a study? You: "I'd use several brands and several requests, and run each with BrandMemory on and off a few times, because one model run varies by itself. I'd fix the measures in advance, like the counts of contractions and of 'you', count them with a script, and report the difference with an interval."
  • (Another professor) Why does it matter that the measures are fixed in advance? You: "Because after seeing two outputs I could pick whichever feature happened to differ, and a demo text has dozens of features I could count, so some will differ just by chance. Contractions and 'you' were the features that two of the brand's rules name: 'avoid contractions' and 'write in the third person'."
  • (Another professor) Put a number on that. If you check 20 features at the 5% level and nothing really changed, how likely is at least one false alarm? You: "If the 20 checks are independent, it's 1 minus 0.95 to the power 20, about 0.64, so nearly two times in three. That's why I'd fix the measures in advance, or hold each one to a stricter level, for example 0.05 divided by 20, which is 0.0025, with the Bonferroni correction."

10. (Prof. Okamura) Your CV says Path AI handles 1,500+ calls a month at 85%+ accuracy. How was that measured? Listen for: either a real, specific method (what counts as correct, how many calls, who judged), or a plain "I'm not sure how that number was measured, so I won't defend it"; ideally adds what a proper measure would need: a definition of failure, a random sample, an interval. Red flag: defending the number without a method. (W7)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Okamura) Say you label a random sample of calls. How many do you need to know the accuracy within plus or minus 5 points? You: "If the accuracy is near 85%, the standard error is the square root of 0.85 times 0.15 over n, and I want 1.96 times that to equal 0.05. Solving gives n of about 196, so about 200 calls. With no idea of the accuracy I'd use 0.5 instead, which gives about 385."
  • (Prof. Okamura) But calls aren't independent: the same lead list, the same model version, the same slow day. Does your formula still hold? You: "Not exactly. If calls come in groups that go well or badly together, the variance grows by the design effect, 1 + (m - 1) times the intraclass correlation, where m is the group size. So I'd sample across days and lead lists and widen the interval, which is the same clustering problem as runs and inputs in my plan."
  • (Prof. Okamura) Compute it: groups of 10 calls with an intraclass correlation of 0.1. How many calls now? You: "The design effect is 1 + 9 times 0.1, which is 1.9, so I'd need about 1.9 times 196, about 370 calls, for the same plus or minus 5 points. Even a small correlation nearly doubles the work."

11. (Prof. Zheng) Your desktop agent's safety checker caught 9 out of 9 risky actions. How sure are you of that number? Listen for: calls it a small sample; with 9 out of 9, a 95% interval still goes down to about 66%; 0 false holds out of 36 still allows a false-hold rate up to about 10%; offline replays, often one run per task; "a good sign, not a proof". (W42)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Zheng) Where does the 66% come from? You: "It's the exact Clopper-Pearson lower bound. If the true catch rate were r, the chance of catching all 9 would be r to the power 9, and the lower bound is the r where that chance is just 2.5%. So r is 0.025 to the power one ninth, about 0.66."
  • (Prof. Zheng) Your checker is a language model, and its answer can change from run to run. What does 'the catch rate' even mean then? You: "Each risky case has its own chance of being caught, like θ for an input in my plan, and the catch rate is the average of that over cases. Since I often ran each case only once, I can't tell a case that is always caught from one caught, say, 8 times in 10, so I'd run each case several times."
  • (Prof. Zheng) Then why not run the checker three times and block the action if any run says no? You: "On a case caught 8 times in 10, three runs would all miss only 0.2 cubed, 0.008 of the time. But on a case the checker almost never catches, three runs miss nearly as often as one. So the gain depends on how the catch chance is spread across cases, the same Var θ question as in my plan. Blocking on any single no also raises the false holds, so I'd measure both before using it."

12. (Another professor) Why do you want to study in Japan? Listen for: leads with the research: this lab builds probability models of how software fails, and the plan builds on Prof. Zheng's N-version paper; any personal reason is short and true; does not lead with "a booming industry" or anything generic; under about a minute. (09 file, Q5)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Another professor) Software reliability is studied in other countries too. Why not a lab there? You: "I looked at a few countries, and Japan came first for me. Of the labs I found in Japan, this one fits my question best, because its N-version paper already asks a close question for language models. On the personal side, Japan's tech industry is growing, with many openings for AI engineers, and my brother lives in Singapore, which is not far. And a master's in Japan means two years in one supervisor's lab, ending in a thesis, which is the kind of master's I want."
  • (Another professor) What does that N-version paper find that matters for you? You: "Its abstract says majority voting made strong models like GPT-4o more reliable but helped little with weaker ones, and that more diversity, from temperature or reused conversation history, did not always mean higher reliability. My plan takes that question to real coding agents whose runs all read one context file. I've only read the abstract, because the full text is behind a paywall."
  • (Another professor) So you build on a paper you've only read the abstract of. Isn't that a problem? You: "It's a gap, and I'd like to close it by asking Prof. Zheng for a copy. My plan takes its question, not its methods: the core maths comes from Eckhardt and Lee, and the baseline measures I compare against come from the two 2026 studies. On 2 November Prof. Zheng also told me more, for example that they used GPT-4o and GPT-4o mini and varied the temperature."

13. (Another professor) And why Hiroshima University in particular? Listen for: the Dependable Systems Laboratory is here and Prof. Zheng agreed to supervise; the lab's fields (software reliability, testing) are where the question sits; the programme joins informatics and data science; this admission route allows applying from India, with the meeting held online. (09 file, Q9, Q10)

32. (Prof. Dohi) Define reliability in your study precisely. Is it over time, or over inputs? Listen for: over inputs, per demand; one version's reliability is one minus its failure probability under the declared input distribution; the vote's reliability likewise; only the field study is over calendar time, with an NHPP. (H39)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Dohi) Per demand. Is that an established definition of software reliability, or your own? You: "It's established. Nelson's input-domain model from the 1970s draws inputs at random from the operational profile, meaning how often each kind of input occurs in use, and estimates reliability as the share of runs that succeed. My failure probability is the same idea, with my declared input distribution standing in for the profile."
  • (Prof. Dohi) Then estimate it. Ten thousand random inputs and 30 failures. You: "The failure probability is 30 over 10,000, which is 0.003, so the reliability is 0.997. An exact 95% interval for the failure probability goes from about 0.0020 to 0.0043, so the reliability lies between about 0.9957 and 0.998."
  • (Prof. Dohi) That is one version. The next run of the agent writes a different version. What can you say about its reliability? You: "Much less. Its failure probability varies from run to run around the mean p, and that spread matters far more than the 10,000 inputs. With ten runs, the next run's value falls between the lowest and the highest of the ten 9 times in 11, about 82%, whatever the distribution. That spread is also why my intervals come from resampling runs, not inputs."

33. (Prof. Okamura) In your field-data part, why doesn't a finite-fault NHPP fit, and what do you use instead? Listen for: agent code keeps arriving, so the number of faults is not fixed; each merged change brings ω_j = L_j exp(β′z_j) expected faults, found after a delay with distribution G; m(t) is the sum of ω_j G(t - τ_j); with one change and an exponential G it reduces to Goel-Okumoto; it is an infinite-server queue, as in Dohi, Osaki and Trivedi (2004), honestly known only from the abstract. (Q34, Q35)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Okamura) With one change and an exponential G you get Goel-Okumoto. Which G gives the delayed S-shaped model? You: "A gamma with shape 2, the time for two exponential stages in a row with the same rate b, so G(t) = 1 - (1 + bt)e^(-bt). With one change, m(t) is ω times that, which is the delayed S-shaped model of Yamada, Ohba and Osaki, where the two stages are read as detecting a failure and then isolating its fault."
  • (Prof. Okamura) For agent code, which delay would you expect, and why? You: "I'd guess S-shaped. Right after a merge the new code is used little, and a fault has to be hit and then traced back before a fix commit appears, so fixes in the first days should be rare, while the exponential says detection is fastest right at the merge. But that's only a guess, and the data should decide."
  • (Prof. Okamura) How would the data decide? You: "Through the detection rate over time, the hazard of G. For the exponential it's constant at b, and for the gamma with shape 2 it's b squared t over (1 + bt), which starts at zero and rises toward b. pysrat already has both, as ExponentialNHPP and GammaNHPP, but as far as I know it fits one stream of failure data, so I'd extend the fit to many changes, then compare the two by AIC and by how well each predicts the later months."

34. (Another professor) Strictly, you only measure disagreement with one person's merged code. Why do you call it a failure probability? Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Another professor) Give me an example where a run disagrees with the merged code but isn't wrong. You: "Say a function returns a user's open orders, and the task never says in what order. The merged code sorts them by date and a run returns them in the order the database gives, so my comparison sees two different lists although both are correct. My rule ignores order only for unordered collections like sets, so a list like this is exactly why I check disagreements by hand before calling them failures."
  • (Another professor) Then for a list, how would you decide whether order matters? You: "From the type alone I can't. For sets and dictionaries I ignore order automatically, but for a list I keep the order unless the docstring, the task text or the tests show it doesn't matter, and I decide that for each entry point before the runs, not after I see which runs disagree."
  • (Another professor) Suppose you decide that before the runs, and the hand check later finds 5 of 20 disagreements are harmless order differences. What then? 35. (Prof. Zheng) Suppose your interval for ρ includes zero. What do you conclude? Listen for: cannot rule out independence, which is not the same as showing it; reports the upper end of the interval and what it means for the vote; if even the upper end changes the vote little, the practical answer is "voting works here". (H23)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Zheng) That's for one task. With ten tasks, what if most of their intervals include zero? You: "Then I wouldn't stop at ten separate results that can't rule out independence. I'd fit a hierarchical model, where each task has its own ρ drawn from one common distribution, so the tasks share information, like pooling small studies in a meta-analysis."
  • (Prof. Zheng) Show me how ten weak results can add up to a strong one. You: "Say each task gives ρ around 0.08 with a standard error of 0.1, so each alone is less than one standard error from zero. If the tasks share one ρ, pooling divides the standard error by the square root of ten, to about 0.032. Then 0.08 is about 2.5 standard errors above zero, enough at the 5% level."
  • (Prof. Zheng) And if the tasks don't share one ρ? You: "Then the pooled value is the average ρ over tasks, and its standard error must include the spread between tasks. If that spread is also 0.1, the standard error becomes the square root of 0.01 plus 0.01, over ten, about 0.045, and 0.08 is only about 1.8 standard errors, short of the usual 1.96. Since ρ can't go below zero, these normal approximations are only a first look, and I'd check them with the bootstrap."

37. (Prof. Zheng) My work includes sensitivity analysis. Is there a sensitivity question in your plan? Listen for: yes, the second part of RQ1, how much the vote failure moves when one factor between runs changes; a concrete example: two runs both fail with p² + ρ p(1 - p), so the change per unit of ρ is p(1 - p); honest that it gets harder for the vote and the best N. (H43)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Zheng) You gave the sensitivity to ρ. What about p, and which one matters more? You: "The derivative in p is 2p + ρ(1 - 2p). But p and ρ live on different scales, so I'd compare elasticities, the percent change in the output for a one percent change in the input. At p = 0.05 and ρ = 0.3, a 10% rise in p raises the chance that both runs fail by about 11%, and a 10% rise in ρ by about 8.5%."
  • (Prof. Zheng) Is that only true at those values? You: "For this formula it holds everywhere. The elasticity for p minus the one for ρ works out to p squared times (2 - ρ), divided by the chance that both fail, and that's always positive. So per percent, the mean failure probability always matters more than the dependence for two runs failing together."
  • (Prof. Zheng) So should a team work on p and forget about ρ? You: "No, because sensitivity says where the same relative change has the most effect, not what each change costs. Cutting p by a tenth may need a much better model, while cutting the dependence may only need a second agent. So I'd put each option into my cost model, the cost of the runs plus the cost of a failed vote, and pick the cheaper one."

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Another professor) Do teams even vote today? Most just take one run. You: "As far as I know, most take one run and review it.   • (Another professor) If a developer picks by reading the runs, not by voting, does your model still say anything? You: "It gives a floor. On an input where every run fails, any pick fails, and for three runs that chance is the mean of θ cubed: with p = 0.05 and ρ = 0.3 it's about 0.008, a sixth of one run's 0.05, against about 0.0001 if the runs were independent. So no way of picking among three correlated runs can get below that."

39. (Prof. Okamura) Your Yuki agent keeps a confidence score of (S + 1) divided by (S + F + 2), times a time decay. What is that formula, statistically? Listen for: Laplace's rule of succession; the posterior mean of a success probability with a uniform Beta(1, 1) prior after S successes and F failures; the decay (0.5 to the power of days over a half-life) makes old evidence count less; links it to the Beta and binomial family in the research plan. (W44)

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Okamura) Why a uniform prior? Most procedures Yuki learns probably work. You: "For the mean, Beta(1, 1) acts like one success and one failure seen before any run, so a new procedure starts at one half and has to earn more confidence through real runs. If most procedures do work, a prior centred higher would fit better, but it would also trust a new, untried procedure more."
  • (Prof. Okamura) How would you choose that prior from data instead of by hand? You: "With empirical Bayes. If each procedure has its own success chance drawn from one Beta, the success counts across all procedures are beta-binomial, so I'd fit a and b by maximum likelihood, the same fit as in my research plan. Yuki's S and F are weighted counts, so I'd fit on the plain counts of runs, and take the fitted Beta as the prior for a new procedure."
  • (Prof. Okamura) Compute it. The fitted prior is Beta(4, 1), and a procedure has 2 successes and 1 failure. You: "The posterior is Beta(4 + 2, 1 + 1), which is Beta(6, 2), with mean 6 over 8, 0.75. Laplace's rule would give 3 over 5, 0.6. The fitted prior counts like five earlier runs with four successes, so with only three runs of its own, the procedure is pulled strongly toward the average of 0.8."

41. (Prof. Zheng) Your plan says you can build agents but can't yet fit reliability models. What exactly can't you do yet? And what is your statistics background? Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Zheng) You say you can do the basics on paper. What is a maximum likelihood estimate? Derive the simplest one. You: "It's the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable. With f failures in n independent runs, the log-likelihood is f log p plus (n - f) log(1 - p). Its derivative, f over p minus (n - f) over (1 - p), is zero at p = f over n, so the estimate is just the observed share."
  • (Prof. Zheng) That gives you a number. How would you put an interval around it? You: "The simple way is the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors, where the standard error is the square root of p(1 - p) over n. But checking that an interval really covers the truth 95% of the time in my setting is the part I haven't practised, and with about ten runs and a ρ that can't go below zero, the usual formulas can fail."
  • (Prof. Zheng) Give me an example where that simple interval fails. You: "That simple one, the Wald interval, fails with small counts. With 0 failures in 20 runs it gives an interval from 0 to 0, and if the true p is 0.05 that happens about 36% of the time, so its real coverage there is only about 64%. For small counts I'd use an exact or a Wilson interval, and for my own model I'd check coverage by simulating data from known values."

44. (Prof. Dohi) Quick question. Three identical units, each with reliability 0.9, and a perfect 2-out-of-3 voter. What is the system reliability? Listen for: 3R² - 2R³ = 3(0.81) - 2(0.729) = 2.43 - 1.458 = 0.972; assumes the units fail independently; nice to add: the vote helps only while R is above 0.5.

Follow-ups (ask them in order, each after the answer):

  • (Prof. Dohi) You said the vote helps only while R is above 0.5. Show me why. You: "The vote is better when 3R² - 2R³ is bigger than R. Dividing by R and rearranging gives 2R² - 3R + 1 below zero, which factors as (2R - 1)(R - 1) below zero, so it holds exactly when R is between one half and one. At R = 0.5 the vote gives 0.75 minus 0.25, which is 0.5, the same as one unit."
  • (Prof. Dohi) In your study R changes from input to input. Say each unit never fails on 80% of inputs and fails with probability one half on the other 20%. What does the vote give? You: "Each unit still has R = 0.8 plus 0.2 times 0.5, which is 0.9. But on the hard inputs every unit sits exactly at the break-even, so the vote's reliability there is also 0.5, and the system gets 0.8 plus 0.2 times 0.5, again 0.9. The vote gains nothing, because all the failures sit on the same inputs, which is Eckhardt and Lee's point."
  • (Prof. Dohi) So what would make the vote help again? You: "Making the units hard on different inputs. If each unit's hard 20% were a different set of inputs, at most one unit could fail on any input, so the vote would never fail, better even than the 0.972 that independence gives. Littlewood and Miller showed in 1989 that forced diversity can in theory give better than independent failure behaviour, and changing the agent or the model between runs is my RQ1's version of that."
