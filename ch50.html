1.4 How the professors for the meeting are chosen

No official or reported source says how they are chosen. What I checked and what I found:

  • Hiroshima says that the meeting is held online over Teams or Zoom and that the head of the program picks the day between 24 Nov and 2 Dec 2026. It says nothing about who joins. The facts are collected in 01_Meeting_Format_and_Previous_Questions/01_meeting_format_what_we_know.md.
  • Hiroshima's previous question sets page says it has no material for meetings like this one.
  • Prof. Zheng on 2 Nov: the questions depend on which professors join. So more than one person. See 01_Meeting_Format_and_Previous_Questions/zheng_meeting_2_nov_review.md.
  • After admission, the program's master's research uses "a main supervisor and two or more sub-supervisors, including one whose specialty differs from the main supervisor's" (主指導教員と，主指導教員とは専門の異なる教員を含む二人以上の副指導教員). That is about supervision after you join, not about this meeting, but it shows the program is used to mixing specialties. Source: https://www.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/info/education2
  • The only first-hand reports I found are student blogs about undergraduate transfer admission (編入学) to the School of Informatics and Data Science, a different process. Both say two professors per room: https://tuktuk-bosi.hatenablog.com/entry/2019/10/17/134518 and https://hu-ka-da.hatenablog.com/entry/2023/08/14/182318 . Weak evidence for your meeting.
  • I found no report from a master's applicant to this program.
  • One general source about meeting professors for graduate admission in Japan (not about Hiroshima): an article by the head of 志樹舎, an online prep school for graduate-school applicants, BCN+R, 5 Jan 2024. It says the people you meet are usually full-time faculty such as professors and associate professors (教授、准教授をはじめとした専任教員); sometimes the faculty are chosen to match the applicant's research theme (in the article's words, there are also cases where a faculty member who matches the applicant's chosen research theme takes charge); about 3 professors and 20 to 30 minutes is typical, though it differs by school; and the professors read the application documents beforehand (in the article's words, they look through the application documents submitted in advance). https://www.bcnretail.com/topics_detail9/20240105_391888.html

So plan for Prof. Zheng plus about two others, possibly from outside the lab, who have read your research plan. My guess, not a fact.

How it touches your plan Your RQ1 starts from his N-version paper [1].

Recent papers (all in GUIDE_2)

  • Zheng, Okamura, Dohi, "Can Generative AI Enhance the Effectiveness of N-Version Programming?", LNCS 16356, 2026. LLM-written versions and voting. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-981-95-6032-5_1
  • Yu, Ding, Dai, Zheng, Wu, Sato, "Toward Scalable LLM-Based Multi-Agent Collaboration ...", Electronics 15(11), 2475, 2026. Parallel LLM agents on a task graph. Note the co-author Yepeng Ding (dossier 2.4). https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15112475

Questions he might ask (in front of the others, often to help you show your work)

  1. "In two sentences, how is your study different from our N-version paper?" > Your paper asked whether LLM-written versions make N-version programming work, and varied the temperature and the reuse of conversation history. I take that question to real repository tasks run by coding agents that all read one context file, and I measure how much more often they fail together than independence predicts, with a beta-binomial model.
  2. "Newer models don't let you set the temperature. How does your plan handle that?" (He raised this himself on 2 Nov.) > In Claude Code and Codex I don't set it. I treat each setting as a distribution of runs, and I vary temperature only in my own harness, where I control it.
  3. "Why do this in a reliability lab and not a software engineering lab?" > I can build and run the agents. What I can't do yet is fit and check a dependence model with proper intervals, and that is what this lab does.
  4. "If we adjust your topic after you join, is that fine?" (He said on 2 Nov that the topic may be adjusted a little to fit the lab.) > Yes. The part I care about most is measuring shared failures with a proper model, and that sits close to your N-version work.

Questions he might ask

  1. "You take boundaries from branch conditions. Our Array paper learns the boundary with ML. Why not that?" > Branch conditions that compare a parameter with a constant are easy to pull out of the merged code, so I start there. Your learned boundaries would help where the condition is not a simple comparison, and I'd like to try your method as a comparison if time allows.
  2. "How exactly will you fit the beta-binomial?" > By maximum likelihood: the beta-binomial likelihood has a closed form with two parameters, so I can maximise it numerically. If I need a mixture, like a zero-inflated or two-Beta model, then an EM algorithm is the natural way, and I'd want to learn how your group does that.

Why your own infinite-server model for the field data?" > Each merged agent change brings new code, so I let each change add its own expected faults with a delay. I haven't compared my model with your NHMP models yet, and they would be a fair baseline to add. I read the preprint's abstract, introduction and section 3: the binomial-type models are under-dispersed and the Polya-type models over-dispersed, giving 22 new models from 11 baseline intensities.

Questions he might ask

  1. "What is the difference between reliability and availability?" (My guess: a basic question in his field. He teaches the master's course "Dependable Computing".) > Reliability is the probability that a system works without failure up to a time t. Availability is the probability that it is working at a given time, so it includes repair. My study measures a failure probability per input, which is closer to reliability on demand.
  2. "Choosing the number of runs looks like choosing redundancy. What is different?" > It is the same trade-off: more runs cost more and can cut failures. The difference is dependence: if runs share a bug, each extra run helps less, so the best number depends on ρ, and it can be 1. (If he mentions his new RESS paper, and you have read its abstract first: "I've read only the abstract. It also picks the best number of units by cost, so it's the same kind of question." The summary is in 07_Lab_Papers/GUIDE_3_new_papers_since_Oct_2026.md.)
  3. "Eckhardt and Lee is from 1985. Do you know later models?" > I know the Littlewood and Miller model only by name: it extends Eckhardt and Lee to versions made by different methods, and then the coincident-failure term can even be smaller than under independence. My comparison of two settings a and b is that case, so I should read it properly before I use it.
  4. "Why not a normal NHPP for the field data?" > A finite-fault NHPP assumes one fixed code base, but agent code keeps arriving. So each merged change adds its own expected faults, found after a delay, which is an infinite-server queue as in your paper with Osaki and Trivedi. (Details: 05_Questions_on_My_Proposal/07_questions_on_my_proposal.md, Q34 to Q36.)

  • Yu, Ding, Sato, "DynTaskMAS: A Dynamic Task Graph-driven Framework for Asynchronous and Parallel LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems", ICAPS 2025. The conference version of the paper with Prof. Zheng. https://doi.org/10.1609/icaps.v35i1.36130

Questions he might ask

  1. "Your agents call remote models that change over time. How do you make runs reproducible?" > I pin the agent versions, run in a clean container, and log the model name, date and full transcript of every run. I can't freeze a remote model, so I run all settings in an interleaved order over a short period, and the open-weight model is fully pinned.
  2. "In our network study, agents failed when they could not see the evidence. Is a shared context file the same kind of thing?" > I think so: every run sees the same code, task text and context file, so they share the same blind spots. If the file points to a helper with a bug, they may all fail the same way, and that shared failure is what I measure.
  3. "How do you decide a failure is real? In SEMA we accept a finding only if it replays." > Same idea: an input counts as a failure only if I can rerun it on the version and on the merged code and the outputs differ under a fixed rule. I also check 20 disagreements per task by hand.
  4. "Could formal methods replace your random testing?" > For small pure functions, an equivalence check might be stronger, but I don't know formal verification well yet. I chose differential testing because it scales to many versions and gives a failure probability, which the reliability model needs.
  5. "Why not use several agents with different roles to break the correlation?" > That's a good next step. A role-based setup would just be one more setting, and I could estimate its covariance with the others in the same way.

2.6 Prof. Takeshi Emura (江村 剛志)
Title, lab Professor, Survival Analysis lab (生存時間解析), since 1 Apr 2025. Before that, Professor at the Institute of Statistical Mathematics (2023 to 2025).
Why likely The only statistician in your program. His lab's listed keywords include "Reliability" and "Maximum likelihood estimation" (English lab page). Your plan is a statistical model of correlated binary outcomes, so if the program wants a statistician in the meeting, he is the natural choice.
Research in plain words Methods for time-to-event data: copulas (a way to model how two outcomes depend on each other), censoring (you stop watching before the event happens) and truncation, small-sample estimation. He wrote a review of field failure data for reliability (QREI 2022) and the introduction to a Dec 2025 special issue on "survival analysis and reliability analysis" (特集 生存時間解析と信頼性解析, in the journal 統計数理; researchmap). Book: Survival Analysis with Correlated Endpoints: Joint Frailty-Copula Models (Springer, 2019). In 2026 he teaches the master's course Data Literacy.
How it touches your plan Your beta-binomial is a model for correlated yes/no outcomes in clusters (inputs as clusters, runs as members). Your field data has delays and an observation window, which means censoring.

Recent papers (from the abstracts)

  • Liang, Emura, Ma, Xin, Huang, "Testing the homogeneity of proportions for correlated bilateral data via the Clayton copula", Commun. Stat. Simul. Comput., 2026. Paired yes/no outcomes (two eyes of one patient) are dependent; a copula models that dependence more flexibly than fixed structures. https://doi.org/10.1080/03610918.2026.2637680 (preprint: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00523)
  • Taketomi, Nakazono, Okada, Emura, "Shrinkage estimation for the rate parameter under the exponential distribution with censored survival data", Commun. Stat. Theory Methods, 2026. When there are few events, the maximum likelihood estimate is biased and noisy; a penalised likelihood gives smaller error. https://doi.org/10.1080/03610926.2025.2602731
  • Baur, Pauly, Emura, "Permutation Tests Based on the Copula-Graphic Estimator and Their Use for Survival Tree Construction", Statistics in Medicine, 2026. Survival trees without assuming independent censoring. https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.70483
  • Background, older: Emura, Michimae, "Left-truncated and right-censored field failure data: Review of parametric analysis for reliability", QREI 38, 2022. https://doi.org/10.1002/qre.3161

Questions he might ask

  1. "Why a beta-binomial and not, say, a random-effects logistic model or a copula?" > The beta-binomial is the simplest model where the failure chance varies across inputs, and its intraclass correlation ρ is the Var θ term of Eckhardt and Lee scaled to the 0 to 1 range: Var θ = ρ p(1 − p). A logistic model with an input random effect would be my second check. I don't know copulas well yet, and I'd like to learn whether they fit this better.
  2. "The beta-binomial assumes the runs are exchangeable. Are they?" > Within one setting I think so: same task, file, agent and model, and each run starts from a clean container. Across settings they are not, so I fit each setting on its own and use the covariance for pairs of settings.
  3. "With only about 10 runs and many inputs with zero failures, your estimate of ρ will be unstable. What then?" > Yes, the runs are the real sample size, so the interval for ρ may be wide, and ρ near 0 sits on the edge of its range, where normal-theory intervals fail. That's why I use a bootstrap over runs. A penalised or Bayesian estimate, like the shrinkage idea in your exponential paper, is something I'd want to try, but I haven't yet.
  4. "In the field data, faults found after your observation window are censored. How do you handle that?" > Faults not yet fixed when the window ends are right-censored, and I'd have to put that into the likelihood rather than ignore them. I haven't worked out the exact form yet; it's one of the things I'd learn in the first year.
  5. "How will you test whether the Beta fits?" > I can compare the observed share of inputs with 0, 1, 2 and more failing runs against what the fitted model predicts, and compare against a zero-inflated model by AIC. Without assuming a Beta, I can also estimate the pair and triple failure terms directly from counts, as a check.

  1. "A context file is text the agent trusts. Isn't that a prompt-injection risk?" > Yes. The same mechanism that makes a good rule reach every run would make a harmful rule reach every run too.
