Linear algebra and calculus: the must-know list

When and how long: Day 10.

The Hiroshima Q&A is about 15 minutes, spoken, and mostly about your plan, so a 10-minute integral is unlikely (the exact form of any maths question is not known yet). But an informatics professor may check basics in one or two sentences ("What is an eigenvalue?", "Is this matrix invertible?", "What is the integral of x e^(-ax)?"). This file lists what you must be able to say, points to solved problems in from_NAIST/, and adds 15 new problems on the topics your NAIST set did not cover: generator matrices, the matrix exponential, Markov chain ODEs, partial derivatives, Lagrange multipliers, double integrals and reliability ODEs.

How the answers were checked. Every answer to the 15 new problems was computed on 12 Nov 2026 with Python and recomputed on 13 Nov 2026: sympy for the symbolic integrals, the ODE solutions, the critical point and the matrix exponential; numpy for the numbers (matrix powers, eigenvalues, numerical integration). All 15 answers matched. The NAIST problems were checked with SymPy when they were written (see the note at the top of that file). Nothing in this file is marked [CHECK].

Where the solved problems are. from_NAIST/02_practice_problems_with_solutions.md, problems LA1 to LA25 (linear algebra) and C1 to C25 (calculus). The statements in from_NAIST/01_topic_checklist_and_key_formulas.md give more detail. The new problems below are L1 to L15.

Part 1. The must-know list

Cover the right-hand side and say each one out loud in one or two sentences.

Linear algebra

Topic Say it Solved problems
Linear independence "Vectors are independent if the only combination that gives zero is the one with all coefficients zero." LA2
Basis and dimension "A basis is an independent set that spans the space. Every basis has the same number of vectors, the dimension." LA2, LA10
Rank "The rank is the number of pivots: the dimension of the column space, which equals that of the row space. Rank plus the dimension of the nullspace is the number of columns." LA7, LA8, LA10
Determinant "The factor by which the matrix scales volume, with a sign. It is zero exactly when the matrix is singular. det(AB) = det A det B, and det(cA) = c^n det A." LA3, LA16, LA17
Inverse "A^(-1) exists exactly when det A is not zero, equivalently rank n. For 2 by 2: 1/(ad - bc) times [d -b; -c a]. (AB)^(-1) = B^(-1) A^(-1)." LA4, LA5, LA18
Eigenvalues and eigenvectors "Ax = lambda x with x not zero. The eigenvalues solve det(A - lambda I) = 0. Their sum is the trace and their product is the determinant." LA19, LA20, L1
Diagonalisation "If A has n independent eigenvectors, A = S Lambda S^(-1), so A^k = S Lambda^k S^(-1) and e^(At) = S e^(Lambda t) S^(-1). Distinct eigenvalues guarantee it." LA19, LA21, L2, L4
Symmetric matrices "Real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors: A = Q Lambda Q^T (spectral theorem)." LA22
Positive definite "Symmetric with x^T A x > 0 for every nonzero x. Equivalently all eigenvalues positive, or all upper-left determinants positive. A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite." LA23, L5, L6
Inner product and orthogonality "x . y = sum x_i y_i = norm(x) norm(y) cos(angle). Orthogonal means the inner product is zero. Cauchy-Schwarz: abs(x . y) <= norm(x) norm(y)." LA1, LA11, LA14
Least squares "Minimise norm(Ax - b)^2, the sum of squared errors. The normal equations A^T A x-hat = A^T b make the error orthogonal to the columns of A. This is linear regression." LA12, LA13, 03_statistics.md S20
Markov matrices "Strang's Markov matrices have columns that sum to 1. Probability books use rows that sum to 1 and write pi P = pi. Either way, 1 is an eigenvalue and its eigenvector is the steady state." LA20, L4
Generator matrices "A CTMC generator has rates off the diagonal and rows that sum to 0, so 0 is always an eigenvalue, and the steady state is its left eigenvector." L1, L2

Calculus

Topic Say it Solved problems
Limits "The value f(x) approaches as x approaches a. For 0/0 forms use L'Hopital or a Taylor expansion. (1 + x/n)^n tends to e^x." C2, C4, L15
Derivatives "The limit of the difference quotient: the slope of the tangent. Product, quotient and chain rules." C3, C6, C7
Taylor series "f(x) = sum f^(n)(a) (x - a)^n / n!. e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2 + ..., ln(1 + x) = x - x^2/2 + ..., 1/(1 - x) = 1 + x + x^2 + ..." C21, L15
Integrals, substitution, by parts "The fundamental theorem links integrals and antiderivatives. By parts: integral u dv = u v - integral v du." C14, C15, C16, L8
Improper integrals, Gamma function "An integral to infinity is a limit. integral_0^infinity x^n e^(-ax) dx = n!/a^(n+1), and Gamma(n) = (n - 1)!, Gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi)." C20, L9, L14
Partial derivatives and gradient "A partial derivative changes one variable and holds the others fixed. The gradient is the vector of partials. It points uphill fastest, and it is zero at a critical point." L6
Hessian and the second-derivative test "The matrix of second partials. Positive definite at a critical point means a minimum, negative definite a maximum." L6
Lagrange multipliers "To optimise f subject to g = 0, solve grad f = lambda grad g with g = 0." L7
Double integrals "Integrate in one variable, then the other (Fubini). You may swap the order if you redraw the region. In polar coordinates dx dy = r dr d(angle)." L12, L13, L14
ODEs "y' = -a y gives y = y0 e^(-at). A linear first-order equation is solved with an integrating factor. A system u' = Au is solved by u(t) = e^(At) u(0)." C25, L3, L10, L11

Part 2. 15 new problems with full solutions

L1. Eigenvalues of a generator matrix

A machine is up (state 0) or down (state 1). It fails at rate lambda and is repaired at rate mu. The generator is Q = [-lambda lambda; mu -mu]. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Why is 0 always an eigenvalue? What does its left eigenvector mean?

Solution.

  • trace Q = -(lambda + mu), det Q = lambda mu - lambda mu = 0. So the eigenvalues are 0 and -(lambda + mu).
  • Each row sums to 0, so Q (1, 1)^T = 0. Right eigenvector (1, 1) for 0.
  • For -(lambda + mu): Q (lambda, -mu)^T = (-lambda^2 - lambda mu, mu lambda + mu^2) = -(lambda + mu)(lambda, -mu). Eigenvector (lambda, -mu).
  • Left eigenvector for 0: pi Q = 0 with pi = (mu, lambda)/(lambda + mu). Check: mu(-lambda) + lambda(mu) = 0. This is the steady state: the machine is up a share mu/(lambda + mu) of the time.

Say it. "Rows of a generator sum to zero, so the all-ones vector gives eigenvalue zero. The other eigenvalue is minus lambda minus mu, from the trace. The left eigenvector for zero is the steady state."

L2. The matrix exponential e^(Qt)

For the same Q, find P(t) = e^(Qt). Give P_00(4) for lambda = 0.01 per hour and mu = 0.5 per hour.

Solution (diagonalise). Write s = lambda + mu.

S = [1 lambda; 1 -mu]   (eigenvectors as columns),   Lambda = diag(0, -s)
S^(-1) = (1/s) [mu lambda; 1 -1]
e^(Qt) = S diag(1, e^(-st)) S^(-1)
       = (1/s) [ mu + lambda e^(-st)    lambda - lambda e^(-st) ]
               [ mu - mu e^(-st)        lambda + mu e^(-st)     ]

Faster trick. Q^2 = -s Q (check: Q^2 = s [lambda -lambda; -mu mu]). So Q^n = (-s)^(n-1) Q, and the series gives

e^(Qt) = I + Q (1 - e^(-st)) / s

Numbers: s = 0.51. P_00(4) = (0.5 + 0.01 e^(-2.04)) / 0.51 = 0.98294. As t grows, both rows tend to (mu, lambda)/s = (0.98039, 0.01961).

Check. Computing e^(4Q) numerically matches all four entries. Each row of P(t) sums to 1, and P(0) = I.

Say it. "I diagonalise Q: eigenvalues 0 and minus s. Then e^(Qt) is S times diag of 1 and e^(-st) times S inverse. Every entry is a constant plus a term that decays like e^(-st), and the constant is the steady state."

L3. Solving the 2-state CTMC equation directly

With p_0(t) = P(up at t), the forward equation is p_0'(t) = -lambda p_0 + mu (1 - p_0), p_0(0) = 1. Solve it. Find the availability at t = 4 h (lambda = 0.01, mu = 0.5) and how fast it settles.

Solution.

p_0' = mu - (lambda + mu) p_0           (linear, constant coefficients)
Steady state: p_0' = 0  ->  p_0 = mu/(lambda + mu) = mu/s
Let q = p_0 - mu/s.  Then q' = -s q, so q(t) = q(0) e^(-st) = (1 - mu/s) e^(-st) = (lambda/s) e^(-st)
p_0(t) = mu/s + (lambda/s) e^(-st)

A(4) = 0.98039 + 0.01961 e^(-2.04) = 0.98294, the same as P_00(4) in L2. The gap to the steady state halves every ln 2 / s = 1.36 h.

Check. sympy's dsolve gives the same function, and p_0(0) = mu/s + lambda/s = 1.

L4. Powers of a 2-state DTMC

P = [1 - alpha, alpha; beta, 1 - beta] with alpha = 0.1, beta = 0.3 (rows sum to 1). Find the eigenvalues, a formula for P^n, P^3 entry (0, 0), and the limit.

Solution. Eigenvalues: 1 (rows sum to 1) and trace - 1 = 1 - alpha - beta = 0.6.

P^n = (1/(alpha + beta)) [beta alpha; beta alpha] + ((1 - alpha - beta)^n / (alpha + beta)) [alpha -alpha; -beta beta]
(P^3)_00 = (0.3 + 0.1 (0.6)^3) / 0.4 = (0.3 + 0.0216)/0.4 = 0.804
Limit: every row -> (beta, alpha)/(alpha + beta) = (0.75, 0.25)

Check. numpy's matrix_power gives 0.804. Note the convention: here pi_(n+1) = pi_n P with row vectors. Strang (LA20) uses columns that sum to 1 and u_(k+1) = A u_k. They are transposes of each other.

L5. A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite

(a) Show that any covariance matrix Sigma is positive semidefinite. (b) Sigma = [4 2; 2 1]. Is it positive definite? What does that mean for X_1 and X_2?

Solution. (a) For any vector a: a^T Sigma a = Var(a^T X) >= 0. A variance is never negative. (b) det = 4 - 4 = 0, eigenvalues 5 and 0: semidefinite, not definite. Take a = (1, -2): a^T Sigma a = 4 - 8 + 4 = 0, so Var(X_1 - 2 X_2) = 0. X_1 = 2 X_2 + constant: one variable is a linear function of the other (correlation exactly 1).

Say it. "a transpose Sigma a is the variance of a transpose X, which can't be negative. A zero eigenvalue means some combination of the variables has zero variance, so they're exactly linearly related."

L6. Gradient, Hessian and a minimum

f(x, y) = x^2 + x y + 2 y^2 - 4x. Find the critical point and decide whether it is a minimum.

Solution.

grad f = (2x + y - 4, x + 4y) = (0, 0)
Second equation: x = -4y.  First: -8y + y = 4, so y = -4/7, x = 16/7.
Hessian H = [2 1; 1 4]: 2 > 0 and det = 8 - 1 = 7 > 0, so positive definite (eigenvalues 3 +- sqrt 2).
f(16/7, -4/7) = 256/49 - 64/49 + 32/49 - 448/49 = -224/49 = -32/7.

Minimum at (16/7, -4/7), value -32/7. Since H is constant and positive definite, it is the global minimum.

Link. Fitting a two-parameter model (GO, beta-binomial) by Newton-Raphson uses exactly this: the gradient of log L and its Hessian. A negative definite Hessian of log L means a maximum, and its inverse (with a minus sign) estimates the covariance of the MLE.

L7. Lagrange multipliers: combining two estimates

Two independent unbiased estimates of the same quantity have variances sigma_1^2 = 1 and sigma_2^2 = 4. You combine them as w_1 X_1 + w_2 X_2 with w_1 + w_2 = 1. Which weights minimise the variance? Then, as a second use, find the MLE of multinomial probabilities.

Solution.

Minimise w_1^2 sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 sigma_2^2 subject to w_1 + w_2 = 1.
L = w_1^2 sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 sigma_2^2 - lam (w_1 + w_2 - 1)
dL/dw_i = 2 w_i sigma_i^2 - lam = 0  ->  w_i = lam / (2 sigma_i^2), proportional to 1/sigma_i^2
w = (1, 1/4) / (5/4) = (0.8, 0.2),  minimum variance = 0.64 (1) + 0.04 (4) = 0.8 = 1/(1/1 + 1/4)

Weights 0.8 and 0.2 (inverse-variance weighting), variance 0.8. Multinomial: maximise sum n_i log p_i subject to sum p_i = 1. n_i / p_i = lam, so p_i = n_i / lam, and the constraint gives lam = n. p_i-hat = n_i / n.

L8. Integrating x e^(-ax), with and without a finite limit

(a) integral_0^infinity x e^(-ax) dx. (b) integral_0^T x e^(-ax) dx. Evaluate (b) for a = 0.5, T = 4. (NAIST C20(b) does part (a) in the form lambda x e^(-lambda x).)

Solution. By parts with u = x, dv = e^(-ax) dx, so du = dx and v = -e^(-ax)/a.

(a) [-x e^(-ax)/a]_0^infinity + (1/a) integral_0^infinity e^(-ax) dx = 0 + 1/a^2
(b) [-x e^(-ax)/a]_0^T + (1/a) [-e^(-ax)/a]_0^T = -T e^(-aT)/a + (1 - e^(-aT))/a^2
                                             = 1/a^2 - e^(-aT) (T/a + 1/a^2)
a = 0.5, T = 4:  4 - e^(-2) (8 + 4) = 4 - 12 e^(-2) = 2.376

(a) 1/a^2. (b) 2.376. With a = lambda, (a) times lambda is the exponential mean 1/lambda.

Check. Numerical integration gives 2.37598. At the boundary, x e^(-ax) tends to 0 as x tends to infinity, because the exponential wins.

Say it. "By parts, u equals x and dv equals e to the minus a x. The boundary term vanishes, and what is left is one over a times the integral of e to the minus a x, so one over a squared."

L9. Higher moments and the Gamma function

(a) integral_0^infinity x^2 e^(-ax) dx. (b) The general integral_0^infinity x^n e^(-ax) dx. (c) Var T for T ~ Exponential(lambda). (d) Gamma(5) and Gamma(3/2).

Solution. (a) By parts with u = x^2: the boundary term vanishes and you get (2/a) integral x e^(-ax) dx = (2/a)(1/a^2) = 2/a^3. (b) Substitute u = ax: integral_0^infinity x^n e^(-ax) dx = (1/a^(n+1)) integral_0^infinity u^n e^(-u) du = Gamma(n + 1)/a^(n+1) = n!/a^(n+1). (c) E[T^2] = lambda (2/lambda^3) = 2/lambda^2, so Var T = 2/lambda^2 - 1/lambda^2 = 1/lambda^2. (d) Gamma(5) = 4! = 24. Gamma(3/2) = (1/2) Gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi)/2 = 0.886 (used in the Weibull MTTF, file 04 R17 and R19; Gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi) comes from L14).

L10. y' = -a y, and constant-hazard reliability

(a) Solve y' = -a y with y(0) = y_0. (b) A part's reliability satisfies R'(t) = -lambda R(t), R(0) = 1, with lambda = 0.002 per hour. Find R(500) and the time at which R = 0.9.

Solution. (a) Separate: dy/y = -a dt, so ln|y| = -at + C and y = y_0 e^(-at). Half-life ln 2 / a. (b) R(t) = e^(-lambda t): R(500) = e^(-1) = 0.368. R = 0.9 when t = -ln(0.9)/0.002 = 52.7 h. The equation says the failure rate per surviving unit, -R'/R, is constant: that is the hazard, and constant hazard means exponential life.

L11. Goel-Okumoto from a differential equation

Assume faults are found at a rate proportional to the number still in the code: m'(t) = b (omega - m(t)), m(0) = 0. Solve it. What if the rate b(t) changes with time?

Solution.

Let r(t) = omega - m(t), the expected remaining faults. Then r' = -m' = -b r, r(0) = omega.
So r(t) = omega e^(-bt), and m(t) = omega (1 - e^(-bt)).        (Goel-Okumoto)
With b(t):  r' = -b(t) r  ->  r(t) = omega exp(-integral_0^t b(u) du)
            m(t) = omega [1 - exp(-integral_0^t b(u) du)] = omega F(t),
where F is the distribution whose hazard rate is b(t).

So every finite-failure NHPP m(t) = omega F(t) says "the detection rate per remaining fault is the hazard of F". For the delayed S-shaped model, b(t) = b^2 t / (1 + b t), which rises from 0 toward b.

Check. sympy's dsolve returns omega - omega e^(-bt).

Say it. "If each remaining fault is found at rate b, the remaining count decays exponentially, so the found count is omega times 1 minus e to the minus b t. That is the Goel-Okumoto model."

L12. A double integral: which happens first?

Failure time X ~ Exponential(lambda) and an independent repair-crew arrival Y ~ Exponential(mu). Find P(X < Y) as a double integral.

Solution.

P(X < Y) = integral_{y=0}^{infinity} integral_{x=0}^{y} lambda e^(-lambda x) mu e^(-mu y) dx dy
         = integral_0^infinity (1 - e^(-lambda y)) mu e^(-mu y) dy
         = 1 - mu/(lambda + mu) = lambda/(lambda + mu)

P(X < Y) = lambda/(lambda + mu). With lambda = 1, mu = 2: 1/3. This is the "race" between two exponential clocks that every CTMC uses (file 02, P14).

Check. Doing the double integral symbolically gives 1/3.

L13. Swapping the order of integration

Compute integral_0^1 integral_x^1 e^(y^2) dy dx.

Solution. e^(y^2) has no elementary antiderivative, so do not integrate in y first. The region is 0 <= x <= y <= 1. Swap the order: for each y in [0, 1], x runs from 0 to y.

integral_0^1 integral_0^y e^(y^2) dx dy = integral_0^1 y e^(y^2) dy = [ e^(y^2)/2 ]_0^1 = (e - 1)/2 = 0.859

Check. Numerical integration in the original order (y inside) gives 0.85914.

Say it. "The inner integral has no closed form, so I draw the region, a triangle under the line x = y, and swap the order. Then the inner integral is just y, and the outer one is a simple substitution."

L14. The Gaussian integral

Show integral_{-infinity}^{infinity} e^(-x^2) dx = sqrt(pi). Why does the normal density integrate to 1?

Solution.

I^2 = integral integral e^(-(x^2 + y^2)) dx dy        (two copies, independent variables)
    = integral_0^(2 pi) integral_0^infinity e^(-r^2) r dr d(angle)    (polar: dx dy = r dr d(angle))
    = 2 pi [ -e^(-r^2)/2 ]_0^infinity = 2 pi (1/2) = pi
I = sqrt(pi) = 1.7725

Substituting x = u / sqrt(2) gives integral e^(-u^2/2) du = sqrt(2 pi), so the N(0, 1) density e^(-u^2/2)/sqrt(2 pi) integrates to 1. Substituting x^2 = t gives Gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi).

L15. Limits and small-p approximations

(a) lim_{n -> infinity} (1 - lambda/n)^n. (b) lim_{x -> 0} (1 - e^(-bx))/x, and its meaning for the GO model. (c) Compare (1 - p)^n with e^(-np) for p = 0.001, n = 1000 and for p = 0.01, n = 100.

Solution. (a) ln[(1 - lambda/n)^n] = n ln(1 - lambda/n) = n (-lambda/n - lambda^2/(2n^2) - ...) -> -lambda. So the limit is e^(-lambda). (This is why the binomial tends to the Poisson.) (b) 0/0 form. L'Hopital: b e^(-bx)/1 -> b. Or Taylor: 1 - e^(-bx) = bx - (bx)^2/2 + ... So for small t, the GO m(t) = omega (1 - e^(-bt)) is about omega b t: at the start, failures come at rate omega b. (c) p = 0.001, n = 1000: 0.999^1000 = 0.36770, e^(-1) = 0.36788. p = 0.01, n = 100: 0.99^100 = 0.36603 against 0.36788. The approximation is better when p is smaller. Also 1 - (1 - p)^n is about np when np is small.

Say it. "ln of 1 minus p is about minus p for small p, so 1 minus p to the n is about e to the minus n p. That's the Poisson limit of the binomial, and it's how I'd estimate the chance that at least one of many inputs fails."
