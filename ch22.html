Questions on why Japan, why Hiroshima and why this lab

When and how long: Fri 20 Nov, 30 minutes.

This file covers the motivation questions the professors may ask in the meeting between 24 Nov and 2 Dec. It was first written for your call with Prof. Zheng on 2 Nov, which is done. On that call he said they will likely ask "Why did you choose Japan?" and "Why did you choose Hiroshima University?". The topics: why a master's, why now, why Japan, why Hiroshima, why this lab and why Prof. Zheng, what you bring and want to learn, plans after the master's, NAIST, Japanese and life in Higashi-Hiroshima. The parts marked [FILL] and [YOU: ...] are yours to complete. Say them only once they are true, and if one is not true for you, drop it.

In the meeting with the professors, change "you" and "your" to "Prof. Zheng" and "Prof. Zheng's", and skip Q2's push line and the questions you ask him in Q22 and Q30.

1. Why a master's, and why now

Q1. Why do you want a master's degree? Why not keep working?

At work I review and test the changes coding agents make every day, but tests only cover the cases someone thought of. I want real numbers for how often this code fails and how much voting helps, and that needs probability models I can't build yet. I want to learn that properly, full time, with people who build these models.

Why he asks: he wants to know you will not leave halfway for a job. If he pushes ("You could learn statistics online"): "I could learn the basics online. But fitting a model to real failure data and defending it is something I want to learn from people who do it every day."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said you want to know how much voting helps. If three runs each fail with probability 0.1, independently, how often does a 2-out-of-3 vote fail?"
    You: "The vote fails when at least two of the three fail, so it's 3 times p squared times 1 minus p, plus p cubed. With p = 0.1 that's 3 times 0.01 times 0.9, which is 0.027, plus 0.001, so 0.028. So voting would cut the failure chance from 0.1 to about 0.028, but only if the runs fail independently."
  2. Prof: "And if they don't? Say every run fails on the same 10% of inputs."
    You: "Then on those inputs all three runs fail, so the vote fails there too, and the failure chance stays at 0.1: voting buys nothing. In general, the vote fails with the average over inputs of 3 theta squared minus 2 theta cubed, where theta of x is the chance that a run fails on input x. So what matters is how the failures spread over the inputs, not only their mean."
  3. Prof: "Can a vote ever be worse than a single run?"
    You: "Yes. Where theta is above one half, most runs are wrong, so the vote is wrong there more often than one run, and if those inputs carry enough weight, the vote is worse overall. Eckhardt and Lee showed this in 1985, which is why I want to measure how theta spreads instead of assuming independence."

Q2. Why now? You only finished your B.E. this year, and you have a good job.

These tools are new, so the questions are open now. Two studies this year found that code written by LLMs fails together more often than independence predicts, and neither used a context file like the ones I maintain at work. After about a year of building LLM systems at Layerpath, I keep meeting questions that engineering alone can't answer.

If he pushes ("Why not work two more years first?"): "This intake is for April 2027, and you have agreed to supervise me for it. I would rather start while the question is still open."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Which two studies, and what exactly did they find?"
    You: "Ron and colleagues had five agent harnesses implement the 1986 Knight and Leveson specification. Two or more of the 48 accepted programs failed together on 429 of one million inputs, against about 115 expected under independence. Nogueira and colleagues used 224 contest problems and twelve models, and voting over three versions captured only 0.43 of the gain that independent failures would allow."
  2. Prof: "Where does the 115 come from?"
    You: "From each program's own failure rate. Assuming independence, the chance that two or more programs fail on one input is 1 minus the chance that none fail, minus the chance that exactly one fails. Times one million inputs, that gives about 115. They compare the observed 429 with it using the Knight and Leveson z statistic, which came out at about 29."
  3. Prof: "Then why not just use that z in your own study?"
    You: "The z says how surprising the joint failures are under independence, but it grows with the number of inputs, so it isn't a size of dependence. The correlation rho of a beta-binomial model is a size: it's 0 for independence and 1 when runs always fail together. Together with the mean failure probability, it gives me the vote's failure chance. I keep the z as a baseline, so my numbers can be compared with theirs."

Q3. You are an LLM engineer. Why reliability modelling?

Because building agents is the part I can already do. What I can't do yet is say how reliable their output is, with a number and an interval. I have seen an instruction to a model have no effect at all while our logs said it was applied, so "the tests passed" doesn't feel like the full answer to me.

If he pushes ("Give an example"): "In BrandMemory, the brand voice section had the lowest priority, so a 3,200-character limit cut it first. After the fix, one A/B comparison showed contractions falling from 6 to 0. One comparison is not a study, but since then I check whether a model really follows an instruction."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said 'a number and an interval'. How would you get an interval, say for your correlation rho?"
    You: "I'd fit the beta-binomial model by maximum likelihood and then bootstrap: resample the runs with replacement, refit each time, and take the middle 95% of the refitted values. I resample runs, not inputs, because the same runs meet every input, so the inputs aren't independent samples."
  2. Prof: "Your plan has ten runs per setting. Is resampling ten runs enough?"
    You: "It may not be, and I haven't checked it yet. With only ten runs the bootstrap has little to work with, so the interval can come out too narrow or unstable. The pilot in the first half of Year 1 will show whether ten runs give a useful interval for rho, and if not, I'd change the number of runs then."
  3. Prof: "Apart from more runs, is there another way to get a usable interval from few runs?"
    You: "A parametric bootstrap might: I'd simulate new data from the fitted model and refit each time, which can help with small samples. The catch is that my beta-binomial treats each input on its own, but one run's failures are linked across inputs, so a simple simulation would make the interval too narrow. Your lab works on parametric bootstrap for NHPP models, which I know only from abstracts, so I'd like to learn how you would handle that."

Yes. I will leave Layerpath before I enrol and study full time, and I'll hand over my work properly before I go. My CEO wrote one of my recommendation letters, so the company knows I'm applying, and it supports this. [FILL: one true sentence on why you are ready to leave, for example "I have built the AI features I joined to build, and now I want to learn how to measure them" / "I want two years on one question, and a startup job doesn't give me that" / "I've talked it over with my family and we have planned for it".]

Q6. Why not study in India?

Among the labs I looked at, yours is the closest to my reliability question. [FILL: one true sentence about India, for example "I want to study away from my job so research gets my full time" / "I want to see how research is done in another country" / "I didn't find a group in India working on N-version programming with LLMs, as far as I looked" (only if you really looked).]

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "What makes ours the closest? Plenty of groups work on software testing."
    You: "A lot of the testing work I've seen stops at whether the tests pass. Your lab puts a probability model on the failures and shows how much quality actually improved, and it has already asked whether generative AI helps N-version programming. That's exactly the step I can't do yet."
  2. Prof: "Give me one probability model this lab uses for failures, and what it tells you."
    You: "The NHPP software reliability growth model. It treats the number of faults found by time t as a Poisson count with mean m of t. In the Goel-Okumoto form, m of t is a times 1 minus e to the minus b t, where a is the expected total number of faults and b is the rate at which each one is found. Fitting a and b to failure data tells you how many faults are probably still left."
  3. Prof: "Then compute it. With a = 100 and b = 0.05 per day, how many faults remain after 30 days?"
    You: "m of 30 is 100 times 1 minus e to the minus 1.5. e to the minus 1.5 is about 0.223, so about 77.7 faults are found by day 30, and about 22.3 are still expected to remain. In general, the expected number left is a times e to the minus b t."

I chose Japan first, and of the labs I found in Japan, yours fits my question best. [FILL: your true reason for Japan, the same one as in Q5, or another true reason, for example "I want a two-year research master's with a thesis" / "I didn't apply to the US or Europe this year".]

    You: "Their study is close to mine, and I use their z statistic as a baseline. 3. Why Hiroshima University and this programme

Q9. Why Hiroshima University?

Because your lab is here, and you have agreed to supervise me. On the programme's list of supervisors, the Dependable Systems group has fields like software reliability engineering and software testing, which is where my question sits. This route also lets me apply from India, and the meeting with the professors is held online.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Software testing is a big field. Which of our testing work is closest to your plan?"
    You: "The boundary value work of Guo, Prof. Okamura and Prof. Dohi. They define the boundary coverage distance, which measures how well a set of test inputs covers the boundaries, and generate test inputs that minimise it within random testing. In my plan I use it as a stress test next to the random inputs."
  2. Prof: "Why only as a stress test? Why not use it to estimate the failure probability?"
    You: "Because the 30 to 50 boundary inputs are chosen on purpose near the edges, not drawn the way inputs really occur, so the share that fail doesn't estimate a failure probability. For the estimate I use at least 10,000 random inputs per entry point, and for the boundary set I report the kill rate, as the paper does."
  3. Prof: "What is the kill rate, and what does it tell you here?"
    You: "You seed small faults into the code, called mutants, for example changing greater-than to greater-or-equal, and the kill rate is the share of mutants the test set detects. In that paper a mutant counts as killed if at least one input takes a different execution path than in the correct code. So it tells me how well the inputs reach the boundaries, not how often a user would hit a failure."

Q10. Why the Informatics and Data Science Program?

I'm an engineer who wants to learn research, and my question sits between software engineering and statistics: agents write the code, and probability models judge how reliable it is. This programme has both sides, so it fits my plan.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Which courses on the statistics side would you take?"
    You: "In this year's syllabus for the programme I saw Bayesian Inference, Statistical Machine Learning, and Dependable Computing taught by Prof. Dohi, all in English. The 2027 list wasn't published when I looked, so I'd check it once it is. I'd take those early, because my only statistics course so far is BCS301."
  2. Prof: "What did BCS301 cover, and what's missing for your plan?"
    You: "It covered probability distributions, Markov chains, hypothesis tests and ANOVA. What's missing is estimation: maximum likelihood, the bootstrap, and fitting and checking models like the beta-binomial and NHPP models."
  3. Prof: "Then tell me what maximum likelihood does for the beta-binomial in your plan."
    You: "For each input I count how many of the k runs fail. The beta-binomial gives the probability of each count from two parameters, a and b, and maximum likelihood picks the a and b that make all the observed counts most probable together. From them, the mean failure probability is a over a plus b, and the correlation rho is 1 over a plus b plus 1."

Q11. How did you find our lab?

Your programme was already on my shortlist in September. On 20 October, looking for labs in Japan close to my question, I found your lab on the programme's list of supervisors. Your group's 2026 paper on whether generative AI can make N-version programming more effective caught my attention, because my own question is about how reliable the code from AI coding agents is. So I wrote to Prof. Dohi that same day with a one-page plan and my CV.

Why he asks: a normal opening question. He wants to see that you chose the lab and did not mass-mail. If he pushes ("Did you write to other professors?"): "Yes, I wrote to several other professors in Japan, and I'm applying to NAIST." If he asks which: "On 20 October I also wrote to one professor each at JAIST, Nagoya, Osaka, Tsukuba, Waseda, Science Tokyo and Ritsumeikan, each time pointing to their recent work that was close to my question."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "What did you take from that paper?"
    You: "I've only read the abstract, because the full text is behind the paywall. It found that majority voting made strong models like GPT-4o more reliable but helped little with weaker ones, and that more diversity from temperature or reused conversation history did not always mean more reliability. My plan asks the same question on real repository tasks."
  2. Prof: "Why do you think more diversity didn't always help?"
    You: "I can think of two reasons: the versions may still fail on the same hard inputs, or a higher temperature may make each version worse on its own. I asked you this on 2 November, and you said you'd show me your team's newer results, so I don't know yet which it is."
  3. Prof: "How would your own study tell those two apart?"
    You: "I'd look at two numbers separately: the mean failure probability p of one run, and the correlation rho between runs. If a higher temperature raises p, each version got worse, and if p stays about the same but rho stays high, the versions still fail together. The vote failure depends on both, so the model separates them."

Q12. Your first email had a different plan. Why did it change?

My first one-page plan asked which instructions in AGENTS.md and CLAUDE.md agents follow, and how they affect reliability. When I looked more closely at your lab's work, I saw that the stronger question for this lab is whether runs that share one file fail together, and what that means for voting. So I rebuilt the plan around coincident failures, your N-version question and the boundary value method.

Why he asks: Prof. Dohi got both versions. Prof. Zheng was copied from 29 Oct. On 2 Nov he said Prof. Dohi had forwarded your email and that he had looked through your CV and proposal, so he may have seen the first plan too.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "So is the first question, which rules agents follow, gone completely?"
    You: "Not completely, but now it's a check, not a research question. A checker confirms that each change to the file really changed what the agent did. I keep a checker only if it agrees with my hand labels on at least 90% of 20 or more examples, and I also report Cohen's kappa."
  2. Prof: "Why kappa, if you already have 90% agreement?"
    You: "Because part of that agreement can happen by chance, especially when most examples fall in one class. Kappa takes out the chance part: it's the observed agreement minus the chance agreement, divided by 1 minus the chance agreement."
  3. Prof: "Give me a number. You and the checker each say 'follows the rule' for 90% of the examples, and you agree on 90% of them. What is kappa?"
    You: "Chance agreement is 0.9 times 0.9 plus 0.1 times 0.1, which is 0.82. So kappa is 0.9 minus 0.82, which is 0.08, divided by 1 minus 0.82, which is 0.18, so about 0.44. That's only moderate agreement, which is why I report kappa next to the 90%."

4. Why this lab, and why Prof. Zheng

Q13. Why the Dependable Systems Laboratory?

Your lab's page says, "In every case we pair the technique with conventional probabilistic evaluation, because what matters is showing quantitatively how much the quality actually improved." My question needs that kind of answer. My plan takes the question of your N-version paper to real repository tasks, and the boundary value method and pysrat fit straight into it. I can bring the agent side, and the reliability modelling is what I want to learn here.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You say pysrat fits straight into your plan. How? It fits NHPP models to one code base."
    You: "Only in the optional field study, and not for one fixed code base, because agent code keeps arriving. Each merged change brings some expected faults that are found after a random delay, and pysrat gives me the families for that delay distribution. Then the expected number found by time t is the sum, over changes, of each change's faults times the chance that one is found by now."
  2. Prof: "How many faults does one change bring in your model, and what does that depend on?"
    You: "Change j brings omega j expected faults, which is L j times e to the beta times z j. L j is the number of added lines, and z j holds features of the change, such as which agent wrote it. So bigger changes bring more faults, and each coefficient in beta says how much one feature raises or lowers the faults per line."
  3. Prof: "Suppose the coefficient for 'written by an agent' comes out at 0.4. What does that mean in numbers?"
    You: "e to the 0.4 is about 1.49, so an agent-written change brings about 49% more expected faults per added line than a human one, with the other features the same. That's close to the gap Xia and Miller saw, where bug fixes changed 4.0% of agent-written lines against 2.7% of human ones, a ratio of about 1.48. But they measured lines changed by fixes, not faults, so I'd use it only as a rough check."

Q14. What do you know about the lab's history and work?

I know it only from the outside, from your website and paper abstracts. The lab works on six areas: software reliability models, support for software development such as test generation, stochastic models, optimal maintenance, dependability and performance, and security. Your page says the lab was among the first to ask when software rejuvenation should be done, and more recently it has turned to large language models, with the N-version work and work on how several agents should cooperate.

If he pushes ("Anything older?"): "In 2004 Prof. Dohi, with Osaki and Trivedi, used an infinite-server queue to unify finite and infinite fault NHPP models, and my optional field study uses the same idea. I have only read that paper's abstract." If he pushes ("Who is in the lab, and what tools does it use?"): "There are four faculty: Prof. Dohi, Prof. Okamura, you and Dr. Wu, with 3 doctoral, 10 master's, 7 undergraduate and 2 research students. The tools include pysrat in Python and Rsrat in R. The older SRATS Excel add-ins are no longer maintained."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Explain rejuvenation simply. Why would there be a best time to do it?"
    You: "Software ages as it runs, for example through memory leaks, so a failure becomes more likely over time, and rejuvenation means restarting it on purpose before it fails. Restarting too often wastes time while the system is down, and restarting too rarely lets it fail. So somewhere in between is the timing that keeps it up the most."
  2. Prof: "Write the availability for the simplest model, with a planned restart at time T."
    You: "Each cycle ends either with a failure before T, with probability F of T, or with the planned restart at T. The expected up time per cycle is the integral of 1 minus F of t from 0 to T. The expected down time is the mean repair time times F of T, plus the mean restart time times 1 minus F of T. By the renewal reward argument, availability is up time over up time plus down time, and I choose T to maximise it."
  3. Prof: "When is there a finite best T? What if the failure time is exponential?"
    You: "If it's exponential, the failure rate is constant, so the system doesn't age, and availability keeps rising with T: the best choice is never to restart. In this basic model a finite best T needs aging, meaning an increasing failure rate such as a Weibull with shape above 1, and a restart that is quicker than a repair. Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You say you'd ask before changing direction. At a startup you change direction fast. Why does it matter more in research?"
    You: "Because in a study, changing something halfway can spoil the comparison: the runs before and after the change no longer come from the same setting. At a startup you just ship the better version, but here the before and after have to be comparable, so I'd bring the result to you first and keep the plan running until we agree."
  2. Prof: "Give me a concrete example from your own study."
    You: "The agent versions. If Claude Code updates halfway through the main runs and I switch, a change in rho could come from the update, not from the file. That's why I pin each agent and its model to fixed versions in a clean container and record them for every run."
  3. Prof: "And if the provider retires that model before you finish?"
    You: "Then I can't finish that setting as planned, so I'd report the runs I have, with wider intervals, and say clearly that the setting was cut short. It's also one reason my plan includes an open-weight model that I run myself, which can't be taken away."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "What does 'simple and clear' English mean in practice?"
    You: "Short sentences, one point at a time, no slang, and the key points written down, on a slide or in the chat, because written English is often easier to follow than fast speech. And I'd check that I've been understood, not assume it."
  2. Prof: "How would you check that someone understood a technical idea, without just asking 'is it clear?'"
