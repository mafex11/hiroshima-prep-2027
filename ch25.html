Basics used by almost every paper

Basics to know (NHPP software reliability models)

  • N(t) is the number of faults found by time t. In an NHPP, N(t) is Poisson with mean m(t), the mean value function, and λ(t) = m′(t) is the rate. Counts in separate time intervals are independent, and the mean equals the variance.
  • Finite-failure models write m(t) = ωF(t): ω is the expected total number of faults and F is the fault-detection time distribution. Goel-Okumoto: m(t) = ω(1 − e^(−bt)). Changing F (gamma, Pareto, log-normal and so on) gives the other models.
  • For fault times t_1, ..., t_n seen up to T: log L = Σ log λ(t_i) − m(T). Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make this largest.
  • AIC = 2k − 2 log L, where k is the number of parameters. Lower is better.
  • Reliability after testing to t: R(x | t) = exp(−[m(t + x) − m(t)]), the chance of no failure in the next x time units.
  • Goodness of fit vs prediction: most of these papers fit on the first 20%, 50% or 80% of the data (early, middle, late testing) and predict the rest. PMSE is a squared-error measure of the predicted cumulative counts on the held-out part (in Qiu et al. it is a square-root form divided by the number of held-out points; check the exact formula in each paper). PMAE uses absolute errors.

1. The two lab works your proposal cites

Semi-parametric self-exciting SRM with a neural kernel (Qiu, Dohi, Wu, Zheng, Okamura, IJMEMS 2026)

Status: your reference [3]. Open access. PDF: 04_Papers/pdfs/REF3_Qiu_et_al_2026_IJMEMS_Hawkes_SRM.pdf.

What it does, in plain words: NHPP models assume one detected fault does not change the chance of finding the next, but in real testing one fault can lead testers to related faults, so detections come in clusters. The paper models this with a Hawkes process: a base rate that falls exponentially over time, plus an excitation kernel learned by a shallow neural network. On eight datasets (four classic ones and four built from GitHub issue histories, 104 to 397 faults each), it had the best PMSE on 5 of 8 datasets at the 20% and 50% points against ten parametric Hawkes models, beat the NHPP models on 7 of 8 at the 50% point, but was best on only 2 at the 80% point.

Link to your proposal: your field-data part would build fix data from GitHub commit messages, as this paper built DS5 to DS8 from GitHub issues, and because your counts will be far below its 104 to 397 faults, you plan to pool repositories.

Honest line: "I've read the abstract, and the data and results parts because I cite it. I haven't worked through how the mean value function is derived." [CONFIRM: only say this once you have read the abstract; keep "and the data and results parts" only if you read them.]

Basics to know (Hawkes process)

  • Rate: λ(t | history) = μ(t) + Σ over past faults φ(t − t_i). μ(t) is the background rate. φ is the extra rate each fault adds, and it fades with time.
  • In this paper μ(t) = v b e^(−bt), which falls as testing goes on, and a parametric kernel is φ(τ) = α f(τ), where α is the strength of self-excitation.
  • If you drop the history term, you get an NHPP back.
  • Cluster view (from the paper): background faults start clusters, and each fault can trigger more.
  • General fact, not from the paper: when f is a density, α is the average number of faults each fault triggers directly. α must be below 1, whatever the base rate, or a cluster's expected size is infinite.
  • The expected rate solves E[λ(t)] = μ(t) + ∫₀ᵗ φ(t − s) E[λ(s)] ds, a Volterra equation. The paper derives such an equation for the expected count and solves it numerically.

Q5. What is the difference between an NHPP and a Hawkes process?

In an NHPP the rate depends only on time, so a fault found now does not change the rate later. In a Hawkes process each past fault adds a bump to the rate that dies away: λ(t) = μ(t) + Σ φ(t − t_i). If you remove the history term, you are back to an NHPP.

Basics to know (phase-type distribution)

  • A phase-type distribution is the time until a small continuous-time Markov chain reaches its end (absorbing) state. Each phase is an exponential wait.

Basics to know (bootstrap)

  • Non-parametric bootstrap: resample the data with replacement, recompute the estimate, repeat many times (say 1,000), and use the spread.
  • Parametric bootstrap: fit the model, simulate new data sets from the fitted model, refit on each.
  • Percentile interval: the middle 95% of the bootstrap estimates.
  • In your RQ1: resample the unit that is independent. The same runs meet every input, so you resample runs, not inputs.

Q12. Why bootstrap?

Because a point estimate alone can be far off, and an exact interval is often hard to derive, which is what your abstract says too. The bootstrap gives the spread by redoing the estimate on many resampled data sets. In my plan the same 10 runs meet every input, so I resample runs, refit the beta-binomial, and take the middle 95%.

Q13. Your bootstrap is non-parametric and ours is parametric. Why the difference?

In RQ1 I don't want to trust a model for how runs differ, so I resample the runs I actually have. A parametric bootstrap simulates new data from the fitted model, which makes sense for an NHPP, where the model is the point of the analysis. For the field-data part, with small fault counts, I'd like to learn your parametric method.

Thinning-based parametric bootstrap (Wu, Dohi, Zheng, Okamura, DASC 2025)

Status: abstract only (paywalled).

Basics to know (thinning)

  • To simulate an NHPP with rate λ(t) ≤ λ: draw a Poisson process with constant rate λ, then keep each point at time t with probability λ(t)/λ*. The kept points form an NHPP with rate λ(t).
  • The reverse use: delete each observed fault independently with probability q. What remains is an NHPP with rate (1 − q)λ(t). Wu et al.'s RIMS 2026 paper (paper 11 below) uses q = 1/n and then corrects by n/(n − 1).

Q15. What is thinning?

It is a way to get one point process from another by deleting points. If you keep each point at time t with probability λ(t)/λ, where λ is a higher constant rate, the kept points form an NHPP with rate λ(t). Your group's RIMS paper credits the idea to Lewis and Shedler. [CONFIRM: only if you read section 3.2]

Q16. How would you check that one bootstrap method is better than another?

By Monte Carlo simulation, as your paper did: generate data from a model whose parameters I know, run each method and see how close the estimates are. I would also check how often the 95% intervals contain the true value. I could do the same for my RQ1, by simulating runs from a known beta-binomial and checking my bootstrap over runs.

Data-driven reliability without assuming the fault-count distribution (Dohi, Zheng, Okamura, Quality Engineering 2020)

Status: abstract only (paywalled).

What it does, in plain words: the paper evaluates software reliability without specifying the fault count distribution, while still treating the fault counts in testing as an NHPP. Basics to know (kernel estimation of a rate)

  • λ̂(t) = Σ_i (1/h) K((t − t_i)/h). K is a bump that integrates to 1 (Gaussian, Epanechnikov and others). h is the bandwidth.
  • Small h gives a spiky curve, large h a flat one. h is chosen from the data, for example by cross-validation.
  • No formula for m(t) is assumed. The price is that the estimate says little beyond the range of the data.

Q17. What is kernel estimation of a fault-detection rate?

You put a small bump, the kernel, at each fault time and add the bumps up, so the rate is high where faults are close together. The bandwidth sets how wide each bump is: too narrow gives a spiky curve, too wide hides real changes. Your paper tried several kernels and several ways to pick the bandwidth.

Q18. Why go non-parametric?

Because the right formula is unknown, and a wrong one gives wrong predictions. As I understand it, the cost is that a kernel estimate only knows the data it has seen, so predicting far ahead is harder. I face the same trade-off in RQ1, between assuming a Beta model and something more flexible.

It is uncertainty from not knowing the model's parameters exactly, because we estimated them from limited data. It is different from the randomness of failures themselves, which is called aleatory uncertainty. More data can reduce epistemic uncertainty, but not aleatory.

Basics to know (proportional intensity)

  • λ(t, x) = λ0(t) exp(xβ). If all β are 0, it is the plain NHPP with rate λ0.
  • One more unit of covariate k multiplies the rate by exp(β_k).
  • This is the same form as the Cox proportional hazards model.

Q21. Where does the exp(β′z) in your field model come from?

It is the Cox-style covariate form, the same one your group used in proportional intensity models: a baseline rate times exp(xβ). In my model each merged change j brings L_j exp(β′z_j) expected faults, so one unit of a covariate multiplies the expected faults by exp(β). My covariates describe the change, for example which agent wrote it, instead of the testing effort.

Are infinite-failure NHPP models useful? (Li, Dohi, Okamura, Software (MDPI) 2023)

Status: open access. PDF: 04_Papers/pdfs/LAB_Li_2023_Infinite_Failure_NHPP_SRMs_Useful.pdf.

What it does, in plain words: finite-failure (type-I) NHPP models assume a fixed number of faults, so the expected count levels off, while infinite-failure (type-II) models, such as Musa-Okumoto, let it grow without limit, for example because debugging can be imperfect. The paper builds 11 type-II models from the same 11 fault-detection time distributions as the type-I models, fits both by maximum likelihood to data from 16 real projects, and compares fit and prediction. Type-II models could predict better, especially early in testing, but the advantage faded as testing went on, and their predictions were more conservative.

Link to your proposal: your field-data part rejects a finite-fault NHPP because agent code keeps arriving, and your reference [12] uses an infinite-server queue to unify finite and infinite fault models.

Honest line: "I've read the abstract." [CONFIRM: only say this once you have read the abstract; add "and the conclusion" only if you read it.]

Basics to know (type-I and type-II)

  • Type-I (finite): m(t) = ωF(t), which levels off at ω, the expected total number of faults.
  • Type-II (infinite): M(t) = −ln(1 − F(t)), the cumulative hazard of F, which grows without limit. With a Pareto F it gives the logarithmic Poisson model of Musa and Okumoto (as the paper notes).
  • In type-I, the time to the next failure has a "defective" distribution: there is a positive chance it never comes.

Basics to know (Poisson binomial)

  • X_i is 1 with probability p_i and 0 otherwise, independently. S = Σ X_i.
  • E[S] = Σ p_i and Var[S] = Σ p_i(1 − p_i). If every p_i = p, it is the binomial.
  • Exact probabilities by a simple recursion: add one trial at a time, P(S = k after trial i) = P(k after i − 1)(1 − p_i) + P(k − 1 after i − 1) p_i.
  • A k-out-of-n:F system fails when at least k of n components fail, so P(system fails) = P(S ≥ k).

Q62. What is cross-validation for choosing a bandwidth?

You leave out part of the data, fit with a given bandwidth, check how well it explains the left-out part, and repeat. The bandwidth with the smallest error, or the highest held-out log-likelihood, wins. It picks the smoothness from the data instead of by eye.

Basics to know (Nadaraya-Watson and local linear)

  • Nadaraya-Watson: the value at a point is a weighted average of nearby observed values, with weights from the kernel.
  • Local linear: fit a weighted straight line around the point instead of a flat average.
  • At the edge of the data, a flat average only sees points on one side and is biased. The local linear fit removes most of that bias.

Q64. Why does behaviour near the boundary matter for prediction?

Prediction happens at the end of the data, which is a boundary. A plain kernel average there only sees points on one side, so it is biased. The local linear estimator fits a small straight line instead of a flat average, which removes most of that bias.

Q65. Several kernels with weights means more parameters. How do you avoid overfitting?

Choosing the weights and the bandwidth by cross-validation, on points left out of the fit, keeps it in check. With very little data it can still overfit, so I'd want to see the results by testing phase, as the abstract reports for the early phase.

Honest line: "I've read the abstract only." [CONFIRM: only say this once you have read the abstract]

Q82. Why treat a fault count as a continuous quantity?

When the code base is very large and the counts are big, a count in the thousands behaves almost like a continuous number, and continuous processes can be easier to handle. It is the same idea as using a normal approximation for a large binomial.

Q83. Why a gamma process rather than a Gaussian one?

A gamma process only goes up, because its increments are positive, which suits a cumulative count. A Gaussian process can go down, which a fault count never does. [CONFIRM: say more only if you read the chapter]
