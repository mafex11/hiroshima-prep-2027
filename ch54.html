Basics used by almost every paper

Basics to know (NHPP software reliability models)

  • N(t) is the number of faults found by time t. In an NHPP, N(t) is Poisson with mean m(t), the mean value function, and λ(t) = m′(t) is the rate. Counts in separate time intervals are independent, and the mean equals the variance.
  • Finite-failure models write m(t) = ωF(t): ω is the expected total number of faults and F is the fault-detection time distribution. Goel-Okumoto: m(t) = ω(1 − e^(−bt)). Changing F (gamma, Pareto, log-normal and so on) gives the other models.
  • For fault times t_1, ..., t_n seen up to T: log L = Σ log λ(t_i) − m(T). Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make this largest.
  • AIC = 2k − 2 log L, where k is the number of parameters. Lower is better.
  • Reliability after testing to t: R(x | t) = exp(−[m(t + x) − m(t)]), the chance of no failure in the next x time units.
  • Goodness of fit vs prediction: most of these papers fit on the first 20%, 50% or 80% of the data (early, middle, late testing) and predict the rest. In Qiu et al. [3] the cut is 20%, 50% or 80% of the total testing time; in Wu et al.'s RIMS 2026 paper it is 20%, 50% or 80% of the data points. PMSE is a squared-error measure of the predicted cumulative counts on the held-out part (in Qiu et al. it is a square-root form divided by the number of held-out points; check the exact formula in each paper). PMAE uses absolute errors.

1. The two lab works your proposal cites

Semi-parametric self-exciting SRM with a neural kernel (Qiu, Dohi, Wu, Zheng, Okamura, IJMEMS 2026)

Status: your reference [3]. Open access. PDF: 07_Lab_Papers/pdfs/REF3_Qiu_et_al_2026_IJMEMS_Hawkes_SRM.pdf.

What it does, in plain words: NHPP models assume one detected fault does not change the chance of finding the next, but in real testing one fault can lead testers to related faults, so detections come in clusters. The paper models this with a Hawkes process: a base rate that falls exponentially over time, plus an excitation kernel learned by a shallow neural network. On eight datasets (four classic ones and four built from GitHub issue histories, 104 to 397 faults each), it had the best PMSE on 5 of 8 datasets at the 20% and 50% points against ten parametric Hawkes models, beat the NHPP models on 7 of 8 at the 50% point, but was best on only 2 at the 80% point.

Link to your proposal: your field-data part would build fix data from GitHub commit messages, as this paper built DS5 to DS8 from GitHub issues. Your 28 Oct proposal to Prof. Zheng adds that your counts will be far below its 104 to 397 faults, so you would pool repositories. The submitted plan does not include that sentence.

Honest line: "I've read the abstract, the model in sections 2 and 3, and the data and results parts because I cite it. I haven't worked through how the mean value function is derived."

Basics to know (Hawkes process)

  • Rate: λ(t | history) = μ(t) + Σ over past faults φ(t − t_i). μ(t) is the background rate. φ is the extra rate each fault adds, and it fades with time.
  • In this paper μ(t) = v b e^(−bt), which falls as testing goes on, and a parametric kernel is φ(τ) = α f(τ), where α is the strength of self-excitation.
  • If you drop the history term, you get an NHPP back.
  • Cluster view (from the paper): background faults start clusters, and each fault can trigger more.
  • General fact, not from the paper: when f is a density, α is the average number of faults each fault triggers directly. α must be below 1, whatever the base rate, or a cluster's expected size is infinite.
  • The expected rate solves E[λ(t)] = μ(t) + ∫₀ᵗ φ(t − s) E[λ(s)] ds, a Volterra equation. The paper derives this equation (its equation 24, written for q(t) = m′(t)), solves it numerically on a time grid, and integrates q to get the expected count m(t).

Q5. What is the difference between an NHPP and a Hawkes process?

In an NHPP the rate depends only on time, so a fault found now does not change the rate later. In a Hawkes process each past fault adds a bump to the rate that dies away: λ(t) = μ(t) + Σ φ(t − t_i). If you remove the history term, you are back to an NHPP.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Take the exponential kernel α β e^(−βτ). What does α mean?"
    You: "β e^(−βτ) integrates to one, so α is the expected number of faults that one fault triggers directly, the branching ratio. Those faults can trigger more, so α has to stay below 1, or a cluster's expected size is infinite."
  2. Prof: "Then what is the expected size of one cluster?"
    You: "One starting fault, α children on average, α squared grandchildren, and so on. So the expected size is 1 + α + α² + ..., which is 1 over 1 − α. With α = 0.5 a cluster has two faults on average."
  3. Prof: "Now take the base rate v b e^(−bt) from Qiu's paper. What is the expected total number of faults over all time?"
    You: "The base rate integrates to v, so v background faults are expected, and each starts a cluster of expected size 1 over 1 − α. So the total is v over 1 − α. The paper's Volterra equation gives the same: integrating it over all time gives Q = v + αQ for the total Q."

Q7. How would you check whether fixes to agent code cluster at all?

First by looking: your paper plots a simple pseudo-intensity, one over the gap between successive faults, for one real dataset, and it looked spiky and clustered. Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "What is the weakness of one over the gap as a rate?"
    You: "It's very noisy. Even for a Poisson process with a constant rate the gaps are exponential, so one over the gap jumps around a lot, and its average isn't even finite because very short gaps are common. So spikes alone don't prove clustering, and I'd compare with the same plot for data simulated from a fitted NHPP."
  2. Prof: "What would you compare between the real and the simulated data, beyond the picture?"
    You: "How spread out the gaps are. For a constant-rate Poisson process the gaps are exponential, so their standard deviation equals their mean, and clustering makes it bigger. Because the rate also falls over time, I'd first rescale time with the fitted mean value function, so t_i becomes m(t_i), which turns a correct NHPP into a Poisson process with rate one, and then look at the gaps."
  3. Prof: "How would you test formally that those rescaled gaps are exponential?"
    You: "With a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test against the exponential with mean one, plus a check of whether short gaps tend to follow short gaps, which is what clustering does. But m was fitted on the same data, so the usual p-values come out too large. I'd get the reference values by simulating from the fitted model and refitting each time, a parametric bootstrap like your lab's."

pysrat, Python package for software reliability (Okamura, GitHub and PyPI 2026)

Basics to know (phase-type distribution)

  • A phase-type distribution is the time until a small continuous-time Markov chain reaches its end (absorbing) state. Each phase is an exponential wait.
  • Chaining and mixing exponential phases can approximate almost any positive distribution, which is why the lab uses them so much.
  • CF1 (canonical form 1): the phases are in a line, the rates increase along the line, and the chain may start in any phase. The README example uses CanonicalPhaseTypeNHPP(3).

I'd fit several families, such as exponential, gamma and log-normal, and compare them by AIC. But your group's papers on bagging and boosting say the model with the lowest AIC is not always the best predictor, so I would also check prediction on the later part of the data.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Say the gamma model's log-likelihood is 1.5 higher than the exponential's. Which has the lower AIC?"
    You: "Gamma has one more parameter, the shape, so its penalty is 2 higher, but its fit term is 2 times 1.5, so 3 lower. So its AIC is lower by 1 and AIC picks gamma, but only just."
  2. Prof: "Those two models are nested. What would a likelihood ratio test say?"
    You: "The exponential is the gamma with shape 1, so I compare twice the log-likelihood gap, which is 3, with a chi-squared with one degree of freedom. That's a p-value of about 0.08, so at the 5% level I wouldn't reject the exponential. So here the test and AIC disagree."

Basics to know (thinning)

  • To simulate an NHPP with rate λ(t) ≤ λ: draw a Poisson process with constant rate λ, then keep each point at time t with probability λ(t)/λ*. The kept points form an NHPP with rate λ(t).
  • The reverse use: delete each observed fault independently with probability q. What remains is an NHPP with rate (1 − q)λ(t). Wu et al.'s RIMS 2026 paper (paper 11 below) uses q = 1/n and then corrects by n/(n − 1).

Q15. What is thinning?

It is a way to get one point process from another by deleting points. If you keep each point at time t with probability λ(t)/λ, where λ is a higher constant rate, the kept points form an NHPP with rate λ(t). Your group's RIMS paper credits the idea to Lewis and Shedler.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why are the kept points again a Poisson process with rate λ(t)?"
    You: "In a short interval of length h around t, the constant-rate process has a point with chance about λ* h, and it's kept with chance λ(t) over λ*, so a kept point appears with chance about λ(t) h. Two kept points in one short interval stay negligible, and each point is kept or dropped on its own coin toss, so counts in separate intervals stay independent. Those are exactly the conditions for an NHPP with rate λ(t)."
  2. Prof: "Dr. Wu's RIMS paper uses it the other way round, deleting each observed fault with probability 1 over n. What does a model fitted to what's left estimate?"
    You: "By the same argument, the faults that are left form an NHPP with rate 1 minus 1 over n, times λ(t). So a model fitted to them estimates only n − 1 over n of the true mean value function, and that's why the paper multiplies the fitted curve by 1 plus 1 over n − 1, which is n over n − 1."
  3. Prof: "With 50 faults, how different is one resampled data set from the original?"
    You: "On average only one fault is deleted, and the chance that none is deleted is 49 over 50 to the power 50, about 0.36. So over a third of the resamples are just the original data, and the correction is small, 50 over 49, about 2%. In the boosting paper the weights change each round, so later rounds delete different faults, but I'd have to read further to know how much variety that gives."

Q25. What is the mean value function of a type-II model in this paper?

In general, a type-II model's mean value function grows without limit, while a type-I one, ωF(t), levels off at ω. In this paper it is M(t) = −ln(1 − F(t)), the cumulative hazard of F. It comes from assuming each failure is minimally repaired, so the software is only as good as just before the failure.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Take F exponential with rate b. What type-II model do you get?"
    You: "Minus the log of e to the minus bt is just bt, so M(t) = bt, a homogeneous Poisson process with constant rate b. There's no reliability growth at all, which makes sense: the exponential's hazard is constant, so putting the software back to how it was just before each failure leaves the rate at b forever."
  2. Prof: "So which F give reliability growth in a type-II model?"
    You: "Those whose hazard rate, the chance of failing in the next instant given no failure so far, falls over time, because the failure rate of the type-II model is exactly the hazard of F. A Pareto has hazard shape over t plus scale, which keeps falling, and that gives Musa-Okumoto. A gamma with shape above 1 has a rising hazard, so its type-II model would fail more and more often."
  3. Prof: "Take Musa-Okumoto with shape 20 and scale 10 hours. How many failures in the first 100 hours, and in the next 100?"
    You: "M(t) is 20 times the log of 1 plus t over 10. At 100 hours that's 20 log 11, about 48, and at 200 hours it's 20 log 21, about 61, so only about 13 in the second hundred hours. The rate at 100 hours is 20 over 110, about 0.18 failures per hour."

Q26. Why do some engineers feel uneasy about finite-failure models?

The paper points out that in a finite-failure model the time to the next failure has a defective distribution: there is a positive chance it never comes, because all the faults may be gone. So measures like the mean time between failures are infinite and not useful. Type-II models do not have that problem.

  1. Prof: "Compute that chance for Goel-Okumoto with ω = 100 and b = 0.05 per day, after 100 days of testing."

Poisson binomial distribution and reliability problems (Dohi, Okamura, Operations Research (ORSJ) 2022, in Japanese)

Status: in Japanese. PDF: 07_Lab_Papers/pdfs/LAB_Dohi_2022_Poisson_Binomial_Distribution_Reliability.pdf.

What it does, in plain words: if independent trials each have their own probability p_i, the number of successes follows the Poisson binomial distribution, with mean Σ p_i and variance Σ p_i(1 − p_i). The article reviews approximate and exact ways to compute it, then applies it to reliability problems: k-out-of-n systems with non-identical components, a group replacement policy, and software bug prediction. In the bug example, a machine-learning model gives each untested module a probability of holding a bug, and the Poisson binomial gives the distribution of how many buggy modules remain (shown on NASA's PC4 data with 2,017 modules).

Link to your proposal: a majority vote over N runs fails when at least (N + 1)/2 runs fail, which is a k-out-of-n system, and if runs from different settings failed independently with different chances, the number of failing runs on an input would be Poisson binomial.

Honest line: "It is in Japanese, so I've read the abstract and the bug prediction section in translation."

Basics to know (Poisson binomial)

  • X_i is 1 with probability p_i and 0 otherwise, independently. S = Σ X_i.
  • E[S] = Σ p_i and Var[S] = Σ p_i(1 − p_i). If every p_i = p, it is the binomial.
  • Exact probabilities by a simple recursion: add one trial at a time, P(S = k after trial i) = P(k after i − 1)(1 − p_i) + P(k − 1 after i − 1) p_i.
  • A k-out-of-n:F system fails when at least k of n components fail, so P(system fails) = P(S ≥ k).

Q27. What is a Poisson binomial distribution, and how is it different from your beta-binomial?

Both count failures among k trials. In the Poisson binomial each trial has its own fixed probability p_i, and the trials are independent. In my beta-binomial all runs share the same θ on an input, and θ changes from input to input, which is what makes runs fail together.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "How do their variances compare with a plain binomial with the same mean?"
    You: "The beta-binomial is wider: its variance is k p(1 − p) times 1 + (k − 1)ρ. With 10 runs, p = 0.1 and ρ = 0.2, that's 0.9 times 2.8, about 2.5, nearly three times the binomial's 0.9. The Poisson binomial goes the other way: it's never wider than the binomial with the same mean."
  2. Prof: "Why narrower? Different probabilities sound like more variation, not less."
    You: "Because the differences are fixed, not random. The sum of p_i(1 − p_i) equals k p̄(1 − p̄) minus the sum of (p_i − p̄) squared, so the more the p_i differ, the smaller the variance. In the extreme, five trials with p = 1 and five with p = 0 give exactly five every time."
  3. Prof: "So where in your experiment would a Poisson binomial actually appear?"
    You: "On one fixed input, with say three Claude Code runs and three Codex runs, the failure count is Poisson binomial if the runs are independent given the input, since each agent has its own θ_s(x). Across inputs these mix, and because the two θ's go up and down together, the mix is wider than binomial again; that's the covariance term in my plan."

Q28. Three runs from three different agents fail independently with probabilities p1, p2 and p3. What is the chance a 2-out-of-3 vote fails?

At least two must fail: p1p2(1 − p3) + p1p3(1 − p2) + p2p3(1 − p1) + p1p2p3, which simplifies to p1p2 + p1p3 + p2p3 − 2p1p2p3. With equal p it becomes 3p² − 2p³, the independent case in my plan. With a θ per input, I'd put θ1(x), θ2(x) and θ3(x) in place of the p's and average over inputs. Each pair term becomes p_a p_b plus the covariance, and the last term becomes E[θ1θ2θ3], a three-way term.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Put in p1 = 0.1, p2 = 0.2 and p3 = 0.3. Is the vote better than just using the best agent?"
    You: "The pair terms are 0.02, 0.03 and 0.06, which add to 0.11, and twice p1p2p3 is 0.012, so the vote fails with 0.098. That's barely below the best agent's 0.1, even with independent failures, so the two weaker agents buy almost nothing here."
  2. Prof: "When exactly do two weaker agents help?"
    You: "If both fail with probability q and the best agent with 0.1, the vote fails with 0.2q plus 0.8q squared, and that's below 0.1 only when q is below 0.25. So under independence the other two can be at most about two and a half times worse than the best agent."
  3. Prof: "And with dependence between the agents?"
    You: "Then each pair term gets its covariance added, p_a p_b plus Cov(θ_a, θ_b), and the three-way term changes too, so with positive dependence the vote usually fails more often and the bar gets stricter. Two weaker agents help only if they fail on different inputs from the best one, which is why RQ1 asks which change between runs lowers the covariance, not only which agent fails least."

Q32. In our RIMS paper we delete each fault with probability 1/n. Why multiply by 1 + 1/(n − 1) afterwards?

If you delete each point with probability 1/n, the kept points are an NHPP with rate (1 − 1/n)λ(t), so a model fitted to them underestimates the curve. Multiplying by 1 + 1/(n − 1), which is n/(n − 1), undoes that, because (1 − 1/n) times n/(n − 1) is exactly 1.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Which parameters does that correction actually fix? Does thinning change the shape of the curve?"
    You: "Only the level, ω; the shape stays the same. For a finite-failure model m(t) = ωF(t), the kept points have mean (1 − 1/n)ωF(t), which is the same model with ω scaled down and F unchanged. So the shape parameters need no correction, and multiplying the curve by n over n − 1 is the same as scaling ω back up."
  2. Prof: "In the boosting rounds the deletion probabilities differ from fault to fault. Does that still hold?"
    You: "Not exactly. If a fault at time t is deleted with probability p(t), what's left has rate λ(t) times 1 − p(t), so the shape changes a little as well as the level. Q34. Why is the best-fitting model not always the best predictor?

A model can fit the past well by bending to its noise, which is overfitting, and then predict the future badly. AIC punishes extra parameters, but it still judges fit to the past. That's why your papers test models on the later part of the data.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "But AIC is meant to estimate prediction error. Why doesn't it work here?"

Bootstrap aggregating: draw many bootstrap samples, fit the model on each, and average the results. It lowers the variance of an unstable estimator. Here, from the abstract, it is also used to rank the candidate models and average them instead of picking one.

Basics to know (overdispersion and the negative binomial)

  • In a Poisson count, the mean equals the variance.
  • A negative binomial is a Poisson whose mean is itself gamma-distributed. Its variance is mean + mean²/r, larger than the mean. That extra spread is overdispersion.
  • Same idea as your beta-binomial against the binomial: a random rate, or a random θ, adds spread.

Q46. Why would anyone use a negative binomial prior?

It is a Poisson whose mean is itself random, so it allows more spread than a Poisson, where the mean equals the variance. If you are unsure about the total number of bugs, that extra spread looks natural. It's the same reason I use a beta-binomial instead of a binomial.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said 'unsure about the total'. With a Poisson prior, what does finding many bugs tell you about how many remain?"
    You: "If the prior mean ω and the detection chances are fixed, surprisingly nothing.   2. Prof: "And with the negative binomial prior?"
    You: "Then the found and remaining bugs share one random, gamma-distributed mean, so they're linked: many found bugs suggest a large mean, and the forecast of remaining bugs goes up too. If the gamma has shape r, after finding x bugs the remaining count is negative binomial with shape r + x."

Basics to know (maximum product of spacings)

  • Sort the data x_(1) < ... < x_(n). The spacings are D_i = F(x_(i); θ) − F(x_(i−1); θ), with F = 0 before the first point and 1 after the last.
  • Choose θ to maximise Σ log D_i, the log of the product of spacings.
  • The idea: under the true θ, the values F(x) spread out like uniform points, so the spacings are even.

Q50. What is maximum product of spacings?

Sort the observations and put them through the fitted distribution function. The gaps between consecutive values are the spacings, and MPS picks the parameters that make the product of the spacings as large as possible, so the transformed points are spread as evenly as possible. It can still work where the likelihood behaves badly.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Give me a case where maximum likelihood breaks but MPS works."
    You: "The classic case is a three-parameter Weibull with shape below one. As its shift parameter moves up to the smallest observation, the density there goes to infinity, so the likelihood has no maximum. Spacings are probabilities, each at most one, so the MPS objective stays bounded. Dr. Wu's paper also cites J-shaped gamma and Weibull distributions, with shape below one, as cases where maximum likelihood may fail."
  2. Prof: "And when the likelihood behaves well, which is better?"
    You: "Then they're the same in large samples: MPS is consistent and as efficient as maximum likelihood, as Dr. Wu's paper notes. They differ in small samples, and which wins there depends on the model, so I'd check by simulation rather than assume."
  3. Prof: "Software data often have ties, two faults logged at the same moment. What happens to MPS?"
    You: "A tie gives a zero spacing, and the log of zero is minus infinity, so the objective breaks. The usual fix is to replace that zero spacing by the density at the tied point, which is the likelihood term. Dr. Wu's GitHub data had no tied timestamps, so they needed no adjustment."

Probabilistic least squares for NHPP models (Wu, Dohi, Zheng, Okamura, WSSE 2025)

Status: abstract only (paywalled).

What it does, in plain words: least squares is often used to fit SRMs, but it ignores the probability law of the fault-count process. The paper proposes a probabilistic least squares method for NHPP models that takes the Poisson nature into account, by minimising the error between the conditional fault-detection time distribution and its empirical distribution. On real datasets it predicted better than existing least squares methods, especially late in testing.

Honest line: "I've read the abstract only."

Basics to know (conditional distribution of fault times)

  • General NHPP fact: given N(T) = n, the n fault times are distributed like n sorted independent draws with distribution function m(t)/m(T).
  • For m(t) = ωF(t), that is F(t)/F(T), so ω cancels out.

Q54. What is wrong with plain least squares for an NHPP?

It fits the curve to the cumulative counts as if the errors were independent with equal variance. But in an NHPP the variance of N(t) equals m(t), so it grows over time, and the cumulative counts are strongly correlated. Your abstract says least squares is irrelevant to the probability law, and this method brings that law back in.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "How correlated are the cumulative counts? Give me the correlation of N(s) and N(t)."
    You: "For s before t, N(t) is N(s) plus an independent increment, so their covariance is the variance of N(s), which is m(s). The correlation is the square root of m(s) over m(t), so with m(s) = 50 and m(t) = 100 it's about 0.71."
  2. Prof: "So what would you fit instead?"
    You: "The counts in separate intervals, because in an NHPP those are independent, each weighted by one over its variance, which is its own mean. That's Pearson's chi-squared, observed minus expected, squared, over expected, summed, and its minimum behaves like maximum likelihood in large samples."
  3. Prof: "Then why does anyone still use least squares?"
    You: "It's simple, needs no distribution and works in a spreadsheet, and the best fit isn't always the best prediction anyway. Your IOCMA paper found PLS gave the lowest prediction error in nine of 24 cases, and your grouped-data paper found that neither ordinary least squares nor maximum likelihood always predicted best. So I'd choose the fitting method by held-out prediction, not by theory alone."

Q55. What is the "conditional fault-detection time distribution"?

As I understand it, if n faults were found by time T in an NHPP with m(t) = ωF(t), their times behave like n sorted independent draws from F(t)/F(T). So you can compare that curve with the empirical distribution of the observed times, and ω drops out. Dr. Wu's IOCMA paper restates the method with the last fault time: PLS fits the shape of F by matching F(t_i)/F(t_n) to i/n, then estimates ω by maximum likelihood.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why is that true? Show me quickly."
    You: "The density of faults at exactly t_1 to t_n and no others by T is the product of the λ(t_i) times e^(−m(T)). Dividing by the chance of n faults, m(T)^n e^(−m(T)) over n factorial, leaves n factorial times the product of λ(t_i) over m(T). That's the density of n sorted independent draws with density λ(t)/m(T), whose distribution function is m(t)/m(T)."
  2. Prof: "And if only daily counts are recorded, not exact times?"
    You: "The same property gives the answer: given n faults by T, the daily counts are multinomial, with each day's probability equal to that day's increase in m divided by m(T). With m = ωF, ω still cancels, so the shape of F can be fitted from the daily shares alone."
  3. Prof: "Does that property hold for our Hawkes models too?"
    You: "No. In a Hawkes process the rate at each fault depends on the earlier faults, so the product of rates doesn't split into independent draws from one density, and given n the times aren't independent. So methods built on this property, like PLS or Dr. Wu's MPS, don't carry over directly; there I'd use the full likelihood, or simulation."

Performance comparison of reliability estimation algorithms (Yano, Dohi, Okamura, IEEE Computer 2024)

Status: the proposal research read its method and conclusion (GUIDE_1 P5, 07_Lab_Papers/GUIDE_1_lab_and_cited_papers.md). The Hiroshima University repository copy is offline now.

What it does, in plain words: automated reliability assessment tools need optimisation algorithms to estimate the parameters of SRMs. The article proposes the Monte Carlo EM algorithm as another option and compares estimation algorithms in extensive numerical experiments.

Link to your proposal: pysrat, which you plan to use, estimates its models with EM.

Basics to know (EM algorithm)

  • E step: with the current parameters, compute the expected value of the hidden data, for example where inside an interval each grouped fault happened, or how many faults are still undetected.
  • M step: maximise the likelihood with those expected values filled in. Repeat until it stops changing.
  • The likelihood never goes down from one round to the next. It can be slow, and each model needs its own E and M steps.

Q56. Why do tools like pysrat use EM?

Grouped data and the faults not yet found make the likelihood awkward to maximise directly. EM treats the missing parts as hidden, fills them in by their expected values, maximises, and repeats. The likelihood never goes down between rounds, so it converges steadily, though it can be slow.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Walk me through one EM round for Goel-Okumoto with fault times up to T."
    You: "The hidden part is the faults not yet found. E step: their expected number is ω e^(−bT), and since the exponential has no memory, each is expected at T + 1/b. M step: the new ω is n plus that expected number, and the new b is the new ω divided by the expected total time, the sum of the observed times plus ω e^(−bT) times (T + 1/b)."
  2. Prof: "EM is known to be slow. When is it slowest?"
    You: "Its speed is set by the share of the information that's missing. For Goel-Okumoto it depends only on the share of faults found: with about 95% found, each round removes roughly half the remaining error, but with only half found it removes only about one percent. pysrat's README even warns that its Poisson-regression loop runs one EM step per iteration, so a few thousand iterations are normal."
  3. Prof: "So how do you know it has really converged and not just slowed down?"
    You: "Small changes per round don't prove it, because a slow EM takes small steps even far from the optimum. I'd check that the slope of the log-likelihood is near zero, start from several points, and, if speed matters, finish with a few Newton steps or use an accelerator such as SQUAREM. The Yano paper compares estimation algorithms in numerical experiments, but I've only read its abstract, so I don't know its conclusion."

Q57. What is Monte Carlo EM?

It is EM where the expectation step is too hard to compute exactly, so you estimate it by simulation. It costs more time, but as I understand it, it can handle models where the exact E step is not available. From the abstract, the paper proposes Monte Carlo EM as another option and compares the algorithms in numerical experiments; I haven't read its conclusion.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the E step is estimated by simulation. Concretely, what would you simulate for an NHPP model fitted to grouped data?"
    You: "The hidden parts are the exact fault times inside each interval and the faults not found yet. So for each interval I'd draw its faults from the fault-time distribution cut to that interval, add a Poisson number of unseen faults with mean ω times 1 minus F(T), placed after T, and average what the M step needs, the total count and the sum of the times, over many draws. For Goel-Okumoto those averages have exact formulas, so I'd use it to check my simulation."
  2. Prof: "Your averages are random now. Does the likelihood still never go down?"
    You: "Not for sure. The simulated E step is noisy, so a round can step down a little, and with a fixed number of draws the estimates never settle but keep jittering around the maximum. That's why, since Wei and Tanner proposed the method in 1990, people use few draws while the steps are big and more as it gets close."
  3. Prof: "How would you decide when to add draws, and when to stop?"
    You: "The noise in a simulated average falls like one over the square root of the number of draws, so halving it costs four times as many. Booth and Hobert's rule adds draws when the old estimate still lies inside a confidence region around the new one, because then the step is mostly noise, and it stops only after the change stays small for about three rounds in a row. I haven't implemented it, so I'd try it first on Goel-Okumoto, where exact EM gives the answer to compare with."

Infinite-failure SRMs based on non-homogeneous Markov processes (Qiu, Li, Dohi, Okamura, Software Quality Journal 2026)

Status: the 2023 preprint is on disk. PDF: 07_Lab_Papers/pdfs/LAB_Qiu_2026_Infinite_Failure_SRMs_NHMP.pdf.

What it does, in plain words: the paper models software faults with non-homogeneous Markov processes (NHMPs), where the fault-detection rate can depend on both time and the number of faults found so far. It builds 22 new NHMP-based models (11 generalized binomial, 11 generalized Polya), each from one of 11 baseline intensity functions, as extensions of infinite-failure NHPP models, and tests them on 8 time-domain and 8 grouped datasets against NHPP models. They fit better in many cases but predicted better in only a limited number of cases.

Honest line: "I've read the preprint's abstract, introduction and section 3."

Q58. In an NHPP the mean equals the variance. Why does this paper see that as a problem?

Because then the variance keeps growing with the mean. The paper says our uncertainty about the number of faults should instead shrink as testing goes on, so that property is questionable. A Markov model whose rate also depends on the faults found can break that link.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said a Markov model can break that link. Which of the two models in the paper actually makes the uncertainty shrink?"
    You: "The generalized binomial one. Its count is binomial with γ trials and success chance 1 − A(t), so the variance is the mean times A, always below the mean. In the paper's new version A goes to zero, so the mean settles at γ and the variance goes to zero. The Polya one goes the other way: its variance is the mean times e^(αΛ(t)), so the spread grows faster than the mean."
  2. Prof: "A variance that goes to zero means the model is sure of the final count. What is it really assuming?"
    You: "That there's a fixed pool of γ faults, found one by one. If β over α is a whole number, each remaining fault is found at rate α times κ(t), like Jelinski-Moranda with a rate that changes over time, and that's realistic only if fixes never add new faults. The paper itself says imperfect debugging is common, and it notes that this version's mean stays bounded, so it can't really be called an infinite-failure model."
  3. Prof: "So whether the pool is fixed is a question for the data. How would you check whether the spread is smaller or larger than Poisson?"
    You: "One project gives me only one path, so I can't see the spread of the final count directly. I'd compare the models by likelihood and AIC, as the paper does, and with grouped data I'd check whether the counts per interval scatter around the fitted NHPP curve more or less than Poisson allows. My RQ1 is easier here: every input gives its own count of failing runs out of k, so extra spread shows up directly across inputs."

Q59. What is a generalized Polya process?

It is a pure birth process where, with n faults found, the rate at time t is (β + αn)κ(t), so each fault found adds ακ(t) to the rate. The count then has a negative binomial distribution. It is a bit like self-excitation, but unlike a Hawkes kernel, that extra part does not depend on how long ago the fault was found.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why would finding a fault make the next one more likely? Fixing it should do the opposite."
    You: "One reading is learning, not contagion. The Polya process is the same as a Poisson process whose overall level is unknown and gamma distributed, so each fault found is evidence that the level is high, and the predicted rate goes up. The paper also says the GPP extends Okamura and Dohi's mixed Poisson model."
  2. Prof: "Show me that a gamma level gives exactly β + αn."
    You: "Let the rate be Z times M′(t), with Z gamma with shape β over α and rate 1, and M(t) = e^(αΛ(t)) − 1. After n faults by time t, Bayes' rule makes Z gamma with shape β over α plus n and rate 1 + M(t), so the expected rate is that shape over that rate, times M′(t). Since M′ is ακ(t) e^(αΛ(t)) and 1 + M is e^(αΛ(t)), the exponentials cancel, leaving β over α plus n, times ακ(t), which is (β + αn)κ(t)."
  3. Prof: "So the rate depends only on the count n. How could data tell that apart from a Hawkes process?"
    You: "Both are self-exciting in the broad sense, and your QRS abstract says the existing models can be unified that way. The difference is fading: in the Polya process the rate depends on how many faults have been found but not on when, while in a Hawkes process the boost fades, so faults bunch up right after earlier ones. I'd fit both and compare them by AIC and by held-out prediction, and the QRS paper did compare Hawkes models with NHMP models and found the Hawkes ones generally better."

Q60. What is the difference between a generalized binomial process and a generalized Polya process?

From the abstract, the binomial one is an inverse death process: you can think of a pool of faults that are found one by one. The Polya one is a birth process. In both, the rate depends on time and on how many faults have been found. The full paper is paywalled for me, but from Qiu et al.'s preprint, which restates both models, in the Polya one each fault found raises the rate.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said a pool of faults. How big is the pool, and what happens when it's empty?"
    You: "As Qiu et al.'s preprint restates it, the rate is (β − αn)κ(t), so it falls with each fault found, and the process stops at n equal to β over α, rounded up.   2. Prof: "Compute: a pool of 100 faults, with 60% expected to be found by now. Mean and standard deviation of the count, against an NHPP with the same mean?"
    You: "The binomial mean is 100 times 0.6, which is 60, and the variance is 60 times 0.4, which is 24, so the standard deviation is about 4.9. An NHPP with mean 60 has variance 60, so a standard deviation of about 7.7. The binomial is more certain because the pool it draws from is fixed."
  3. Prof: "And what does that mean for the number of faults still remaining?"
    You: "In the binomial model, once I've seen 60, the faults left in the pool are exactly 100 minus 60, which is 40, with no spread apart from not knowing the parameters. Q61. What is the software release problem?

Deciding when to stop testing and release: testing longer costs money, but releasing early leaves more faults to fail in the field. You pick the time that minimises the expected total cost. My best number of runs N has the same shape: each run costs money, and each shared failure costs more.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Write that cost down for Goel-Okumoto and find the best release time."
    You: "Say fixing a fault costs c1 in testing and c2 in the field, with c2 larger, and testing costs c3 per day. The expected total is c1 m(T) plus c2 times ω minus m(T), plus c3 T, and its slope is zero when λ(T) equals c3 over c2 minus c1, so I stop when the rate of finding faults falls to that level. For Goel-Okumoto, λ(T) = ωb e^(−bT), so T = (1/b) log of ωb(c2 − c1) over c3, or zero if that's negative."

    You: "Then I'd release at the later of the two times. Q62. What is cross-validation for choosing a bandwidth?

You leave out part of the data, fit with a given bandwidth, check how well it explains the left-out part, and repeat. The bandwidth with the smallest error, or the highest held-out log-likelihood, wins. It picks the smoothness from the data instead of by eye.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the smallest error. Error against what? We never observe the true fault rate."
    You: "Against the true rate, through a trick that needs only the data. The integrated squared error is the integral of the estimate squared, minus twice the integral of the estimate times the true rate, plus a term without h, and for a Poisson process that middle integral can be estimated by summing the leave-one-out estimate at each fault time. So least-squares cross-validation minimises the integral of the estimate squared minus twice that sum, which is the form in your IOCMA note."
  2. Prof: "Why leave one out? What happens if you keep the fault in?"
    You: "Then the estimate at each fault includes that fault's own bump, K(0) over h, which grows without limit as h shrinks. So the criterion would always reward a smaller bandwidth, a spike on every fault, and run off to h = 0. Leaving the fault out removes that self-credit."
  3. Prof: "So leaving one out stops least squares running off to zero. The note also uses log-likelihood cross-validation. Does that one have a trap of its own?"
    You: "It has the same form as the NHPP log-likelihood, the sum of log rates at the faults minus the integral, which I like because the NHPP side is fitted the same way. Its trap is a lone fault: with a kernel that is zero beyond h, like the uniform or triangular, a fault with no neighbour within h gets a leave-one-out rate of zero, and log zero is minus infinity, so one isolated fault forces a large bandwidth. Least squares has no such trap but is known to give quite variable bandwidths, so I'd run both and look closely where they disagree."

Multi-kernel local linear regression for reliability prediction (Bai, Dohi, Zheng, Okamura, DASC 2025)

What it does, in plain words: NHPP models rest on strong assumptions, while kernel regression is more flexible, and the local linear estimator works better near the boundaries of the data. Basics to know (Nadaraya-Watson and local linear)

  • Nadaraya-Watson: the value at a point is a weighted average of nearby observed values, with weights from the kernel.
  • Local linear: fit a weighted straight line around the point instead of a flat average.
  • At the edge of the data, a flat average only sees points on one side and is biased. The local linear fit removes most of that bias.

Q64. Why does behaviour near the boundary matter for prediction?

Prediction happens at the end of the data, which is a boundary. A plain kernel average there only sees points on one side, so it is biased. The local linear estimator fits a small straight line instead of a flat average, which removes most of that bias.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said it's biased. Which way, for a cumulative fault curve that's still rising?"
    You: "Downward. At the last point, a flat average uses only earlier points, which are all lower on a rising curve, so it lags behind the true value. The bias is about the slope times the average distance back, which grows in proportion to h, while in the middle of the data the two sides cancel and the bias is only of order h squared."
  2. Prof: "Put a number on it: slope 2 faults per day, Gaussian kernel, bandwidth 5 days."
    You: "With points spread evenly before the end, the Gaussian weights form half a bell curve, whose mean distance is h times the square root of 2 over π, about 0.8h, so 4 days here. So the flat average sits about 2 times 4, which is 8 faults, below the true value at the last point."
  3. Prof: "And why does fitting a local straight line remove that?"
    You: "Because a weighted straight-line fit reproduces any straight-line trend exactly, wherever the points sit, so the slope-times-distance part cancels even when all the points are on one side. What's left comes from the curve's bending, of order h squared, as in the middle. In my example it gives the true value at the last point instead of 8 faults short."

Q65. Several kernels with weights means more parameters. How do you avoid overfitting?

Choosing the weights and the bandwidth by cross-validation, on points left out of the fit, keeps it in check. With very little data it can still overfit, so I'd want to see the results by testing phase, as the abstract reports for the early phase.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "But you tune the bandwidth and several weights on the same cross-validation score. Can't you overfit the cross-validation itself?"
    You: "Yes. The score is itself noisy, and the more things I tune on it, the more the best score is partly luck, so it looks better than the method will really predict. The clean check is to judge the final choice on data never used for tuning, like the later part of the data that your papers hold out for prediction."
  2. Prof: "You said 'the more things I tune'. How would you even count the parameters of a kernel smoother?"
    You: "A kernel smoother is linear in the data: the fitted values are a matrix S times the observed values.   3. Prof: "Roughly, then: 50 equally spaced points, Gaussian kernel, bandwidth equal to the spacing."
    You: "In the middle, a point's weight on itself is the kernel's peak, about 0.4 for the Gaussian, divided by h in units of the spacing, because the weights over all the points add up to about h.     You: "In standard theory, the Gaussian needs only about 5% more data than the best kernel, the Epanechnikov, for the same accuracy, and the triangular only about 1.5% more.   2. Prof: "That theory is for the middle of the data. We predict at the end and beyond. Does it still hold there?"
    You: "Not fully, because it's about squared error averaged over the middle. At the end, the shape decides how far back the estimate reaches: a kernel that is zero beyond h, like the triangular, ignores everything older than one bandwidth. And more than one bandwidth past the last point it has no data at all, so a plain kernel average isn't even defined there, while a Gaussian one always is."
  3. Prof: "You said a kernel with a cut-off can run out of data. Late in testing the gaps between faults get long. What would you do about the bandwidth then?"
    You: "Let it grow where faults are sparse. The adaptive rules in the IOCMA note scale each bandwidth by one over the square root of the estimated rate, so where the rate is a quarter of the typical level, the bandwidth doubles. But the note found the fixed bandwidth more stable when the history is sparse, because the adaptive rule leans on rate estimates that are themselves noisy."

Q68. Why is long-term prediction hard for kernel methods?

As I understand it, a kernel estimate only uses data near the point you predict, and far in the future there is no data nearby. Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "How does that step-by-step prediction work?"
    You: "It first estimates the rate at each past fault with kernels.   2. Prof: "So each predicted fault is put back as if it were real. What does that do?"
    You: "Errors build up: if the first predicted fault comes too early, the recent rate looks higher, so the next one comes early too, and the note itself says recursive extrapolation can propagate errors.   3. Prof: "How would you get an interval, then?"
    You: "By simulating: make the integral of the predicted rate reach a random target drawn from an exponential with mean one, so the note's rule is the same thing with the target replaced by its mean. Repeating whole paths a thousand times gives a band. I haven't tried it, and I'd check its coverage by cutting the past data at earlier points."

Q72. How is pysrat related to SRATS?

Both fit NHPP growth models by maximum likelihood with EM. The 11 models listed as SRATS models in Dr. Wu's RIMS paper are the same families pysrat has, and pysrat adds CF1, metrics-based models and a Python interface. Your lab's tools page now lists SRATS as unsupported.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said pysrat adds metrics-based models. What can they do that the SRATS models can't?"
    You: "In the SRATS models the detection curve depends only on time. In pysrat's metrics models, each testing period's chance of finding a remaining fault depends on that period's metrics through a link function such as the logit, so the curve can climb faster in a period with more testing."
  2. Prof: "Write that model down."
    You: "Let p_k be the chance that a remaining fault is found in period k, with logit p_k = β′x_k for that period's metrics x_k. A fault still hidden after n periods has escaped all of them, so the expected number found by then is ω times 1 minus the product of (1 − p_k) for k up to n."
  3. Prof: "pysrat also offers probit and cloglog links. Why would anyone prefer cloglog?"
    You: "Because it comes from continuous time: if a remaining fault is found at a constant hazard h within a period of length Δ, then p = 1 − e^(−hΔ), so cloglog p = log h + log Δ, and covariates in log h give a proportional hazards model. That's why the README says an offset turns the cloglog model into a Cox model. When p is small the two links nearly agree, for example −2.94 for the logit and −2.97 for the cloglog at p = 0.05."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

Continuous-state software reliability modelling (Dohi, Jin, Zheng, Okamura, Bentham book chapter 2018)

Status: abstract only (paywalled chapter).

What it does, in plain words: for very large software, the fault-counting process in testing can be treated approximately as a continuous-state process. The chapter summarises SRMs based on time-nonhomogeneous Gaussian and gamma processes and compares them with geometric Brownian motion and NHPP models. On real project data, the gamma-process models often fit and predicted better.

Honest line: "I've read the abstract only."

Q82. Why treat a fault count as a continuous quantity?

When the code base is very large and the counts are big, a count in the thousands behaves almost like a continuous number, and continuous processes can be easier to handle. It is the same idea as using a normal approximation for a large binomial.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "How large is large? When would that approximation fail for fault counts?"
    You: "A common rule of thumb is an expected count of about 10 or more. A Poisson's skewness is one over the square root of its mean, about 0.32 at a mean of 10 and 0.1 at 100, so it gets close to a symmetric normal. It fails late in testing, when a week brings zero, one or two faults."
  2. Prof: "But the cumulative count is in the thousands even late in testing. Doesn't that save it?"
    You: "Not really, because a Gaussian or gamma process model with independent increments is fitted on the increments, the faults found in each interval, and those are small late in testing however large the total is. A total going from 5,030 to 5,032 is still two separate events, not a smooth rise."
  3. Prof: "Put a number on it: a Gaussian increment with mean 2 and variance 2, as a Poisson would have. What goes wrong?"
    You: "The standard deviation is the square root of 2, about 1.41, so zero is only 1.41 standard deviations below the mean, and the normal puts about 8% of its probability on a negative count, which is impossible. With a mean of 100 and variance 100, zero is 10 standard deviations away, so the problem disappears."

Q83. Why a gamma process rather than a Gaussian one?

In general, a gamma process only goes up, because its increments are positive, which suits a cumulative count, while a Gaussian process can go down, which a fault count never does. I've only read the chapter's abstract, which says the gamma-process models often fit and predicted better, so I don't know how the chapter itself argues the choice.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Define a gamma process."
    You: "It starts at zero, has independent increments, and the increment over any interval is gamma distributed, with shape equal to how much a shape function v(t) grows over that interval and a fixed scale c. So its mean at time t is v(t) times c, and by choosing v(t) it can follow any growth curve, like a mean value function."
  2. Prof: "And its variance, compared with an NHPP's?"
    You: "The variance is v(t) times c squared, so the variance divided by the mean is c. An NHPP forces that ratio to be 1; the gamma process estimates it, so c above 1 allows more spread than a Poisson and below 1 less, though the ratio stays the same over time."
  3. Prof: "So with c = 1 it's just an NHPP?"
    You: "Only in mean and variance; the shapes differ, most of all near zero. A gamma increment is never exactly zero, so the closest thing to no failure next week is a rise of less than one: with an expected 0.5 faults and c = 1 that's about 84%, while a Poisson with the same mean gives about 61% for zero failures. I don't know how the chapter defines reliability for these models, so I'd want to read that part."
