Chapter 30 of 62

Stochastic processes and reliability

When and how long: Days 3, 4, 6 and 7.

This is the core mathematics of the Dependable Systems Laboratory: Poisson processes and NHPP models (software reliability growth), Markov chains (availability, rejuvenation), queues, and reliability of systems. If the professors ask a math question that is not about your plan, it will most likely come from here.

Part 1 is a list of statements you should be able to say out loud. Part 2 has 25 problems with full solutions. Your earlier 01_reliability_and_probability_primer.md sections 5 and 6 give the short version; this file goes further.

How the answers were checked. Every numeric answer was recomputed on 12 Nov 2026 with Python (numpy, scipy), and again on 13 Nov 2026. The stationary distributions, the absorbing chain and the CTMC of R12 were checked with linear algebra, the queue numbers by direct computation, the two MLE fits and the optimal times in R16 and R24 with a numerical optimiser, and R9 and R25 with simulation. Items marked [CHECK] are ones I am not fully sure of.

Notation: lambda (rate), mu (repair or service rate), omega (expected total faults), exp(x) = e^x, ln = natural log.

Part 1. Statements to say out loud

1.1 Poisson process (rate lambda)

  • Definition. N(0) = 0. Counts in disjoint intervals are independent. N(s + t) - N(s) ~ Poisson(lambda t): P(k events) = e^(-lambda t) (lambda t)^k / k!.
  • Equivalent view. The times between events are independent Exponential(lambda), mean 1/lambda. The time of the n-th event is Gamma(n, lambda), mean n/lambda.
  • Mean and variance of N(t) are both lambda t.
  • Superposition. Independent Poisson processes with rates lambda_1 and lambda_2 add up to a Poisson process with rate lambda_1 + lambda_2.
  • Thinning. Keep each event with probability q, independently. The kept events form a Poisson process with rate q lambda, independent of the dropped ones.
  • Conditional uniformity. Given N(t) = n, the n event times are like n independent Uniform(0, t) points, sorted.
  • Say it: "A Poisson process counts events that happen independently at a constant rate. Counts in disjoint intervals are independent and Poisson, and the gaps are exponential."

1.2 NHPP (non-homogeneous Poisson process)

  • Same as above, but the rate changes with time: intensity lambda(t), mean value function m(t) = integral_0^t lambda(u) du. N(t) ~ Poisson(m(t)), and N(t + x) - N(t) ~ Poisson(m(t + x) - m(t)).
  • Software reliability over the next x time units after t: R(x | t) = exp(-[m(t + x) - m(t)]).
  • Finite-failure (type I) models: m(t) = omega F(t), F a fault-detection time distribution. It levels off at omega. Expected remaining faults: omega - m(t).
    • Goel-Okumoto: m(t) = omega (1 - e^(-bt)), lambda(t) = omega b e^(-bt).
    • Delayed S-shaped (Yamada): m(t) = omega [1 - (1 + bt) e^(-bt)].
  • Infinite-failure (type II) models: m(t) grows without limit. Musa-Okumoto (logarithmic Poisson): m(t) = (1/theta) ln(1 + lambda_0 theta t).
  • The type I and type II names go back to Kuo and Yang (JASA, 1996). The lab uses them, for example Li, Dohi and Okamura, "Are Infinite-Failure NHPP-Based Software Reliability Models Useful?" (Software, 2023).
  • Log-likelihood, failure-time data t_1 < ... < t_n observed up to T: log L = sum_i log lambda(t_i) - m(T).
  • Log-likelihood, grouped data (x_i failures in (s_{i-1}, s_i]): log L = sum_i x_i log[m(s_i) - m(s_{i-1})] - m(s_k) - sum_i log(x_i!).
  • Say it: "An NHPP is a Poisson process whose rate changes with time. In a reliability growth model the rate falls as faults are found and fixed, and m(t) is the expected number of faults found by time t."

1.3 Discrete-time Markov chain (DTMC)

  • Markov property: the next state depends only on the current state. Transition matrix P, rows sum to 1, P_ij = P(next = j | now = i).
  • n-step transitions: P^n (Chapman-Kolmogorov). The distribution after n steps is pi_0 P^n (row vector).
  • Stationary distribution: pi P = pi, sum pi_i = 1. For a finite, irreducible, aperiodic chain it is unique, and P^n converges to it from any start.
  • Absorbing chains: with Q the transient-to-transient block, N = (I - Q)^(-1) gives expected visits; row sums of N are expected steps to absorption.
  • Say it: "A Markov chain forgets its past: only the current state matters. The stationary distribution solves pi P = pi and gives the long-run share of time in each state."

1.4 Continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC)

  • Generator Q: q_ij >= 0 is the rate from i to j (i != j), and q_ii = -sum_{j != i} q_ij, so each row sums to 0. The chain stays in state i for an Exponential(-q_ii) time.
  • Transition probabilities: P(t) = e^(Qt) = I + Qt + (Qt)^2/2! + ...
  • Kolmogorov equations: forward P'(t) = P(t) Q, backward P'(t) = Q P(t), with P(0) = I.
  • Steady state: pi Q = 0, sum pi_i = 1. Balance: flow out of a state = flow into it.
  • Birth-death chain: only moves n -> n + 1 (rate lambda_n) and n -> n - 1 (rate mu_n). pi_n = pi_0 prod_{i=1}^{n} lambda_{i-1} / mu_i.
  • Say it: "A CTMC jumps between states with exponential holding times. The generator has the rates off the diagonal and rows that sum to zero. The steady state solves pi Q = 0."

1.5 Queues

  • Kendall notation A/S/c: arrival process / service distribution / number of servers. M = Markovian (Poisson arrivals or exponential service), G = general.
  • Little's law: L = lambda W (average number in system = arrival rate times average time in system). It holds for almost any queue.
  • M/M/1 (rho = lambda/mu < 1): P(N = n) = (1 - rho) rho^n, L = rho/(1 - rho), W = 1/(mu - lambda), Lq = rho^2/(1 - rho), Wq = rho/(mu - lambda).
  • M/M/infinity and M/G/infinity: every arrival gets its own server. In steady state N ~ Poisson(lambda E[S]), which depends on the service distribution only through its mean. Starting empty, N(t) ~ Poisson(lambda integral_0^t (1 - G(u)) du), and the number of departures by t is Poisson(lambda integral_0^t G(u) du).
  • Link to your plan. Faults are customers, the time until a fault is found is the service time, and a detection is a departure. If all faults arrive at time 0 you get a finite-fault NHPP, m(t) = omega G(t). If they keep arriving you get an infinite-fault model. Dohi, Osaki and Trivedi [12] used this view to unify the two. Your field model m(t) = sum_j omega_j G(t - tau_j) is the same idea with arrivals at merge times (see 05_math_inside_my_research_plan.md D14).

1.6 Renewal processes

  • Lifetimes X_1, X_2, ... i.i.d. with mean mu; each failed item is replaced at once. N(t) = number of renewals by t.
  • Elementary renewal theorem: E[N(t)] / t -> 1/mu.
  • Renewal-reward theorem: long-run cost per unit time = E[cost per cycle] / E[cycle length]. This is how optimal maintenance and rejuvenation times are found.
  • A Poisson process is the renewal process with exponential lifetimes.

1.7 Reliability of one component

  • Lifetime T. F(t) = P(T <= t). Reliability R(t) = P(T > t) = 1 - F(t). Density f = F'.
  • Hazard rate h(t) = f(t) / R(t): the failure rate at t, given survival to t. R(t) = exp(-integral_0^t h(u) du).
  • MTTF = E[T] = integral_0^infinity R(t) dt.
  • Exponential: h = lambda constant (memoryless), MTTF = 1/lambda.
  • Weibull: R(t) = exp(-(t/eta)^beta), h(t) = (beta/eta)(t/eta)^(beta - 1), MTTF = eta Gamma(1 + 1/beta). beta < 1: falling hazard (infant mortality). beta = 1: exponential. beta > 1: rising hazard (wear-out).
  • Bathtub curve: the hazard falls early (burn-in), stays flat (random failures), and rises late (wear-out). Software has no wear-out in the physical sense, but long-running processes show "aging" (memory leaks, fragmentation), which is what rejuvenation targets.

1.8 Systems

  • Series (all must work): R = prod R_i. With exponential parts, the system is exponential with rate sum lambda_i.
  • Parallel (one is enough): R = 1 - prod (1 - R_i).
  • k-out-of-n (at least k of n identical, independent parts work): R = sum_{j=k}^{n} C(n, j) R^j (1 - R)^(n - j). 2-out-of-3: 3R^2 - 2R^3.
  • These formulas assume independent failures. Common-cause failures make parallel systems much worse (the same point as Eckhardt and Lee).

1.9 Availability and rejuvenation

  • Availability = share of time the system works. Steady state: A = MTTF / (MTTF + MTTR). For a 2-state CTMC with failure rate lambda and repair rate mu: A = mu / (lambda + mu).
  • Software rejuvenation: restart or clean a running system before it ages into failure. Planned downtime is short and cheap. Unplanned failure downtime is long. The question is when to rejuvenate. Models: CTMC with an aging state (Huang et al. 1995), and semi-Markov or renewal models that give an optimal rejuvenation time (Dohi's group, for example Dohi, Goseva-Popstojanova and Trivedi, 2000). Prof. Zheng's own rejuvenation papers use Markov regenerative processes (Stochastic Models, 2022) and non-Markovian models solved by phase expansion (IEICE Transactions, 2020).
  • Checkpointing: save state regularly so a failure loses less work. Too often wastes time on saving, too rarely loses more work on failure, so there is an optimal interval.
  • Say it: "Rejuvenation trades a short planned outage for a lower chance of a long unplanned one. With a renewal-reward argument you can find the restart time that maximises availability."

Part 2. 25 problems with full solutions

Cover the solution, say the plan out loud, solve on paper, then check.

R1. Poisson counts

Failures arrive as a Poisson process with rate 3 per hour. (a) P(exactly 4 failures in 2 hours)? (b) Mean and variance of the count in 2 hours? (c) P(no failure in 20 minutes)?

Solution. (a) N(2) ~ Poisson(6). P(N = 4) = e^(-6) 6^4 / 4! = e^(-6) * 1296 / 24 = 54 e^(-6) = 0.134. (b) Both 6. (c) 20 minutes = 1/3 hour, so N ~ Poisson(1). P(0) = e^(-1) = 0.368.

Say it. "In 2 hours the count is Poisson with mean 6, so four failures has probability e to the minus 6, times 6 to the 4, over 4 factorial, about 0.13."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "In part (c) you got e to the minus 1 for 20 minutes. How short must the window be for a 90 percent chance of no failure?"
    You: "No failure in t hours has probability e to the minus 3t. Setting that to 0.9 gives t equals ln of 1 over 0.9, divided by 3, which is about 0.035 hours. So roughly 2 minutes."
  2. Prof: "That 2 minutes assumes the rate is exactly 3. Suppose the 3 came from seeing 12 failures in 4 hours. How sure are you of the rate?"
    You: "The count 12 is Poisson, so its standard deviation is about the square root of 12, about 3.5. Dividing by 4 hours, the rate is 3 with a standard error of about 0.87 per hour, so a rough 95 percent interval is 1.3 to 4.7 per hour. That's wide."
  3. Prof: "So what would you report for the 90 percent window?"
    You: "The window is ln of 1 over 0.9, about 0.105, divided by lambda, in hours. Across that rate interval it runs from about 1.3 to about 4.9 minutes, so I'd report that range, not just 2 minutes. I think the lab's papers call this epistemic uncertainty propagation: the parameter is treated as a random variable, and its uncertainty is carried into the measure you report."

R2. Waiting for the third failure

Same process (rate 3 per hour). T_3 = time of the third failure. Find P(T_3 <= 1 hour) and E[T_3].

Solution. The third failure happens by time 1 exactly when there are at least 3 failures in [0, 1]. N(1) ~ Poisson(3). P(T_3 <= 1) = 1 - P(N(1) <= 2) = 1 - e^(-3)(1 + 3 + 9/2) = 1 - 8.5 e^(-3) = 0.577. T_3 is the sum of 3 independent Exponential(3) gaps, so E[T_3] = 3 * (1/3) = 1 hour (it is Gamma(3, 3)).

Check. scipy's Gamma(3, scale 1/3) cdf at 1 gives 0.5768.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Your probability 0.577 is above one half, but the mean is exactly 1 hour. So is the median of T_3 above or below 1 hour?"
    You: "Below, because more than half the probability is already used up by 1 hour. The Gamma is skewed to the right, so a few long waits pull the mean up. The median is about 0.89 hours."
  2. Prof: "How would you check that 0.89 with just a calculator?"
    You: "With the same link to the count: T_3 is at most t exactly when N(t) is at least 3, so I try values. At t = 0.9, 3t is 2.7, and e to the minus 2.7 times (1 + 2.7 + 3.645) is about 0.49. So P of T_3 at most 0.9 is about 0.51, just over one half, and the median is just under 0.9."
  3. Prof: "Does that link still work for an NHPP, say Goel-Okumoto?"
    You: "Yes, the event is the same, so P of T_3 at most t is 1 minus e to the minus m(t), times (1 + m(t) + m(t) squared over 2). But in Goel-Okumoto m(t) levels off at omega, so the third failure may never come: that has probability e to the minus omega times (1 + omega + omega squared over 2), which is 0.42 if omega is 3. Then the mean of T_3 is infinite, so I'd report probabilities, not a mean."

R3. Superposition and thinning

Two independent services fail as Poisson processes with rates 2 and 3 per hour. Each failure is "critical" with probability 0.2, independently. (a) Rate of all failures? (b) Rate of critical failures? (c) P(no critical failure in 2 hours)?

Solution. (a) Superposition: 5 per hour. (b) Thinning: 5 * 0.2 = 1 per hour, and the critical and non-critical streams are independent Poisson processes. (c) Poisson(2) count, P(0) = e^(-2) = 0.135.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "A critical failure has just happened. What is the chance it came from the first service?"
    You: "2 over 5, so 0.4. In the merged stream each failure comes from service 1 with probability lambda_1 over lambda_1 plus lambda_2, and the critical label is independent of the source, so knowing it is critical doesn't change that."
  2. Prof: "What if service 1's failures are critical with probability 0.5 and service 2's with 0.1?"
    You: "Then I thin each stream on its own: the critical rates are 1 and 0.3, so 1.3 per hour in total, and it's still a Poisson process. A critical failure now comes from service 1 with probability 1 over 1.3, about 0.77. Each event can be kept with its own probability, as long as the choices are independent."
  3. Prof: "Even if the keep probability changes over time?"
    You: "Yes. Keeping an event at time t with probability q(t), independently, gives an NHPP with intensity q(t) times lambda(t). My field model uses the same idea: each fault from change j counts as found by time t with probability G(t minus tau_j), independently, so the found faults of change j are Poisson with mean omega_j G(t minus tau_j)."

R4. Conditional uniformity

A Poisson process had exactly 3 events in [0, 10]. (a) P(all 3 in [0, 5])? (b) If there was exactly 1 event in [0, 10], what is its expected time?

Solution. Given N(10) = 3, the times are like 3 independent Uniform(0, 10) points. (a) Each is in [0, 5] with probability 1/2, so (1/2)^3 = 0.125. (b) Uniform(0, 10) has mean 5.

Follow-up. Why is this useful? It lets you simulate a Poisson process or test whether event times look uniform, given the count.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said you can check whether event times look uniform, given the count. How exactly?"
    You: "Under a constant rate, given n events in [0, T], the times divided by T behave like n independent uniform points. A simple check is the Laplace trend test, which compares their average with T over 2. A Kolmogorov-Smirnov test against the uniform distribution is another."
  2. Prof: "Write down the Laplace statistic and say why it works."
    You: "U is the mean event time minus T over 2, divided by T times the square root of 1 over 12n. A uniform point on [0, T] has mean T over 2 and variance T squared over 12, so under a constant rate U is about standard normal by the central limit theorem. A clearly negative U means events bunch early, which is reliability growth."
  3. Prof: "Apply it to ten failures at 2, 5, 9, 14, 20, 27, 35, 45, 58 and 75 days, with testing stopped at day 100."
    You: "The times add up to 290, so the mean is 29, and the top is 29 minus 50, which is minus 21. The bottom is 100 times the square root of 1 over 120, about 9.1, so U is about minus 2.3, below minus 1.96, and the data show reliability growth at the 5 percent level. U is negative exactly when the sum is below nT over 2, here 290 against 500, which is also the condition for the Goel-Okumoto MLE to exist on these data."

R5. An NHPP

An NHPP has intensity lambda(t) = 2t. (a) m(t)? (b) P(no event in (1, 2])? (c) Are counts in (0, 1] and (1, 2] independent? Identically distributed?

Solution. (a) m(t) = integral_0^t 2u du = t^2. (b) N(2) - N(1) ~ Poisson(m(2) - m(1)) = Poisson(3), so P(0) = e^(-3) = 0.0498. (c) Independent, yes (independent increments). Not identically distributed: the means are 1 and 3. That is what "non-homogeneous" means.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Given N(2) = 1, where do you expect that single event to be?"
    You: "For an NHPP, given the count, the event times are like independent points with density lambda(t) over m(T). Here that's 2t over 4, so t over 2 on [0, 2]. The expected time is the integral of t times t over 2 from 0 to 2, which is 8 over 6, so four thirds, later than the midpoint because the rate rises."
  2. Prof: "A rising rate. When would a software failure intensity actually rise like that?"
    You: "Early in testing. Testers are still learning the system, and isolating the fault behind a failure takes time, so detections speed up before they slow down. Yamada's delayed S-shaped model captures this with two stages per fault, failure observed and then fault isolated. But 2t rises forever, so it only fits the early part."
  3. Prof: "Where does the delayed S-shaped intensity reach its peak?"
    You: "m(t) is omega times (1 minus (1 + bt) e to the minus bt), so lambda(t) is omega b squared t e to the minus bt. Its derivative is zero when 1 minus bt is zero, so the peak is at t = 1 over b, with height omega b over e. After that the intensity falls, which is the reliability growth part."

R6. Goel-Okumoto numbers

GO model with omega = 100 faults and b = 0.05 per day. At t = 30 days find: (a) expected faults found, (b) expected remaining, (c) the failure intensity, (d) the reliability for the next day, R(1 | 30).

Solution. (a) m(30) = 100 (1 - e^(-1.5)) = 100 (1 - 0.2231) = 77.7. (b) 100 - 77.7 = 22.3. (c) lambda(30) = omega b e^(-b t) = 100 * 0.05 * 0.2231 = 1.12 per day. (d) m(31) - m(30) = 100 (e^(-1.5) - e^(-1.55)) = 22.31 (1 - e^(-0.05)) = 22.31 * 0.04877 = 1.088. R(1 | 30) = e^(-1.088) = 0.337.

Say it. "After 30 days about 78 of the expected 100 faults are found, so about 22 remain. The next day brings about 1.09 expected failures, so the chance of a failure-free day is e to the minus 1.09, about one third."

Follow-up. Why is R(x | t) an exponential of minus the increment? Because the number of failures in (t, t + x] is Poisson, and "no failure" is the k = 0 term, e^(-mean).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said about 22 faults remain. How much longer must they test until only 5 are expected to remain?"
    You: "Remaining is omega e to the minus bt, so 100 e to the minus 0.05t. Setting that to 5 gives e to the minus 0.05t equals 0.05, so t is ln 20 over 0.05, about 60 days. That's about 30 more days of testing."
  2. Prof: "Would you really test until then? How would you decide when to release?"
    You: "I'd treat it as a cost trade-off. If a day of testing costs c_3, a fault fixed in testing costs c_1 and one found in the field costs c_2, bigger than c_1, the expected cost is c_1 m(T) plus c_2 times (omega minus m(T)) plus c_3 T. Setting the derivative to zero says release when lambda(T) equals c_3 over (c_2 minus c_1), that is, when the faults you would still find per day are no longer worth a day of testing. This is the classic cost model usually credited to Okumoto and Goel in 1980."
  3. Prof: "Put in numbers: a day of testing costs 1, and a field fault costs 2 more than one found in testing."
    You: "Then I release when lambda(T) is 0.5. That's 5 e to the minus 0.05T equals 0.5, so T is ln 10 over 0.05, about 46 days, with about 10 faults still expected to remain. The weak point is that omega and b are estimates, so this release time carries their uncertainty too."

R7. Maximum likelihood for Goel-Okumoto, failure-time data

Failures were seen at t = 2, 5, 9, 14, 20, 27, 35, 45, 58, 75 days. Testing stopped at T = 100. (a) Write log L. (b) Find omega-hat in terms of b. (c) Get the equation for b and solve it. (d) When does a finite MLE exist?

Solution. (a) log L = sum_i log lambda(t_i) - m(T), with lambda(t) = omega b e^(-bt):

log L(omega, b) = n log omega + n log b - b sum_i t_i - omega (1 - e^(-bT))

(b) d/d omega: n/omega - (1 - e^(-bT)) = 0, so omega-hat = n / (1 - e^(-bT)). (c) d/db: n/b - sum t_i - omega T e^(-bT) = 0. Put in omega-hat:

n/b - sum t_i - n T e^(-bT) / (1 - e^(-bT)) = 0

This has no closed form. Solve it in one dimension (bisection or Newton). Here n = 10, sum t_i = 290, T = 100. Result: b-hat = 0.0284 per day, omega-hat = 10.62, so about 0.6 faults remain. log L at the maximum = -30.22. (d) As b -> 0, the left side of (c) tends to nT/2 - sum t_i. The left side falls to -sum t_i as b grows. So a positive root exists when sum t_i < nT/2, that is, when the average failure time is before T/2 (failures are slowing down). Here the average is 29 < 50, so it exists. If failures are not slowing down, the data show no reliability growth and the MLE runs off to b -> 0.

Check. A two-parameter Nelder-Mead maximisation gives the same (10.62, 0.0284).

Say it. "The NHPP log-likelihood is the sum of log intensities at the failure times minus m(T). Setting the omega derivative to zero gives omega-hat equals n over 1 minus e to the minus bT. Then b solves one equation numerically, and a solution exists only if the failures are concentrated in the first half of testing."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Your fit says only 0.6 faults remain. How sure are you of that?"
    You: "Not very, with only 10 failures. From the curvature of the log-likelihood the standard error of omega-hat is about 3.5, and the profile likelihood interval for omega runs from about 5 to about 28. For expected remaining faults, the profile interval runs from almost zero to about 17 and a half."
  2. Prof: "Why not just take omega-hat plus or minus 1.96 standard errors?"
    You: "Because the likelihood is lopsided. A slower process with more faults, small b and large omega, explains the same ten times almost as well, so the likelihood falls off slowly on the high side. The plus or minus interval, about 3.8 to 17.4, is symmetric and misses that long upper tail, while the profile interval follows the real shape."
  3. Prof: "You said the likelihood has a long upper tail. Would a parametric bootstrap pick that up?"
    You: "It can, but there's a trap. A simulated data set has a finite MLE only if its mean failure time is below T over 2, and about 1 percent of samples from the fitted model break that, so for them b-hat goes to zero and omega-hat to infinity. If you drop those samples, the upper end of the 95 percent interval for remaining faults falls from about 15 to about 10, which hides exactly that long tail. Wu, Dohi, Zheng and Okamura have recent papers on parametric bootstrap methods for NHPP models, and I'd want to read them properly before doing this."

Three independent components each have R = 0.9. (a) Series? (b) Parallel? (c) Two of them in parallel, then in series with a third part of R = 0.95?

Solution. (a) 0.9^3 = 0.729. (b) 1 - 0.1^3 = 0.999. (c) (1 - 0.1^2) * 0.95 = 0.99 * 0.95 = 0.9405.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "In part (c), if you could improve just one part, which would you pick?"
    You: "The third one, in series. Its Birnbaum importance, how much the system reliability rises per unit rise in its own reliability, is the reliability of the parallel pair, 0.99. Each parallel part's is 0.1 times 0.95, which is 0.095, so the series part matters about ten times more."
  2. Prof: "Birnbaum importance ignores how reliable the part already is. Is there a measure that doesn't?"
    You: "Criticality importance: Birnbaum times the part's unreliability, divided by the system's unreliability, which is the chance that the part is failed and critical, given that the system has failed. The system fails with probability 0.0595, so the series part gets 0.99 times 0.05 over 0.0595, about 0.83, and each parallel part about 0.16. It points to the same part."
  3. Prof: "Those numbers treat 0.9 and 0.95 as known exactly. What if they're estimates from a small amount of data?"
    You: "Then the importances are uncertain too, so I'd put a Beta distribution on each reliability from its counts, draw many times, and see how often the ranking changes. With only 20 demands behind each number, Birnbaum's ranking never changed in my draws, while criticality put the parallel parts first in about 7 to 16 percent of them, depending on the prior. Zhang, Zheng, Okamura and Dohi's 2021 note on criticality importance under epistemic uncertainty makes the same point: the ranking itself can change."

R21. 2-out-of-3 (triple modular redundancy)

(a) Three independent units, each R = 0.9. The system works if at least 2 work. R_system? (b) Each unit has an Exponential(lambda) life. Find R_TMR(t) and its MTTF. Compare with one unit. (c) When is TMR better than one unit?

Solution. (a) 3R^2 - 2R^3 = 3(0.81) - 2(0.729) = 0.972. Note 1 - 0.972 = 0.028, the 2-of-3 vote failure with p = 0.1. Same formula, with p = 1 - R. (b) R = e^(-lambda t): R_TMR(t) = 3 e^(-2 lambda t) - 2 e^(-3 lambda t). MTTF = 3/(2 lambda) - 2/(3 lambda) = 5/(6 lambda), which is less than 1/lambda for one unit. (c) 3R^2 - 2R^3 > R <=> R(1 - R)(2R - 1) > 0 <=> R > 1/2, that is, t < ln 2 / lambda. TMR is better early and worse late, because after two failures it is dead while a single unit may still be alive.

Say it. "TMR is more reliable while each unit's reliability is above one half, but its mean life is only five sixths of one unit's. This is the same algebra as majority voting: voting helps only on inputs where theta is below one half."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You showed TMR's mean life is only five sixths of one unit's. Why would anyone use TMR then?"
    You: "Because what usually matters is surviving a short mission, not the mean life. For a mission with lambda t = 0.01, one unit fails with probability about 1 percent, while TMR fails with about 3 times (lambda t) squared, 0.0003, about 33 times less. TMR only loses in the long tail, after two units have failed while a single unit might still be running."
  2. Prof: "And if a failed unit can be repaired while the other two keep working?"
    You: "Then it's a small CTMC with states three working, two working, and system failure as absorbing, assuming independent exponential failures and one repairer. The first-step equations give an MTTF of (5 lambda plus mu) over 6 lambda squared, so with lambda = 0.01 and mu = 0.5 per hour that's about 917 hours, against 100 for one unit and 83 for TMR without repair."
  3. Prof: "You assumed the units fail independently. In your plan the three versions come from agent runs on the same task and meet the same inputs. Does 3R squared minus 2R cubed still hold?"
    You: "Only on one input at a time. If theta(x) is the chance a run fails on input x, the vote fails with 3 theta squared minus 2 theta cubed on that input, so overall it fails with E of 3 theta squared minus 2 theta cubed. In my example with theta from a Beta(0.5, 4.5), mean 0.1, that's 0.057, about twice the 0.028 that independence predicts, and on inputs where theta is above one half, voting is worse than a single run."

R22. Dependent failures in a parallel pair

Two units each fail with probability 0.1 on a demand. (a) Unreliability of the parallel pair if failures are independent? (b) If P(both fail) = 0.03? (c) The correlation of the two failure indicators?

Solution. (a) 0.1^2 = 0.01. (b) 0.03, three times worse. (c) Cov = 0.03 - 0.01 = 0.02. Var of each = 0.1 * 0.9 = 0.09. Corr = 0.02/0.09 = 0.22. This is Eckhardt and Lee for hardware: a shared cause (same input, same power supply, same bug) makes redundancy worth much less than the independent formula says.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "A correlation of only 0.22 tripled the failure probability. How can such a small correlation matter so much?"
    You: "Because P(both fail) is p squared plus the covariance, and the covariance is rho times p times (1 minus p). With p = 0.1, p squared is 0.01 but the covariance is already 0.02, so the dependent part is the bigger one. With p = 0.01 and the same rho, the pair would fail about 23 times more often than independence says, so the better the units, the more the correlation dominates."
  2. Prof: "Where would that correlation come from, if the two units don't affect each other at all?"
    You: "From demands that are hard for both, as in the Eckhardt and Lee model. For example, if a third of demands are hard, and on those each unit fails with probability 0.3 independently, while on the rest neither fails, each unit fails with probability 0.1, and both fail with one third times 0.09, which is 0.03. The units are independent given the demand, but they find the same demands hard."
  3. Prof: "In your plan you can't see which inputs are hard. How would you estimate that 0.03 from the runs alone?"
    You: "On each input I'd count J, how many of my k versions fail it. Given theta, J is binomial, so J times (J minus 1) over k times (k minus 1) has expectation theta squared, and its average over inputs estimates the chance that two runs both fail, with no model for theta. Subtracting p-hat squared then gives the variance of theta, 0.02 here, which is exactly the part that independence leaves out."

R23. Availability

(a) MTTF = 1000 h, MTTR = 10 h: steady-state availability? (b) A = 0.999: hours down per year? (c) Two independent units with A = 0.99 in series? In parallel?

Solution. (a) 1000/1010 = 0.990. (b) 0.001 * 8760 = 8.76 h. (c) Series: 0.99^2 = 0.9801. Parallel: 1 - 0.01^2 = 0.9999 (assuming each has its own repairer).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "In (a) you used only the MTTF and the MTTR. Does 1000 over 1010 need exponential times?"
    You: "No. By renewal-reward, the long-run share of time up is the mean up time over the mean cycle length, for any up and down distributions, as long as the cycles are independent and alike. The shape only changes how availability behaves before it settles, the instantaneous availability A(t)."
  2. Prof: "Then work out A(t) in the exponential case, starting from up."
    You: "With failure rate 0.001 and repair rate 0.1 per hour, A(t) is mu over (lambda plus mu), plus lambda over (lambda plus mu) times e to the minus (lambda plus mu) t. That's 0.990 plus 0.0099 times e to the minus 0.101 t, so it starts at 1 and settles with a time constant of about 10 hours, close to the mean repair time. After one day it's about 0.991, essentially the steady state."
  3. Prof: "A user cares whether the system stays up through a whole 8-hour shift. Is that 0.990?"
    You: "No, that's interval reliability: the chance it's up at the start and doesn't fail during the shift. For one unit with an exponential life it's A(t) times e to the minus 8 lambda, so in steady state 0.990 times e to the minus 0.008, about 0.982. Prof. Zheng, Prof. Okamura and Prof. Dohi have a 2020 Software Quality Journal paper on transient interval reliability for software rejuvenation models with phase expansion, which I'd like to read next."

R24. Software rejuvenation: when to restart

A server's time to an aging failure is Uniform(0, 100) hours. A planned restart (rejuvenation) takes 0.1 h. Recovery after a failure takes 1 h. You restart at age t0 or at failure, whichever comes first. Each restart or recovery makes the server as good as new. Find the t0 that maximises availability, and compare with never restarting.

Solution. Renewal-reward for one cycle:

Uptime     U(t0) = integral_0^t0 (1 - u/100) du = t0 - t0^2/200
Downtime   D(t0) = 0.1 * P(no failure before t0) + 1 * P(failure before t0)
                 = 0.1 (1 - t0/100) + t0/100 = 0.1 + 0.009 t0
A(t0) = U / (U + D)   -> maximise A  <=>  minimise D / U
D' U = D U'  <=>  0.009 (t0 - t0^2/200) = (0.1 + 0.009 t0)(1 - t0/100)
             <=>  0.000045 t0^2 + 0.001 t0 - 0.1 = 0
             <=>  t0^2 + 22.22 t0 - 2222.2 = 0   ->  t0 = 37.3 h
A(37.3) = 30.36 / (30.36 + 0.436) = 0.9858
Never restart (t0 = 100): U = 50, D = 1, A = 50/51 = 0.9804

Restart at age 37.3 h (unless a failure comes first): availability 0.9858 against 0.9804. Downtime falls by about 28%.

Check. A numerical maximiser over t0 gives 37.32 h and 0.98584.

Say it. "Each cycle ends with a short planned restart or a long failure. By renewal-reward, availability is expected uptime over expected cycle length, and here it is best to restart at about 37 hours, which cuts downtime by more than a quarter." (This toy model is mine. Prof. Zheng's papers use richer models, Markov regenerative processes and non-Markovian models solved by phase expansion. Some of them, such as IEICE Transactions 2020 and ISSRE Workshops 2020, look for the rejuvenation time that maximises steady-state availability, as here.)

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You found 37.3 hours. Operators prefer round numbers. How much do you lose by restarting every 24 or every 48 hours?"
    You: "Very little. Availability is about 0.98526 at 24 hours and 0.98563 at 48, against 0.98584 at the optimum, so the curve is flat near its top. Either one still cuts downtime by about a quarter compared with never restarting."
  2. Prof: "Flat in t0, fine. But what if the aging failure time were exponential with the same 50-hour mean?"
    You: "Then restarting can only hurt. The exponential is memoryless, so a restart doesn't make the server any younger and just adds 0.1 hours of downtime: restarting at 37.3 hours gives about 0.9787, below the 0.9804 of never restarting. So whether to rejuvenate at all depends on the shape of the failure-time distribution, not just its mean."
  3. Prof: "So how would you find that shape in a real system, and has our lab looked at this kind of sensitivity?"
    You: "I'd log the times to aging failure, count the runs that were restarted before failing as censored, and fit a Weibull to see whether beta is above 1. Prof. Zheng, Prof. Okamura and Prof. Dohi asked 'How robust is the optimal software rejuvenation timing?' at the ISSRE workshops in 2020: with a Weibull failure time, they took the first derivatives of the optimal time with respect to each parameter and found it more robust to the failure-time parameters than to the others. That's a local result around one setting, so it doesn't clash with my exponential example, which swaps the whole distribution."

R25. Mean time to failure with repair

Same system as R12 (two units, lambda = 0.01, mu = 0.5, one repairer), but now "both down" is absorbing. Starting with both up, find the MTTF. Compare with no repair.

Solution. Let T_2 and T_1 be the expected times to absorption from states 2 and 1.

T_2 = 1/(2 lambda) + T_1
T_1 = 1/(lambda + mu) + [mu/(lambda + mu)] T_2       (from 1: go to 0 or back to 2)
Substitute:  T_2 = 1/(2 lambda) + 1/(lambda + mu) + [mu/(lambda + mu)] T_2
             T_2 [lambda/(lambda + mu)] = 1/(2 lambda) + 1/(lambda + mu)
             T_2 = (lambda + mu)/(2 lambda^2) + 1/lambda = (3 lambda + mu)/(2 lambda^2)

MTTF = (0.03 + 0.5) / 0.0002 = 2,650 h. Without repair (mu = 0): 1/(2 lambda) + 1/lambda = 150 h. Repair makes the pair last about 18 times longer.

Check. Simulation with 20,000 runs: 2,676 h (standard error about 19 h).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said repair makes the pair last about 18 times longer. Can you see the 2,650 hours without the equations?"
    You: "Yes. From both up, the first failure comes after 1 over 2 lambda, 50 hours on average, and the second unit then fails before the repair ends with probability lambda over (lambda plus mu), 1 in 51. So there are 51 tries on average, each about 50 hours plus about 2 hours with one unit down, and 51 times about 52 gives the 2,650."
  2. Prof: "A geometric number of tries. So what is the chance the pair survives a whole year?"
    You: "A geometric sum of short tries is close to exponential, so R(t) is about e to the minus t over 2,650. For 8,760 hours that's e to the minus 3.3, about 0.037, so the pair fails within a year about 96 percent of the time, even though its availability with repair is 0.99923. Availability is the share of time up, while reliability asks for no failure at all."
  3. Prof: "That's an approximation. How would you get the exact value?"
    You: "I'd keep only the two working states, whose generator block has rows minus 2 lambda, 2 lambda and mu, minus (lambda plus mu), and compute the start vector times the matrix exponential of that block times t, times a vector of ones. That gives 0.0366, so the approximation's 0.0367 was very close, because the block's slow eigenvalue corresponds to about 2,648 hours and the fast one dies out within about 2 hours. In fact the time to failure is exactly a sum of two exponential stages with those two rates, a two-phase phase-type distribution in the CF1 form that pysrat uses."
