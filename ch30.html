Chapter 30 of 61

Probability

When and how long: Days 4 to 6.

Probability is the language of the whole lab, and of your plan. Prof. Zheng expects most questions to be on your plan, and said on 2 Nov that the rest depends on which professors join. If one of them asks a "basic" question, it is likely to come from this file: a definition, a distribution, or a small calculation like the chance that the first of three parts to fail is part 3.

Part 1 is the list of statements to say out loud. Part 2 has 30 short problems to solve out loud, with full solutions. 01_reliability_and_probability_primer.md section 1 has the very short version.

How the answers were checked. Every numeric answer was recomputed on 12 Nov 2026 with Python (numpy, scipy), and again on 13 Nov 2026. P14, P15, P23, P24 and P29 were also checked by simulation (200,000 to 500,000 draws each). Nothing in this file is marked [CHECK].

Part 1. Statements to say out loud

1.1 Events and rules

  • Axioms (Kolmogorov). P(A) >= 0. P(whole sample space) = 1. For disjoint events, P(A1 or A2 or ...) = P(A1) + P(A2) + ...
  • Consequences. P(not A) = 1 - P(A). P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B) (inclusion-exclusion).
  • Conditional probability. P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B), for P(B) > 0.
  • Multiplication rule. P(A and B) = P(A | B) P(B).
  • Law of total probability. If B1, ..., Bn split the space, P(A) = sum_i P(A | Bi) P(Bi).
  • Bayes. P(B | A) = P(A | B) P(B) / P(A). Say: "posterior is likelihood times prior, divided by the evidence."
  • Independence. P(A and B) = P(A) P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A). Disjoint events with positive probability are never independent.
  • Mutual independence of three events needs all pairs and P(A and B and C) = P(A) P(B) P(C). Pairwise is not enough (P5).
  • Conditional independence is not independence. Two runs can be independent given the input and still dependent overall. This is the whole point of Eckhardt and Lee.

1.2 Distributions (ASCII table)

Distribution pmf or pdf Mean Variance Where you meet it
Bernoulli(p) p^x (1-p)^(1-x), x = 0, 1 p p(1-p) one run fails or not
Binomial(n, p) C(n,k) p^k (1-p)^(n-k) np np(1-p) failing runs out of n, if independent
Geometric(p) (1-p)^(k-1) p, k = 1, 2, ... 1/p (1-p)/p^2 attempts until first success
Poisson(lambda) e^(-lambda) lambda^k / k! lambda lambda failure counts, NHPP
Uniform(a, b) 1/(b-a) (a+b)/2 (b-a)^2/12 random input, conditional event times
Exponential(lambda) lambda e^(-lambda x), x >= 0 1/lambda 1/lambda^2 time to failure, memoryless
Gamma(n, lambda) lambda^n x^(n-1) e^(-lambda x) / Gamma(n) n/lambda n/lambda^2 time to n-th event
Normal(mu, sigma^2) exp(-(x-mu)^2 / (2 sigma^2)) / (sigma sqrt(2 pi)) mu sigma^2 CLT, tests
Beta(a, b) theta^(a-1) (1-theta)^(b-1) / B(a,b), 0 < theta < 1 a/(a+b) ab / ((a+b)^2 (a+b+1)) theta(x) in your plan

Gamma here uses shape n and rate lambda. Geometric here counts the trial of the first success (some books count failures before it; then the mean is (1-p)/p).

MGFs M(t) = E[e^(tX)]: Bernoulli 1 - p + p e^t. Binomial (1 - p + p e^t)^n. Poisson exp(lambda (e^t - 1)). Exponential lambda/(lambda - t) for t < lambda. Gamma (lambda/(lambda - t))^n. Normal exp(mu t + sigma^2 t^2 / 2).

Links between them. A sum of n independent Bernoulli(p) is Binomial(n, p). Binomial(n, p) with n large and p small is close to Poisson(np). A sum of n independent Exponential(lambda) is Gamma(n, lambda). Poisson process gaps are exponential. A Beta mixture of binomials is the beta-binomial (your plan).

1.3 Expectation, variance, covariance

  • E[aX + b] = a E[X] + b. E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] always, even when X and Y are dependent.
  • Var X = E[X^2] - (E X)^2. Var(aX + b) = a^2 Var X.
  • Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X] E[Y]. Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y + 2 Cov(X, Y).
  • Corr(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y) / (sd(X) sd(Y)), always between -1 and 1.
  • Independent implies Cov = 0. Cov = 0 does not imply independent (P22).
  • Law of total expectation: E[X] = E[ E[X | Y] ].
  • Law of total variance: Var X = E[ Var(X | Y) ] + Var( E[X | Y] ). Say: "the average of the variance inside groups, plus the variance of the group means." This gives the beta-binomial variance in your plan.
  • MGF use: M^(n)(0) = E[X^n], and the MGF of a sum of independent variables is the product of their MGFs.

1.4 Inequalities and limit theorems

  • Markov: for X >= 0 and a > 0, P(X >= a) <= E[X] / a.
  • Chebyshev: P(|X - mu| >= k sigma) <= 1/k^2.
  • Law of large numbers: the sample mean of i.i.d. variables with mean mu tends to mu as n grows. Say: "averages settle down to the true mean."
  • Central limit theorem: for i.i.d. variables with mean mu and variance sigma^2, (X-bar - mu) / (sigma / sqrt(n)) is approximately N(0, 1) for large n, whatever the original distribution. Say: "sums and averages of many independent pieces look normal."

1.5 Exponential order statistics (used all the time in reliability)

  • Memoryless: P(T > s + t | T > s) = P(T > t). The exponential is the only continuous distribution with this property.
  • Minimum: if T_i ~ Exponential(lambda_i) are independent, min T_i ~ Exponential(sum lambda_i), and P(T_j is the smallest) = lambda_j / sum lambda_i.
  • Gaps: for n i.i.d. Exponential(lambda), the gaps between consecutive failures are independent exponentials with rates n lambda, (n-1) lambda, ..., lambda. So E[k-th failure time] = (1/lambda) sum_{i=0}^{k-1} 1/(n - i), and E[max] = (1/lambda)(1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/n).

Part 2. 30 problems with full solutions

P1. Inclusion-exclusion

P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.4, P(A and B) = 0.1. Find P(A or B), P(neither), P(exactly one). Are A and B independent?

Solution. P(A or B) = 0.3 + 0.4 - 0.1 = 0.6. P(neither) = 1 - 0.6 = 0.4. P(exactly one) = 0.6 - 0.1 = 0.5. P(A) P(B) = 0.12, not 0.1, so not independent.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said A and B are not independent. In which direction? Does knowing B make A more or less likely?"
    You: "Less likely. P of A given B is 0.1 over 0.4, which is 0.25, below P of A, which is 0.3. So they are negatively dependent: they happen together less often than independence would give."
  2. Prof: "Put a number on that. What is the correlation between their indicator variables?"
    You: "The covariance of the indicators is P of A and B minus P of A times P of B, so 0.1 minus 0.12, which is minus 0.02. Each indicator's variance is p times 1 minus p, so I divide by the square root of 0.21 times 0.24, and the correlation is about minus 0.09."
  3. Prof: "Now let A and B be two program versions failing on a random input. Could their failures be negatively correlated like this in your plan?"
    You: "Not for two runs from the same setting. Given the input they fail independently, so both fail with p squared plus the variance of theta, which is never below p squared. For runs from two different settings it can happen when they find different inputs hard, and that is the best case for voting."

P2. Picking modules

10 modules, 2 of them buggy. You review 3 at random. P(you see at least one buggy module)?

Solution. Use the complement. P(no buggy) = C(8, 3) / C(10, 3) = 56 / 120. So P(at least one) = 1 - 56/120 = 64/120 = 8/15 = 0.533.

Say it. "It's easier to count the complement: all three from the eight clean modules, 56 out of 120 ways, so at least one buggy is 8 over 15."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Instead of the complement, give me the whole distribution of the number of buggy modules you see. What is its mean?"
    You: "It's hypergeometric. Zero buggy has probability 56 over 120, one buggy is 2 times 28, also 56 over 120, and two buggy is 8 over 120. The mean is 3 times 2 over 10, so 0.6."
  2. Prof: "0.6 is also the mean of a binomial with n = 3 and p = 0.2. So what changes if you sample with replacement?"
    You: "Then each pick is buggy with chance 0.2 independently, and P of at least one is 1 minus 0.8 cubed, 0.488, lower than 0.533 because I can waste a review on a module I've already seen. The mean stays 0.6, but the variance rises from 0.373 to 0.48. Without replacement the picks are slightly negatively dependent, which multiplies the variance by 7 over 9."
  3. Prof: "You keep assuming every module has the same 0.2 chance. Real modules don't. How would you handle that?"
    You: "I'd give each module its own bug probability from a model on its metrics, like size and complexity. Then the number of buggy modules is a sum of independent Bernoullis with different p's, which is the Poisson binomial distribution. Prof. Dohi and Prof. Okamura's 2022 review does this on NASA's PC4 data: they test the riskiest modules first and use the Poisson binomial to predict how many buggy modules are left, to decide when to stop."

P3. Bayes with a static analyser

2% of functions are buggy. A tool flags 90% of buggy functions and 5% of clean ones. A function is flagged. P(it is buggy)?

Solution.

P(flag) = 0.9 (0.02) + 0.05 (0.98) = 0.018 + 0.049 = 0.067
P(buggy | flag) = 0.018 / 0.067 = 0.269

About 27%. Most flags are false alarms, because clean functions are so common (the base rate).

Say it. "By Bayes, it is the true flags, 0.018, over all flags, 0.067, so about 27 percent. Most flags are false because only 2 percent of functions are buggy."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Your answer depends heavily on the 2 percent. What if 20 percent of functions were buggy?"
    You: "Then P of a flag is 0.9 times 0.2 plus 0.05 times 0.8, which is 0.18 plus 0.04, so 0.22. P of buggy given a flag is 0.18 over 0.22, about 82 percent. Same tool, very different meaning."
  2. Prof: "So how would you describe the tool's strength in a way that doesn't depend on the base rate?"
    You: "With the likelihood ratio, 0.9 over 0.05, which is 18: a flag multiplies the odds of a bug by 18. At 2 percent the prior odds are 2 to 98, and times 18 that is about 0.37, which is 27 percent. At 20 percent the odds go from 0.25 to 4.5, which is 82 percent."
  3. Prof: "If a second run of the tool flags it again, do you multiply by 18 again?"
    You: "Only if the two flags are independent given whether the function is buggy. A deterministic analyser gives the same answer twice, so the second flag adds nothing. A second tool, just as accurate and with independent errors, would take the odds to about 6.6, or 87 percent. Real tools tend to flag the same tricky code, though, so the gain is smaller, and that's the same question as runs failing together in my plan."

P4. Disjoint against independent

Can two disjoint events with positive probability be independent?

Solution. No. Disjoint means P(A and B) = 0. Independent needs P(A and B) = P(A) P(B) > 0. If A happens, you know B did not, which is the opposite of independence.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said that if A happens you know B did not. Write that as a conditional probability."
    You: "P of B given A is P of A and B over P of A, which is 0. But P of B is positive. Independence needs P of B given A to equal P of B, so learning A has moved B's chance all the way to zero."
  2. Prof: "How strong is that dependence? What is the covariance of the two indicators?"
    You: "It's P of A and B minus P of A times P of B, so it is minus P of A times P of B, always negative. For example, if each has probability 0.1, the covariance is minus 0.01, and the correlation is minus 0.01 over 0.09, about minus 0.11."
  3. Prof: "Negative correlation sounds good for redundancy. What if three program versions had pairwise disjoint failure regions?"
    You: "Then on any input at most one version fails, so a 2-out-of-3 vote never fails, even if each version fails on 10 percent of inputs. That is the ideal N-version programming hopes for. Eckhardt and Lee's point is that versions from one development process share the same hard inputs, so in practice the regions overlap."

P5. Pairwise but not mutually independent

Toss two fair coins. A = first is heads, B = second is heads, C = both show the same face. Are A, B, C pairwise independent? Mutually independent?

Solution. P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = 1/2. P(A and C) = P(HH) = 1/4 = P(A) P(C). Same for (B, C) and (A, B). So pairwise independent. But P(A and B and C) = P(HH) = 1/4, not 1/8. Not mutually independent: A and B together decide C.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said A and B together decide C. What is P of C given A and B?"
    You: "It's 1, because if both coins are heads the faces match. But P of C is one half. So C is independent of A alone and of B alone, but not of the pair."
  2. Prof: "Then how many conditions must you check for mutual independence of n events?"
    You: "One product condition for every subset of two or more events. For three events that is the three pairs plus the triple, so four equations. For n events it is 2 to the n, minus n, minus 1."
  3. Prof: "For a 2-out-of-3 vote, are the pair conditions enough?"
    You: "No. P of at least two failing is the sum of the three pairwise probabilities minus 2 times the triple one, so the triple term enters directly. If A, B and C here were three versions failing, every pair looks independent, yet at least two fail with probability 1/4, against 1/2 under full independence. That's why my vote formula needs E of theta cubed, not only pairwise correlations."

P6. Binomial

10 independent runs each fail with probability 0.2. P(at most one fails)?

Solution. P(0) + P(1) = 0.8^10 + 10 (0.2)(0.8^9) = 0.1074 + 0.2684 = 0.376.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "This assumes the 10 runs fail independently. In your plan the runs share each input. What changes?"
    You: "Given the input, the count is still binomial, but with that input's own chance theta. Over random inputs, with a Beta model for theta as in my plan, it becomes beta-binomial: the mean stays 2, but the variance is multiplied by 1 plus 9 rho. So more inputs see no failure at all, and more see many."
  2. Prof: "Give me the number. Take theta from a Beta of 1 and 4."
    You: "That has mean 0.2 and rho of 1 over 6. With a equal to 1 the terms are simple: P of 0 is 4 over 14, which is 0.286, and P of 1 is 10 times 4, over 14 times 13, which is 0.220. So P of at most one failure rises from 0.376 to about 0.51, and the variance goes from 1.6 to 4."
  3. Prof: "So dependence makes 'at most one failure' more likely. Is that good news for voting?"
    You: "No, because the spread fattens the other tail too. The chance that 6 or more of the 10 runs fail, so the majority is wrong, goes from 0.006 to 0.07, more than ten times higher. A vote fails exactly on the inputs where most runs fail together."

P7. Binomial mean and variance from indicators

Derive the mean and variance of Binomial(n, p). Which step needs independence? Give the numbers for n = 20, p = 0.1.

Solution. J = F_1 + ... + F_n with F_i ~ Bernoulli(p). E[J] = sum E[F_i] = np (no independence needed). Var J = sum Var F_i + 2 (sum over pairs i < j of Cov(F_i, F_j)) = np(1 - p) only if all covariances are 0 (here independence is needed). n = 20, p = 0.1: mean 2, variance 1.8.

Link to your plan. If runs share the input, Cov(F_i, F_j) = Var theta > 0, and the variance becomes np(1 - p)[1 + (n - 1) rho] (file 05, D3).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the variance step needs independence. What if the runs are independent, but each run has its own failure chance?"
    You: "Then the count has a Poisson binomial distribution. The mean is still the sum of the p's, and the variance is the sum of p times 1 minus p, which is never more than the binomial variance at the average p. For example, ten runs at 0.05 and ten at 0.15 give 1.75 instead of 1.8, so unequal chances alone make the count less spread, not more."
  2. Prof: "But your link to the plan says shared inputs make it more spread. How do you reconcile the two?"
    You: "It depends on what varies. If each run has its own fixed chance, the runs still fail independently and the variance can only go down. If the chance varies by input and all runs meet the same input, they move together, and n times n minus 1 times the variance of theta is added. So overdispersion in my data points to shared hard inputs, not just to some runs being worse than others."
  3. Prof: "How big must that effect be to matter? Take n = 20 and p = 0.1."
    You: "To double the binomial variance of 1.8, I need n times n minus 1 times Var theta to equal n p times 1 minus p. That gives Var theta equal to 0.09 over 19, about 0.0047, which is rho equal to 1 over 19, about 0.05. So even a small correlation doubles the variance when 20 runs share each input."

P8. Geometric

Each attempt to make a flaky test pass succeeds with probability 0.3, independently. (a) Expected attempts until success? (b) P(more than 3 attempts)? (c) P(more than 5 | more than 2)?

Solution. (a) 1/0.3 = 3.33. (b) The first 3 all fail: 0.7^3 = 0.343. (c) Memoryless: P(X > 5 | X > 2) = 0.7^5 / 0.7^2 = 0.7^3 = 0.343.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You used memorylessness in part (c). Is a real flaky test memoryless?"
    You: "Only if every attempt passes with the same chance, independently of the others. Real flaky tests often depend on something that persists, like a slow machine or a busy shared service, so failures come in streaks. Then after several failures the next pass is less likely than 0.3."
  2. Prof: "How would you check that from retry logs?"
    You: "For each k, I'd take all attempts that came right after k failures in a row and compute the share that passed. If the test is memoryless, that share is about 0.3 for every k. A share that falls as k grows means the failures cluster."
  3. Prof: "Suppose it does fall. How would you model it?"
    You: "I'd let the pass chance vary between sessions, for example with a Beta of 3 and 7, whose mean is 0.3. Then the chance of passing on attempt k, given k minus 1 failures, is a over a plus b plus k minus 1: 0.3, then 0.27, then 0.25, and still falling. It's the same idea as theta of x in my plan: a mixture of memoryless pieces is not memoryless."

P9. Poisson

Failures per week ~ Poisson(2). P(no failures)? P(at least 2)?

Solution. P(0) = e^(-2) = 0.135. P(>= 2) = 1 - e^(-2)(1 + 2) = 1 - 3 e^(-2) = 0.594.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "What is the chance of two weeks in a row with no failures, and what are you assuming?"
    You: "If the rate stays at 2 per week and counts in different weeks are independent, the two-week count is Poisson with mean 4, so P of none is e to the minus 4, about 0.018. That assumes a homogeneous Poisson process: a constant rate and independent counts in periods that don't overlap."
  2. Prof: "During testing the rate falls as bugs are fixed. What changes?"
    You: "Then it's a non-homogeneous Poisson process. The count in any interval is still Poisson, but its mean is m of t2 minus m of t1, where m is the mean value function. For the Goel-Okumoto model, m of t is a times 1 minus e to the minus b t, where a is the expected total number of faults and b is the detection rate."
  3. Prof: "Show me with numbers how much safer a later week is."
    You: "Take a = 40 and b = 0.05 per week, so the first week has about 1.95 expected failures, close to the 2 we started with. Week 21 has 40 times e to the minus 1 minus e to the minus 1.05, about 0.72, so P of no failure that week is about 0.49 instead of 0.14. That is exactly what NHPP models call software reliability: e to the minus the expected number of failures between t and t plus x."

P10. Poisson approximation to the binomial

1,000 inputs, each fails independently with probability 0.002. Compare the exact P(no failure) with the Poisson approximation. When is the approximation good?

Solution. Exact: 0.998^1000 = 0.13506. Poisson(np = 2): e^(-2) = 0.13534. Good when n is large, p is small and np is moderate. Reason: (1 - lambda/n)^n tends to e^(-lambda).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said 1 minus lambda over n, to the power n, tends to e to the minus lambda. Why?"
    You: "Take logs: n times ln of 1 minus lambda over n. For small x, ln of 1 minus x is about minus x minus x squared over 2, so this is about minus lambda minus lambda squared over 2n, which tends to minus lambda. Exponentiating gives e to the minus lambda."
  2. Prof: "Does that second term explain the small gap you found?"
    You: "Yes. With lambda = 2 and n = 1,000 the correction is 4 over 2,000, which is 0.002, so the exact value should be about e to the minus 2.002. That is 0.13506, the exact answer to five places, so the Poisson value is about 0.2 percent too high, as predicted."
  3. Prof: "That's only P of zero. How close is the whole distribution?"
    You: "Le Cam's inequality bounds the total variation distance, the largest error over any event, by the sum of the p's squared: here 1,000 times 0.002 squared, which is 0.004. It also holds when every input has its own p, which is the Poisson binomial case. The actual distance here is about 0.00045, roughly a tenth of the bound."

P11. Sum of independent Poissons

X ~ Poisson(2) and Y ~ Poisson(3), independent. Show X + Y ~ Poisson(5). Find P(X + Y = 0).

Solution. MGFs multiply: exp(2(e^t - 1)) exp(3(e^t - 1)) = exp(5(e^t - 1)), the Poisson(5) MGF. An MGF fixes the distribution, so X + Y ~ Poisson(5). P(0) = e^(-5) = 0.0067 (= e^(-2) e^(-3), as it should).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Given that X plus Y equals 5, what is the distribution of X?"
    You: "Binomial with n = 5 and p = 2 over 5. P of X = k given the sum is P of X = k times P of Y = 5 minus k, over P of the sum being 5. The exponentials cancel and leave C of 5, k, times 2 fifths to the k, times 3 fifths to the 5 minus k, so on average 2 of the 5 come from X."
  2. Prof: "What does that 2 over 5 mean physically?"
    You: "It's X's share of the total rate. If failures come from two independent Poisson processes with rates 2 and 3, the merged stream is Poisson with rate 5, and each failure came from the first source with chance 2 over 5, independently of the others."
  3. Prof: "And if the two sources share a common cause, so X and Y are dependent?"
    You: "Then the sum need not be Poisson. Say X is U plus W and Y is V plus W, with independent Poissons U, V and W of means 1, 2 and 1, where W counts shocks that hit both. X and Y are still Poisson 2 and Poisson 3, but X plus Y is U plus V plus 2W, with mean 5 and variance 7. So a variance above the mean is one simple sign of a shared cause."

P12. Memoryless property

Prove that the exponential is memoryless. A part with MTTF 1,000 h has worked for 500 h. P(it works 500 h more)?

Solution. P(T > s + t | T > s) = e^(-lambda (s + t)) / e^(-lambda s) = e^(-lambda t) = P(T > t). With lambda = 0.001: e^(-0.5) = 0.607, the same as for a new part.

Say it. "For an exponential lifetime, a used part is as good as new: the chance of lasting another t hours doesn't depend on its age. That is why constant-hazard models can't describe aging."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said constant-hazard models can't describe aging. What would you use instead?"
    You: "A Weibull with shape k greater than 1. Its hazard is k over eta, times t over eta to the power k minus 1, which rises with age, and k = 1 gives back the exponential."
  2. Prof: "Redo your number with a Weibull of shape 2 and the same MTTF of 1,000 hours."

P16. Gamma and Poisson

P23. Total expectation and total variance

70% of modules are small, with fault count ~ Poisson(1). 30% are large, with ~ Poisson(5). For a random module find E[N] and Var N. Is N Poisson?

Solution.

E[N]   = 0.7 (1) + 0.3 (5) = 2.2
E[Var(N | type)] = 0.7 (1) + 0.3 (5) = 2.2           (Poisson: variance = mean)
Var(E[N | type]) = 0.7 (1)^2 + 0.3 (5)^2 - 2.2^2 = 0.7 + 7.5 - 4.84 = 3.36
Var N = 2.2 + 3.36 = 5.56

Not Poisson, because Var N > E[N]. A mixture of Poissons is overdispersed, just as a mixture of binomials (your beta-binomial) is.

Say it. "By the law of large numbers the share of failing inputs settles on p, and its standard deviation is root p times 1 minus p over n. At one percent and 10,000 inputs that's about plus or minus 20 percent at 95 percent confidence."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Your formula for n needs p, which is what you are trying to estimate. How would you choose n in practice?"
    You: "The formula says n times p, the expected number of failures, must be about 380 for plus or minus 10 percent, whatever p is. So the precision really depends on how many failures I see. I'd either plan for the smallest p I care about, or keep drawing inputs until I've seen about 380 failures."
  2. Prof: "Does stopping at a fixed number of failures bias the estimate?"
    You: "Slightly. The number of inputs is then random, negative binomial, and failures over inputs comes out a little too high on average. Haldane showed that r minus 1, over n minus 1, is exactly unbiased. With 380 failures the bias is about 0.3 percent, but with only 10 failures it would be about 11 percent."
  3. Prof: "In your plan a 2-out-of-3 vote fails much less often than one run. How many inputs would you need to measure the vote directly?"
    You: "If runs failed independently at p = 0.01, the vote would fail with about 0.0003, so seeing 380 vote failures would take about 1.3 million inputs. Shared failures make the vote fail more often, which helps a little, but it stays much rarer than a single failure. That's why I estimate the vote from the fitted model, which uses every run's result on every input, not only the rare inputs where two of three fail."

P28. Central limit theorem

(a) 100 i.i.d. task durations with mean 2 min and variance 4. P(total > 220 min)? (b) X ~ Binomial(100, 0.5). Use the normal approximation with continuity correction for P(X >= 60). Compare with the exact value.

Solution. (a) Sum: mean 200, variance 400, sd 20. P(sum > 220) is about P(Z > 1) = 0.159. (b) Mean 50, sd 5. P(X >= 60) = P(X > 59.5) is about P(Z > 1.9) = 0.0287. Exact: 0.0284.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why 59.5 and not 60?"
    You: "The binomial only takes whole numbers, so P of X at least 60 is the same as P of X above 59. The normal is continuous, so I treat each whole number k as the bar from k minus a half to k plus a half, and start at 59.5. Without the correction I'd get P of Z above 2, 0.0228, much further from the exact 0.0284."
  2. Prof: "Would it work as well for rare failures, say a Binomial of 100 and 0.02, and P of at least 5?"
    You: "No. The mean is 2 and the standard deviation 1.4, so the count is skewed and pressed against zero. Even with the correction the normal gives about 0.037, while the exact value is 0.051 and the Poisson with mean 2 gives 0.053, much closer. For rare failures the Poisson is the right approximation."
  3. Prof: "Then how would you put a confidence interval on a failure probability when failures are rare?"
    You: "Not with p hat plus or minus 1.96 standard errors: for 2 failures in 100 runs that gives minus 0.007 to 0.047, which goes below zero. I'd use the exact Clopper-Pearson interval, built from Beta quantiles, which gives 0.002 to 0.070, or the Wilson interval, 0.006 to 0.070. Both stay inside 0 to 1 and are honest about how little 2 failures tell me."

P29. Second failure of three (TMR)

Three i.i.d. Exponential(lambda) units. A 2-out-of-3 system fails at the second unit failure. Its mean life?

Solution. First failure: rate 3 lambda, mean 1/(3 lambda). Then two units remain (memoryless): rate 2 lambda, mean 1/(2 lambda). E = 1/(3 lambda) + 1/(2 lambda) = 5/(6 lambda), less than one unit's 1/lambda.

Check. Simulation with lambda = 1: 0.831. Same answer as in 04_stochastic_processes_and_reliability.md R21.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "So TMR lives shorter on average than one unit. Why would anyone use it?"
    You: "Because it is more reliable early on. Its reliability is 3R squared minus 2R cubed, with R equal to e to the minus lambda t, and that beats R whenever R is above one half, so for t below ln 2 over lambda, about 0.69 over lambda. For a short mission, say lambda t = 0.1, one unit fails with 0.095 but TMR only with 0.025."
  2. Prof: "Now add repair: a failed unit is repaired at rate mu while the other two keep running. What is the mean life?"
    You: "I'd use a Markov chain with states 3 up, 2 up and failed: from 3 it moves to 2 at rate 3 lambda, and from 2 it goes back at rate mu or fails at rate 2 lambda. Solving the two mean-time equations gives 5 lambda plus mu, over 6 lambda squared. With mu = 100 lambda the mean life jumps from 0.83 over lambda to 17.5 over lambda, assuming the units fail independently and repair is perfect."
  3. Prof: "And for three program versions voting, does that independence assumption hold?"
    You: "Not in general. Software doesn't wear out, so the clock is the stream of inputs: on one input the vote fails with 3 theta squared minus 2 theta cubed, the same polynomial with the failure chance in place of R. Averaged over inputs, when failure chances are small, the spread Var theta makes that larger than for independent versions, and measuring that spread is exactly my plan."

P30. Splitting Poisson failures

Module A has X ~ Poisson(2) failures and module B has Y ~ Poisson(3), independent. There were 5 failures in total. P(exactly 2 were in A)?

Solution. Given X + Y = 5, X ~ Binomial(5, 2/5):

P(X = 2 | X + Y = 5) = P(X = 2) P(Y = 3) / P(X + Y = 5)
                     = [e^-2 2^2/2!][e^-3 3^3/3!] / [e^-5 5^5/5!]
                     = C(5,2) (2/5)^2 (3/5)^3 = 10 * 0.16 * 0.216 = 0.3456

0.346. Each of the 5 failures is from A with probability 2/5, independently.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Turn it around: a Poisson stream with 5 failures on average, each going to A with chance 2 over 5. What can you say about the two counts?"
    You: "They are independent Poissons with means 2 and 3. That is Poisson thinning, and the independence is the surprising part: a week with many failures in A tells me nothing about B."
  2. Prof: "Does that independence survive if the total is not Poisson, say exactly 5 failures every week?"
    You: "No. In general the covariance of the two counts is p times 1 minus p, times Var N minus E N. A fixed total has variance 0, so the covariance is 0.24 times minus 5, which is minus 1.2: Y is just 5 minus X. The Poisson, with variance equal to its mean, is exactly the case where the covariance is zero."
  3. Prof: "And if the total is overdispersed, as real failure counts often are?"
    You: "Then the covariance is positive: with mean 5 and variance 10 it is 0.24 times 5, which is 1.2, a correlation of about 0.33. That is what happens when the weekly rate itself varies, for example with the amount of testing, and given that rate the two counts are still independent Poissons. So before calling it a shared fault I'd condition on the testing effort, the same lesson as in my plan, where a shared input makes runs that are independent given it fail together."
