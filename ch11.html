Chapter 11 of 45

Hard committee questions on your plan

When and how long: Thu 19 Nov: H1 to H25. Fri 20 Nov: H26 to H47.

What this file is: 47 new questions about your research plan, the kind a committee member from outside the lab, or a skeptic, would ask. They are different from the 55 questions in 07_questions_on_my_proposal.md (Q1 to Q55); where one builds on an old question, it says so. Each has a short spoken answer in your voice and the one point the examiner is listening for.

How to use it:

  • Read the "Listening for" line first. If your own words hit that point, your answer is fine, even if it isn't the model answer.
  • Say the first sentence, then stop. These examiners want to see how you think under pressure, not a speech.
  • If you don't know: "I don't know yet. I'd find out by ..." is always a good answer here.
  • Facts come from the paper texts in paper_text/ (checked 12 Nov 2026; see 04_My_Proposal/04_every_number_in_my_plan.md). [CONFIRM] marks something only you can check.

Who asks what: the committee will likely include Prof. Zheng, and may include Prof. Okamura, Prof. Dohi and faculty from other labs in the programme. The roles below are guesses, to help you practise the tone. Outside members ask the "so what" and "why should I believe it" questions. Lab members ask the modelling ones.

A. Novelty and contribution

H1. (Committee member) In one sentence: what will we know after your thesis that we don't know now?

How strongly repeated coding-agent runs on real repository tasks fail together, as a size with an interval, and which change between runs lowers it most. From that, how many runs are worth voting over at a given cost.

Listening for: a measured quantity and a decision, not "I will study X".

H2. (Committee member) Take the context file out of your title. What's left, and is it still a thesis?

RQ1 is still there: coincident failures of agent runs on real tasks, ten runs per setting, and a fitted model of how often they fail together. The file is what makes it specific: it's the part of the specification every run shares, and RQ2 tests one rule directly. Without the file it's a careful follow-up of Ron et al. on real tasks. With it, it's a question I haven't seen asked, as far as I know.

Listening for: knows which part is the core (the RQ1 model) and which part is the hook (the file).

H3. (Prof. Okamura) The beta-binomial is a textbook model. What is new in your modelling?

The model isn't new, and I don't claim it is. It's a standard model for counts that vary more than a binomial allows. What's new is using it as the distribution of Eckhardt and Lee's θ for agent runs, fitting it on real tasks, and turning it into a vote failure probability and a cost-optimal number of runs.

Listening for: honesty that the tool is standard; the contribution is the application and the decision.

H4. (Committee member) Everyone already knows LLM outputs are correlated. So what?

We know they're not independent. We don't know how strongly on real repository tasks, or what lowers it. And the size matters for decisions: in Nogueira et al., voting got 0.43 of the possible gain overall, but only 0.27 when all three versions came from one model. A team choosing between rerunning the same agent and switching agents needs that number for its own setting.

Listening for: the difference between "not independent" and "how much, and what to do about it".

H5. (Prof. Dohi) Eckhardt and Lee already asked whether an optimal number of versions exists. Isn't your "best N" old?

Yes, the question is old. Their report has a figure on the existence of an optimal N, and a theorem on when N versions beat one on average. What's new is estimating the distribution of θ from real agent runs, and adding the cost of each run. Their N minimises the failure probability. Mine minimises run cost plus expected failure cost.

Listening for: knows the classic result (NASA TM-86369, Theorem 3 and Figure 4, "Existence of optimal N") and states the difference plainly.

H6. (Prof. Zheng) My group is already working on related questions. How do you avoid repeating our work?

I'd like to align the topic with you from the start. You said we might adjust it when I join. The part I can bring is the repository side: real tasks, context files, and running agents at scale and checking what they did. If your team's new results already answer part of RQ1, I'd build on them, not repeat them.

Listening for: flexibility, not defensiveness; knows what only the candidate can bring.

H7. (Committee member) Two close papers came out this summer. What if someone publishes your exact study before April?

Then I'd read it carefully and build on it, and the pilot would change. I'll keep checking arXiv before I start. The parts least likely to be repeated are the fitted dependence model and the cost decision, because the empirical papers so far stop at a test of independence.

Listening for: calm, and a plan, not "that won't happen".

B. Feasibility in two years

H8. (Committee member) What is the single riskiest step, technically?

Input generation for real functions. Many functions in real repositories take objects, files or settings, not plain numbers and strings. If only a few entry points can be fed with generated inputs, I'll have few usable tasks. The pilot checks this first, before any main runs.

Listening for: one concrete risk and an early check, not a list of everything.

H9. (Committee member) How long will it take you to prepare one task: the rule checker, the input generator, the hand checks?

I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. The pilot measures it on the first two tasks. If it's much slower than I hope, I cut the extra settings before I cut tasks.

Listening for: no fake precision; measure first, then decide what to cut.

H10. (Prof. Zheng) Suppose only RQ1 works, and on three tasks. Is that a master's thesis?

I think so, if it's careful: a fitted dependence model with intervals on real agent runs, compared with the Knight and Leveson z and Nogueira's pairwise score, and the vote failure and best N from it. Three tasks would limit what I can claim, and I'd say so clearly. I'd like your view on what is enough.

Listening for: knows the minimum result that still stands, and asks the supervisor.

C. Cost and who pays

H11. (Committee member) Your prices are for early-2026 models. By 2027 Sonnet 4.5 and GPT-5.2 may be gone. Then what?

Then I use the versions that exist when I start, pin them, and measure the real cost in the pilot. Nothing in the method depends on one model. The cost estimate is a size check, not a promise, and newer models may cost more: Zhang et al. report about five dollars per task with Claude Opus 4.6.

Listening for: the method is model-independent, and the cost is measured again, not assumed.

H12. (Committee member) If there is no budget at all, what is the smallest study that still answers RQ1?

Only my own harness with the open-weight model, at two temperatures. That keeps RQ1 for one agent with temperature as the change between runs, and RQ2 with the two file versions on that model. I'd lose the comparison across commercial agents, and I'd say that's a real loss. [CONFIRM: ask whether the lab has a GPU for a 30-billion-parameter model; don't assume it.]

Listening for: a real fallback, and honesty about what it loses.

D. Statistics, power and sample size

H13. (Prof. Okamura) How many runs per setting do you need to estimate ρ within plus or minus 0.1?

I haven't computed it yet. I'd do it by simulation before the pilot: draw θ from a Beta with plausible values of p and ρ, simulate k runs on many inputs, fit the model, bootstrap over runs, and see how wide the interval for ρ is at k = 10, 15 and 20. Then the pilot data replace my guesses.

Listening for: power by simulation, not a number from nowhere.

H14. (Prof. Zheng) What is your real sample size: 10,000 inputs, or 10 runs?

For ρ it's much closer to the runs, and to the number of different bugs. One buggy run fails on a whole region of inputs at once, so those inputs aren't independent pieces of information. That's why I bootstrap over runs, and why adding inputs alone won't make the interval for ρ much narrower.

Listening for: understands clustering and effective sample size.

H15. (Committee member) You compare four ways of changing runs. How do you avoid declaring "the agent matters more than the temperature" from noise?

I report each comparison with its bootstrap interval, and I only call one change better if the intervals clearly separate. The comparisons use the same tasks and the same inputs, which helps. With four comparisons, I'd either correct for multiple testing or present them as estimates, not as a ranking.

Listening for: intervals, paired data, and multiple comparisons.

H16. (Committee member) Will you fix your analysis before you see the data?

Yes. After the pilot and before the main runs, I'd write down the output rule, the input distributions, the models, the rules for dropping inputs and the comparisons. Anything I add later, I'd label as exploratory.

Listening for: a pre-registered analysis, and a clear line between planned and exploratory results.

H17. (Prof. Dohi) You quote means from Ron et al.: 387 failures for one version, 131 for a vote. Is the mean the right summary?

Not on its own. In their data most versions fail on no input at all, so the median is zero for both, and the mean is pulled up by a few very bad versions; the worst one fails on 10,469 of a million inputs. I'd report the share of failure-free versions, the median and the upper tail as well. Ron et al. also show that voting mainly cuts that tail.

Listening for: knows that skewed data need more than a mean.

E. Why a Beta distribution

These build on Q16 and Q17 in the old file (fit and moments).

H18. (Prof. Okamura) Is θ really continuous? With only a few bugs, θ takes only a few values.

That's fair. Each input falls in some failure region, roughly one per bug, and θ is about the same inside it. So a finite mixture, a latent-class model, is the more honest picture, and the Beta is a smooth approximation of it. If the data show a few clear clusters, I'd fit the mixture and compare the two by AIC and by how well each predicts the observed vote failures.

Listening for: understands where the variation in θ comes from, and has an alternative.

H19. (Prof. Okamura) With k = 10 runs per input, what can you identify about the distribution of θ?

Its first ten moments. If Y of k runs fail on an input, then C(Y, j) divided by C(k, j) has expected value E[θ^j], for any j up to k. The Beta needs only the first two moments. A richer model needs more, which is one more reason for ten runs and not three.

Listening for: knows that k limits what can be identified (factorial moments).

H20. (Prof. Dohi) Our lab has written about the Poisson binomial distribution. Your runs have different failure probabilities. Why beta-binomial and not Poisson binomial?

The Poisson binomial is for independent trials with different probabilities, so it models differences between runs. My question is dependence: runs failing together because they share an input that is hard for all of them. The beta-binomial models that, with one θ per input shared by every run. If runs also differ a lot from each other, a model with both parts could be a next step.

Listening for: separates "independent but different" from "dependent through a shared input". [CONFIRM: read the abstract of Dohi and Okamura's Japanese article on the Poisson binomial distribution, 07_Lab_Papers/pdfs/LAB_Dohi_2022_Poisson_Binomial_Distribution_Reliability.pdf, before you mention it.]

F. If the dependence is not what you expect

Q49 in the old file covers "ρ near zero". These are the other surprises.

H21. (Committee member) What if ρ is large, but voting still helps a lot, as in Ron et al.?

That's not a contradiction. Dependence lowers the gain from voting; it doesn't remove it. Ron et al. had strong dependence and still cut the mean failures to about a third. So I'd report both: ρ, and how much of the independent-case gain the vote really gets, like Nogueira's redundancy effectiveness.

Listening for: dependence does not mean no benefit; report both numbers.

H22. (Committee member) What if ρ is 0.9 on one task and near zero on the others?

Then the spread between tasks is the result, and I wouldn't average it away. I'd report each task, look at what the high one has that the others don't, for example a rule that forces one helper, and if I need one summary, use a hierarchical model that shows the spread between tasks.

Listening for: treats heterogeneity as a finding, not noise.

H23. (Prof. Zheng) Suppose your interval for ρ includes zero. What do you conclude?

That I can't rule out independence on that task, which isn't the same as showing independence. I'd report the upper end of the interval and what it means for the vote: for example, even at the upper end, how much worse the vote is than the independent formula says. If that's small, the practical answer is "voting works here", whatever the exact ρ is.

Listening for: "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence", and a practical reading through the upper bound.

G. When the checker or the oracle is wrong

Q38 in the old file covers "the merged code shares the bug". These are the other ways the measuring tools can fail.

H24. (Committee member) Your checker is validated on 20 examples. Suppose it's really only 85% accurate. How does that bias RQ2?

The checker only says whether the manipulation worked; it doesn't enter the failure probability. If it makes random mistakes in both file versions, the compliance difference looks smaller than it is, so I'd underestimate how much the rule changed behaviour, not overestimate it. The reliability numbers in RQ2 come from the generated inputs, not from the checker.

Listening for: random misclassification pulls toward no difference; the checker is not part of the outcome.

H25. (Committee member) You hand-label the examples that validate your own checker. Isn't that circular?

A little, yes. I'd label before I see the checker's output, and I'd ask a lab member to label a subset too, so I can report agreement between two people, not only between me and my own script.

Listening for: blind labelling and a second person.

H26. (Prof. Zheng) You drop inputs after looking at disagreements. Doesn't that let you shape the result?

It could, so the drop rule is written down before the main runs: drop an input only if it's outside the documented domain of the function, or if the merged code contradicts its own tests or docstring. The rule applies to all settings the same way, I report how many inputs I dropped, and I show the results with and without the drops.

Listening for: a rule fixed in advance, applied the same to every setting, plus a with-and-without check.

H. Limits of differential testing

H27. (Committee member) Strictly, you measure disagreement with one person's code. Why call it a failure probability?

Strictly, it's the probability of disagreeing with the merged reference under my declared input distribution. I call a disagreement a failure only after the hand check, and in the thesis I'd name it plainly: failure relative to the merged code. That's a limit on what my measure means, and I'll state it.

Listening for: precise naming (construct validity).

H28. (Committee member) What about a version that's correct but a hundred times slower, or never stops?

A run that doesn't finish within a fixed timeout counts as a failure, because an infinite loop can only be stopped, not detected. I'd set the timeout from the merged code's own time on that input, with a wide margin. Plain slowness isn't in my failure definition, so I'd report it separately.

Listening for: timeouts tied to the reference, and a clear scope for "failure".

H29. (Prof. Okamura) Every admitted version passes the same tests. Doesn't that acceptance step itself push the failures into the same untested places?

Yes, I think it does. If every admitted version passes the same tests, the failures that are left must sit where the tests don't look, so they cluster there and ρ goes up. Knight and Leveson's versions all passed one acceptance test too, so the same effect may be in their data. I can't remove it, but both file versions face the same tests, so the RQ2 comparison stays fair, and I report ρ as "given admission".

Listening for: sees that the selection step itself can create dependence.

I. Training-data leakage

Q39 in the old file covers the training cutoff. These go one step further.

H30. (Committee member) How would you notice that a run copied the merged code from memory?

I'd compare each run's diff with the merged diff. Near-identical diffs, much more often on old tasks than on new ones, would be the sign. Copying makes a run agree with the reference, so it would hide failures, which is why I report old and new tasks separately.

Listening for: a concrete check (diff similarity, before and after the cutoff).

H31. (Prof. Zheng) Memory could also make runs agree with each other. How do you separate dependence from memory from dependence from the context file?

In RQ2 the two file versions run on the same task with the same model, so whatever the model remembers is the same in both arms, and the difference between arms is the rule. For the overall ρ in RQ1 I can't separate them fully, but comparing tasks from before and after the cutoff shows how much memory adds.

Listening for: the within-task contrast holds memory fixed; the cutoff split estimates the rest.

J. Beyond Python

Q40 in the old file covers "why only Python". These are the follow-ups.

H32. (Committee member) Many Python functions have no type hints, or use Any. Doesn't that bias you toward simple functions?

Yes. Functions with clear types are easier to feed, so my sample leans toward them. I'd count how many entry points I could use and how many I couldn't, and report both. Where hints are missing, I can take the types from the values the tests pass in.

Listening for: names the selection bias and measures it.

H33. (Committee member) Would your results carry over to a typed language like Rust or Java?

The method would: the same model and the same vote formula. The numbers might not, because types catch some bugs before the code runs, so the failures left could be fewer and different. Ron et al. found shared failures in Python, Rust and Pascal, which suggests the problem isn't just a Python problem.

Listening for: separates the method from the numbers, and uses evidence.

K. Licensing and ethics

Licences and author names are in 10_other_likely_questions.md Q27 and Q28. These are the other ethics questions.

H34. (Committee member) Could your runs ever touch the real projects: push code, open pull requests, bother maintainers?

No. Each run is in a container with no git remotes and no credentials, so it can't push or open anything, and I never contact maintainers through the agents. The only thing that leaves the container is the diff, which I keep for the analysis.

Listening for: full isolation, no side effects on real projects or people.

H35. (Committee member) What if your hand checks find a real bug in a project's merged code?

Then I'd report it to the maintainers the way the project asks, as an issue, or privately if it looks security-related, before I publish anything about it. That's separate from the agent failures, which are in code that was never merged.

Listening for: responsible disclosure.

L. Reproducibility as models change

H36. (Committee member) The agents update every few weeks and models get retired. How can anyone check your numbers in 2028?

They can redo my analysis exactly, because I'll release every diff, every input and every pass or fail result. Regenerating the runs is harder. I pin and record every version, and my open-weight harness can be rerun exactly. For the hosted models, the reusable part is the method, not the exact numbers.

Listening for: separates reproducing the analysis from regenerating the runs.

H37. (Prof. Okamura) Even with pinned versions, the model behind an API name can change during your runs. How would you notice?

I'd mix the settings over time instead of running one setting after another, so a change hits all of them equally, and I'd rerun one fixed task every day as a check and watch its pass rate. If it jumps, something changed underneath.

Listening for: randomised run order plus a fixed daily check task.

M. Why these agents

Q26 in the old file covers why Claude Code, Codex and your own harness. This is the skeptic's version.

H38. (Committee member) Cursor, Copilot and Gemini CLI are popular too. Isn't picking the two agents you use every day just convenience?

Partly, yes, and I'll say so. But they also read the two most common file names, CLAUDE.md and AGENTS.md, and Gloaguen et al. report their costs on these exact tasks. My harness setup is meant to make adding an agent cheap, so if the budget allows, a third commercial agent is the first thing I'd add.

Listening for: admits the convenience, gives real reasons, and shows the design can grow.

N. What "reliability" means here

Q3 in the old file asks whether this is reliability or software engineering. These ask what the word means in your plan.

H39. (Prof. Dohi) Define reliability in your study precisely. Over time, or over inputs?

Over inputs, per demand. The reliability of one admitted version is one minus its failure probability under my declared input distribution, and for the vote it's one minus the vote's failure probability. Only the field study is over time, where the NHPP counts the faults found by calendar time.

Listening for: per-demand reliability, and awareness that classic reliability growth models are time-based.

H40. (Committee member) Before anything is merged, a human reviews it. Isn't the reviewer the real safety net? Why measure before review?

The reviewer is a safety net, but a reviewer reads the diff and the tests. A bug on an input nobody tested is easy to miss, and Xia and Miller's numbers suggest some get through: agent lines got more bug fixes after merge. My measure would tell a reviewer how far to trust several runs that all agree.

Listening for: the study supports review; it doesn't replace it.

O. Helping practitioners

H41. (Committee member) A team won't run 10,000 inputs. What would a team actually do differently after reading your thesis?

If the results go the way I expect, three things. If runs share a design forced by a rule, rerunning the same agent buys little, so switch the agent or the model instead. Check rules that force one helper, because they can copy one bug into every run. And add cheap generated-input tests beyond the PR's tests before trusting runs that agree.

Listening for: actionable advice, honestly conditioned on the results.

H42. (Committee member) Why not just tell the agent to "be different" each time?

Nogueira et al. tried different prompting strategies, and it helped little: 0.41 against 0.44 at three versions, while changing the model made the biggest difference. I could add a "be different" setting in my own harness cheaply, but the evidence so far says the model and the agent matter more than the wording.

Listening for: answers with evidence, not opinion.

P. Connection to the lab's past work

09_questions_why_japan_why_this_lab.md Q18 lists which lab papers your plan uses. These ask how deep the connection goes.

H43. (Prof. Zheng) My work includes sensitivity analysis. Is there a sensitivity question in your plan?

Yes. The second part of RQ1 is one: how much does the vote failure move when I change one factor between runs? In the model it's direct. Two runs both fail with p² + ρ p(1 - p), so the change per unit of ρ is p(1 - p). For the vote and for the cost-optimal N it's less simple, and that's something I'd like to learn to do properly here.

Listening for: a concrete link to sensitivity, with an actual derivative.

H44. (Prof. Okamura) Our lab has proportional-intensity reliability models with covariates. How is your ω_j = L_j exp(β′z_j) related?

It's the same basic idea: covariates multiply a baseline through an exponential, like in a Cox model. Those models put time-dependent development metrics into the intensity. I put each change's own features into how many faults it brings. pysrat's metrics-based models might already cover part of it, and I'd ask you.

Listening for: recognises the exp(β′z) proportional structure and links it to the lab. [CONFIRM: read the abstract of Li, Dohi and Okamura, Electronics 2022 (07_Lab_Papers/pdfs/LAB_Li_2022_Proportional_Intensity_SRMs_Covariates.pdf) before you name it.]

H45. (Prof. Dohi) Our lab asked whether infinite-failure NHPP models are useful. In your field model, how would you choose between finite and infinite?

By fitting both and checking which predicts later fixes better: fit on the earlier months, predict the next ones, and compare prediction error and AIC. My infinite-server form holds both cases. If changes stop arriving, it levels off like a finite model. If they keep arriving, it keeps growing.

Listening for: model choice by prediction, and knowing the queue form covers both cases. [CONFIRM: you know Li, Dohi and Okamura, "Are Infinite-Failure NHPP-Based Software Reliability Models Useful?" (Software, 2023) only from its abstract. Say so if asked.]

H46. (Committee member) In the field study, a commit message that says "fix" isn't always a fault fix. How does label noise affect your fit?

Wrong labels add fake fault times and miss real ones, so the fitted fault counts can be off in either direction. I'd check a random sample of labelled commits by hand, report how many are really fixes, and see whether the fitted model changes when I drop the doubtful ones. Xia and Miller used a rule-based and LLM-assisted classifier and list the same limit.

Listening for: measures the label error and checks how much it matters.

H47. (Prof. Zheng) My lab studies software aging. Does anything "age" in a coding agent?

Maybe. As an engineer I see long agent sessions get worse as the context fills up, and a fresh session helps, which looks a little like aging and rejuvenation. I haven't measured it and it isn't in my plan, so it's only a hunch. [CONFIRM: say this only if it matches your own experience.]

Listening for: can connect ideas across fields, and flags speculation as speculation.
