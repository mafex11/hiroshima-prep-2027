Part 5: Maths

Chapter 41 of 62

How to answer math questions out loud (online meeting)

When and how long: Before day 1, 15 minutes.

This adapts your NAIST guide (from_NAIST, file 03: how to do math out loud) to the Hiroshima format. The NAIST format puts two problems on screen. Hiroshima is different: about 15 minutes of presentation, then about 15 minutes of questions from the professors, on Teams or Zoom. On 2 Nov Prof. Zheng said he thinks most of the Q&A will focus on your proposal, but that it is "maybe not limited to" the proposal (speech-to-text wording) and depends on which professors join. So a math question will come out loud, in the middle of a conversation, and you will have about one or two minutes for it.

The goal is the same as at NAIST: the professors should be able to follow your thinking at every moment.

The few numbers in this file come from files 04 and 05 and were checked there (13 Nov 2026). The one claim I am not sure of is marked [CHECK].

1. What a math question will probably look like

From most to least likely, given your plan and this lab:

  1. "Explain this formula on your slide." For example: why E[theta^2] = p^2 + Var theta, what rho means, why the vote fails with E[3 theta^2 - 2 theta^3]. (Answers: 05_math_inside_my_research_plan.md.)
  2. "What is ...?" A definition: an NHPP, maximum likelihood, a p value, a Markov chain, the bootstrap, Cohen's kappa.
  3. "Can you show us how you get ...?" A short derivation: the binomial variance, the MLE of an exponential rate, the steady state of a two-state chain, the minimum of independent exponentials.
  4. "What happens if ...?" A limit or special case: what if rho = 0, what if N grows, what if no input fails.
  5. A general basics question (rare): an eigenvalue, an integral by parts, Big-O of quicksort.

2. The answer shape: short answer first, then offer more

For a definition or a "why" question:

  1. One sentence answer. "An NHPP is a Poisson process whose rate changes with time."
  2. One example or formula. "In Goel-Okumoto, m(t) = omega (1 - e^(-bt)), so the rate falls as faults are found."
  3. One link to your plan, if natural. "My field model is a sum of such terms, one per merged change."
  4. Stop. Look at the camera and let them ask more.

For a derivation:

  1. Short answer first: "It comes out as p squared plus the variance of theta."
  2. Offer: "Shall I derive it? It takes about a minute." If they say yes, go to section 3.
  3. This keeps you from spending three of your fifteen minutes on something they did not want.

3. Deriving on paper while you talk

Setup (prepare once, test this week).

  • Plain white A4 paper (a pad or sketchbook) and a thick black marker, about 2 to 3 mm tip. Write 6 to 10 lines per page. That feels too big; it is right for a webcam.
  • Light from the front, nothing bright behind you. Test with a recorded call: can you read a minus sign, an exponent, a fraction bar and the letter "theta" written as a symbol?
  • Hold the paper flat and still, filling most of the frame, for 3 to 5 seconds.
  • Mirroring: Teams and Zoom usually show you a mirrored self-view, so your writing looks backwards to you, but the professors see it the right way round. Do not flip the page. Test this in a call with a friend. [CHECK: confirm on the exact app and settings you will use]
  • Prof. Zheng will send the details of the meeting. Even so, ask before writing: "May I write this on paper and show it to the camera?" Have blank paper ready on the desk.

While you write.

  • Say each step as you write it, in words: "Given the input, the two runs are independent, so both fail with theta squared."
  • Write the plan in one line at the top: "P(both) = E[ P(both | X) ]".
  • Write big, leave space, make minus signs long. Write "theta" as a clear symbol, not a squiggle.
  • Look up at the camera now and then. A silent head looking down for 30 seconds feels long on a call.
  • Box the final result, then say it as a sentence and hold the page up: "So two runs both fail with p squared plus the variance of theta."

If they prefer no paper: say the derivation in words, line by line, as you would dictate it. Practise this for D1, D3 and D5 in file 05. It is harder than writing, so it is worth one rehearsal each.

4. Checks to say out loud

A quick check shows you understand the result, not only that you remember it. Pick one:

Check Example
Special case "If theta is the same for every input, Var theta is zero and I get p squared back, the independent case."
Small N "For N = 1 the vote formula should give p, and it does."
Limits in time "At t = 0, m(t) is 0; as t grows it goes to omega. That makes sense for a finite number of faults."
Range "This is a probability and it stays between 0 and 1: 3 theta squared minus 2 theta cubed is 0 at theta = 0 and 1 at theta = 1."
Direction "More runs should not make the vote worse when theta is below one half, and the formula agrees."
Units "lambda is per hour, so lambda t has no units, as an exponent must."
Sum to 1 "The steady-state probabilities add to one: 0.961 + 0.038 + 0.001." (file 04, R12)
Size "The answer 0.057 is between the independent 0.028 and the single-run 0.1, which is what I expected."

5. When you are stuck

Never stay silent for more than 10 to 15 seconds. Climb this ladder out loud:

  1. Say the definition of the key word. Often the definition is the method. "Variance is E of X squared minus the square of E X."
  2. Condition on something. In this lab's math, the trick is usually to fix the input (or the state, or the number of events) and then average: law of total probability or total expectation.
  3. Try the simplest case. One run, N = 1, two states, theta constant, t = 0.
  4. Work backwards from what is asked. "To get P(vote fails) I need P(J >= 2 | theta), which is a binomial tail."
  5. Ask for a hint, early. It costs little and keeps the conversation moving.
  6. If only arithmetic is left, give the structure and stop: "The rest is solving one equation numerically for b; that's what I'd do in pysrat or scipy."

If you really do not know the topic, say so, then show how you would start:

"I haven't studied that yet. My guess is that it works like ..., and I would check it in [book or paper]."

That is the same honest line as in 04_My_Proposal/03_weak_points_and_honest_answers.md. It is much better than bluffing.

6. When a professor speaks

  • "Are you sure?" It usually means the last line has a slip. Say "Let me check", and redo the last step slowly. If you find it: "Thank you, the sign was wrong." If not: "I get the same again, because ... . Please tell me if I missed something."
  • A hint: thank them, repeat it in your words, use it at once.
  • A question you did not understand (accents, a bad line): "Sorry, could you repeat the question?" or "Do you mean the variance of J or of theta?" That is normal and costs nothing.
  • A follow-up: short answer, one reason, stop.

7. Ten phrases to use

  1. "Let me make sure I understood: you're asking about ... ?"
  2. "The short answer is ... . Shall I derive it? It takes about a minute."
  3. "May I write this on paper and hold it up to the camera?"
  4. "I'll start from the definition."
  5. "The trick is to fix the input first and then average over inputs."
  6. "Let me check it with a special case: if rho is zero, I should get the independent formula back, and I do."
  7. "I'm not sure about the exact constant, but the shape of the answer is ... ."
  8. "Sorry, I made a mistake in the last line. It should be ... ."
  9. "Could you give me a small hint about where to start?"
  10. "I haven't studied that yet. I would start by ..., and it's in my Year 1 plan."

Avoid: "obviously", "trivially", "it's easy", long silences, and guessing a number without saying you are guessing.

8. A sample exchange

Professor: "On your slide, the number of failing runs is beta-binomial. What is its variance?"

You: "The short answer: k p (1 - p) times 1 plus (k - 1) rho, so it's bigger than the binomial variance whenever rho is above zero. Shall I show where it comes from?"

Professor: "Please."

You: "May I write it on paper?" (writes) "I use the law of total variance. Given theta, J is binomial, so the conditional variance is k theta (1 - theta), and the conditional mean is k theta. So Var J is E of k theta (1 - theta), plus the variance of k theta. The first part is k times p minus E theta squared. The second is k squared Var theta. Putting E theta squared = p squared + Var theta in, I get k p (1 - p) plus k (k - 1) Var theta." (holds up the page) "And with rho = Var theta over p (1 - p), that's k p (1 - p) times 1 plus (k - 1) rho. Quick check: if rho is zero, it's the binomial variance, as it should be."

Why it works: short answer first, an offer, a named tool (total variance), each step said in words, a check, and a stop.

9. Practice drills (10 minutes a day, from 13 Nov)

  • Derive out loud, on paper, with a 2-minute timer: one of D1, D3, D4, D5, D6, D7, D10, D11, D14 from file 05. Record yourself on Teams or Zoom once and check that the page is readable.
  • Rapid definitions (2 minutes): pick five words at random (NHPP, MLE, p value, bootstrap, kappa, generator matrix, hazard rate, availability, eigenvalue, Big-O) and give each a one-sentence answer.
  • Practice Q&A twice, by 23 Nov (days 9 and 11 of the plan in file 00): someone (a friend, or Claude playing the professors) asks 5 questions mixing your plan and basics, says "Are you sure?" once at random, and asks one question you cannot answer, so you practise the honest line.
  • Review the recording: short answer first? Silent more than 15 seconds? Page readable? Did you do a check? Did you stop?
