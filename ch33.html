Linear algebra and calculus: the must-know list

When and how long: Day 10.

The Hiroshima Q&A is about 15 minutes, spoken, and mostly about your plan, so a 10-minute integral is unlikely (the exact form of any maths question is not known yet). But an informatics professor may check basics in one or two sentences ("What is an eigenvalue?", "Is this matrix invertible?", "What is the integral of x e^(-ax)?"). This file lists what you must be able to say, points to solved problems in from_NAIST/, and adds 15 new problems on the topics your NAIST set did not cover: generator matrices, the matrix exponential, Markov chain ODEs, partial derivatives, Lagrange multipliers, double integrals and reliability ODEs.

How the answers were checked. Every answer to the 15 new problems was computed on 12 Nov 2026 with Python and recomputed on 13 Nov 2026: sympy for the symbolic integrals, the ODE solutions, the critical point and the matrix exponential; numpy for the numbers (matrix powers, eigenvalues, numerical integration). All 15 answers matched. The NAIST problems were checked with SymPy when they were written (see the note at the top of that file). Nothing in this file is marked [CHECK].

Where the solved problems are. from_NAIST/02_practice_problems_with_solutions.md, problems LA1 to LA25 (linear algebra) and C1 to C25 (calculus). The statements in from_NAIST/01_topic_checklist_and_key_formulas.md give more detail. The new problems below are L1 to L15.

Part 1. The must-know list

Cover the right-hand side and say each one out loud in one or two sentences.

Linear algebra

Topic Say it Solved problems
Linear independence "Vectors are independent if the only combination that gives zero is the one with all coefficients zero." LA2
Basis and dimension "A basis is an independent set that spans the space. Every basis has the same number of vectors, the dimension." LA2, LA10
Rank "The rank is the number of pivots: the dimension of the column space, which equals that of the row space. Rank plus the dimension of the nullspace is the number of columns." LA7, LA8, LA10
Determinant "The factor by which the matrix scales volume, with a sign. It is zero exactly when the matrix is singular. det(AB) = det A det B, and det(cA) = c^n det A." LA3, LA16, LA17
Inverse "A^(-1) exists exactly when det A is not zero, equivalently rank n. For 2 by 2: 1/(ad - bc) times [d -b; -c a]. (AB)^(-1) = B^(-1) A^(-1)." LA4, LA5, LA18
Eigenvalues and eigenvectors "Ax = lambda x with x not zero. The eigenvalues solve det(A - lambda I) = 0. Their sum is the trace and their product is the determinant." LA19, LA20, L1
Diagonalisation "If A has n independent eigenvectors, A = S Lambda S^(-1), so A^k = S Lambda^k S^(-1) and e^(At) = S e^(Lambda t) S^(-1). Distinct eigenvalues guarantee it." LA19, LA21, L2, L4
Symmetric matrices "Real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors: A = Q Lambda Q^T (spectral theorem)." LA22
Positive definite "Symmetric with x^T A x > 0 for every nonzero x. Equivalently all eigenvalues positive, or all upper-left determinants positive. A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite." LA23, L5, L6
Inner product and orthogonality "x . y = sum x_i y_i = norm(x) norm(y) cos(angle). Orthogonal means the inner product is zero. Cauchy-Schwarz: abs(x . y) <= norm(x) norm(y)." LA1, LA11, LA14
Least squares "Minimise norm(Ax - b)^2, the sum of squared errors. The normal equations A^T A x-hat = A^T b make the error orthogonal to the columns of A. This is linear regression." LA12, LA13, 03_statistics.md S20
Markov matrices "Strang's Markov matrices have columns that sum to 1. Probability books use rows that sum to 1 and write pi P = pi. Either way, 1 is an eigenvalue and its eigenvector is the steady state." LA20, L4
Generator matrices "A CTMC generator has rates off the diagonal and rows that sum to 0, so 0 is always an eigenvalue, and the steady state is its left eigenvector." L1, L2

Calculus

Topic Say it Solved problems
Limits "The value f(x) approaches as x approaches a. For 0/0 forms use L'Hopital or a Taylor expansion. (1 + x/n)^n tends to e^x." C2, C4, L15
Derivatives "The limit of the difference quotient: the slope of the tangent. Product, quotient and chain rules." C3, C6, C7
Taylor series "f(x) = sum f^(n)(a) (x - a)^n / n!. e^x = 1 + x + x^2/2 + ..., ln(1 + x) = x - x^2/2 + ..., 1/(1 - x) = 1 + x + x^2 + ..." C21, L15
Integrals, substitution, by parts "The fundamental theorem links integrals and antiderivatives. By parts: integral u dv = u v - integral v du." C14, C15, C16, L8
Improper integrals, Gamma function "An integral to infinity is a limit. integral_0^infinity x^n e^(-ax) dx = n!/a^(n+1), and Gamma(n) = (n - 1)!, Gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi)." C20, L9, L14
Partial derivatives and gradient "A partial derivative changes one variable and holds the others fixed. The gradient is the vector of partials. It points uphill fastest, and it is zero at a critical point." L6
Hessian and the second-derivative test "The matrix of second partials. Positive definite at a critical point means a minimum, negative definite a maximum." L6
Lagrange multipliers "To optimise f subject to g = 0, solve grad f = lambda grad g with g = 0." L7
Double integrals "Integrate in one variable, then the other (Fubini). You may swap the order if you redraw the region. In polar coordinates dx dy = r dr d(angle)." L12, L13, L14
ODEs "y' = -a y gives y = y0 e^(-at). A linear first-order equation is solved with an integrating factor. A system u' = Au is solved by u(t) = e^(At) u(0)." C25, L3, L10, L11

Part 2. 15 new problems with full solutions

L1. Eigenvalues of a generator matrix

A machine is up (state 0) or down (state 1). It fails at rate lambda and is repaired at rate mu. The generator is Q = [-lambda lambda; mu -mu]. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Why is 0 always an eigenvalue? What does its left eigenvector mean?

Solution.

  • trace Q = -(lambda + mu), det Q = lambda mu - lambda mu = 0. So the eigenvalues are 0 and -(lambda + mu).
  • Each row sums to 0, so Q (1, 1)^T = 0. Right eigenvector (1, 1) for 0.
  • For -(lambda + mu): Q (lambda, -mu)^T = (-lambda^2 - lambda mu, mu lambda + mu^2) = -(lambda + mu)(lambda, -mu). Eigenvector (lambda, -mu).
  • Left eigenvector for 0: pi Q = 0 with pi = (mu, lambda)/(lambda + mu). Check: mu(-lambda) + lambda(mu) = 0. This is the steady state: the machine is up a share mu/(lambda + mu) of the time.

Say it. "Rows of a generator sum to zero, so the all-ones vector gives eigenvalue zero. The other eigenvalue is minus lambda minus mu, from the trace. The left eigenvector for zero is the steady state."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the left eigenvector for zero is the steady state. Why the left one and not the right one?"
    You: "Because I write the distribution as a row vector, and it changes by p prime of t equals p of t times Q. A distribution that stops changing needs p times Q to be zero, with the row vector on the left of Q. The right eigenvector, all ones, only says each row sums to zero, so the total probability stays one."
  2. Prof: "But Q is singular, so pi Q = 0 has a whole line of solutions. How do you pick one, and when is it unique?"
    You: "I replace one of the equations with the condition that the entries sum to one, and solve. The answer is unique when the chain is irreducible, meaning every state can reach every other. If it split into two closed groups, zero would be a double eigenvalue and the long run would depend on the start. Here it gives the availability, mu over (lambda plus mu)."
  3. Prof: "Take lambda = 0.01 and mu = 0.5 per hour. Which raises that availability more: halving lambda or doubling mu?"
    You: "Exactly the same. Availability is 1 over (1 plus lambda over mu), and both changes halve that ratio, taking it from 0.9804 to 0.9901. Per unit of rate it's very different: the derivative in lambda is minus mu over (lambda plus mu) squared, about minus 1.92, and in mu it's lambda over (lambda plus mu) squared, about 0.038. Prof. Zheng's 2015 availability importance paper with Prof. Okamura and Prof. Dohi divides these derivatives by A, and there the failure-rate measure was larger for every component."

L2. The matrix exponential e^(Qt)

For the same Q, find P(t) = e^(Qt). Give P_00(4) for lambda = 0.01 per hour and mu = 0.5 per hour.

Solution (diagonalise). Write s = lambda + mu.

S = [1 lambda; 1 -mu]   (eigenvectors as columns),   Lambda = diag(0, -s)
S^(-1) = (1/s) [mu lambda; 1 -1]
e^(Qt) = S diag(1, e^(-st)) S^(-1)
       = (1/s) [ mu + lambda e^(-st)    lambda - lambda e^(-st) ]
               [ mu - mu e^(-st)        lambda + mu e^(-st)     ]

Faster trick. Q^2 = -s Q (check: Q^2 = s [lambda -lambda; -mu mu]). So Q^n = (-s)^(n-1) Q, and the series gives

e^(Qt) = I + Q (1 - e^(-st)) / s

Numbers: s = 0.51. P_00(4) = (0.5 + 0.01 e^(-2.04)) / 0.51 = 0.98294. As t grows, both rows tend to (mu, lambda)/s = (0.98039, 0.01961).

Check. Computing e^(4Q) numerically matches all four entries. Each row of P(t) sums to 1, and P(0) = I.

Say it. "I diagonalise Q: eigenvalues 0 and minus s. Then e^(Qt) is S times diag of 1 and e^(-st) times S inverse. Every entry is a constant plus a term that decays like e^(-st), and the constant is the steady state."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Your faster trick used Q squared equals minus s Q. Why is that true? Is it luck?"
    You: "It isn't luck. The eigenvalues are 0 and minus s, so the characteristic polynomial is x times (x plus s), and by Cayley-Hamilton Q satisfies it: Q squared plus s Q is zero. Every 2-state generator has this, so every power of Q is a multiple of Q and the series for e to the Q t collapses."
  2. Prof: "A real availability model has a hundred states, so that trick is gone. How would you compute e to the Q t then?"
    You: "A standard method is uniformisation. I pick a rate q at least as big as the largest exit rate, and P equal to I plus Q over q is then an ordinary transition matrix. Then e to the Q t is the sum over n of Poisson probabilities with mean q t, times P to the n. Every term is non-negative, so nothing cancels, and the Poisson tail bounds the error from stopping early."
  3. Prof: "You said the Poisson tail bounds the error. How many terms would you need for our example at t = 4?"
    You: "The largest exit rate is mu, 0.5, so q t is 2 and the weights are Poisson with mean 2. Keeping n from 0 to 12 leaves a tail of about 2 in 10 million, so 13 terms give six correct decimals. The count grows like q t plus a few times its square root, so a stiff model, with fast repairs over a long time horizon, needs thousands of terms or more."

L3. Solving the 2-state CTMC equation directly

With p_0(t) = P(up at t), the forward equation is p_0'(t) = -lambda p_0 + mu (1 - p_0), p_0(0) = 1. Solve it. Find the availability at t = 4 h (lambda = 0.01, mu = 0.5) and how fast it settles.

Solution.

p_0' = mu - (lambda + mu) p_0           (linear, constant coefficients)
Steady state: p_0' = 0  ->  p_0 = mu/(lambda + mu) = mu/s
Let q = p_0 - mu/s.  Then q' = -s q, so q(t) = q(0) e^(-st) = (1 - mu/s) e^(-st) = (lambda/s) e^(-st)
p_0(t) = mu/s + (lambda/s) e^(-st)

A(4) = 0.98039 + 0.01961 e^(-2.04) = 0.98294, the same as P_00(4) in L2. The gap to the steady state halves every ln 2 / s = 1.36 h.

Check. sympy's dsolve gives the same function, and p_0(0) = mu/s + lambda/s = 1.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the gap halves every 1.36 hours. What controls that speed, failures or repairs?"
    You: "Almost entirely repairs. The decay rate is lambda plus mu, 0.51, and mu is 0.5 of it, so the machine settles on the time scale of one repair, about 2 hours, even though it fails only about once every 100 hours."
  2. Prof: "So over the first few hours the start still matters. What is the average availability over the first 4 hours?"
    You: "I integrate p zero from 0 to 4 and divide by 4. That gives mu over s, plus lambda times (1 minus e to the minus 2.04), over 4 s squared, which is about 0.9888. It's above the steady state of 0.9804 because the machine starts up, and like the whole Markov model it assumes repair times are exponential."
  3. Prof: "What if a repair always takes exactly 2 hours instead?"
    You: "Then it's no longer a Markov chain, because how long a repair has been running matters. The long-run availability doesn't change: by renewal theory it's mean up time over mean cycle time, 100 over 102, still 0.9804. The transient curve does change, and I'd approximate the fixed repair time by a chain of exponential phases, the phase expansion that Prof. Zheng, Prof. Okamura and Prof. Dohi used in their 2020 paper on rejuvenation and checkpointing."

L4. Powers of a 2-state DTMC

P = [1 - alpha, alpha; beta, 1 - beta] with alpha = 0.1, beta = 0.3 (rows sum to 1). Find the eigenvalues, a formula for P^n, P^3 entry (0, 0), and the limit.

Solution. Eigenvalues: 1 (rows sum to 1) and trace - 1 = 1 - alpha - beta = 0.6.

P^n = (1/(alpha + beta)) [beta alpha; beta alpha] + ((1 - alpha - beta)^n / (alpha + beta)) [alpha -alpha; -beta beta]
(P^3)_00 = (0.3 + 0.1 (0.6)^3) / 0.4 = (0.3 + 0.0216)/0.4 = 0.804
Limit: every row -> (beta, alpha)/(alpha + beta) = (0.75, 0.25)

Check. numpy's matrix_power gives 0.804. Note the convention: here pi_(n+1) = pi_n P with row vectors. Strang (LA20) uses columns that sum to 1 and u_(k+1) = A u_k. They are transposes of each other.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the second eigenvalue is the trace minus 1. Why?"
    You: "Rows sum to one, so P times the all-ones vector gives the all-ones vector back, which is eigenvalue 1. The eigenvalues add up to the trace, 2 minus alpha minus beta, so the other one is 1 minus alpha minus beta, which is 0.6 here."
  2. Prof: "And what does that 0.6 do to P to the n?"
    You: "It sets how fast the chain forgets where it started: the gap to the limit is multiplied by 0.6 every step. Entry (0, 0) is 0.75 plus 0.25 times 0.6 to the n, so after 10 steps the gap is 0.25 times about 0.006, roughly 0.0015."
  3. Prof: "What if alpha plus beta is bigger than 1, say alpha 0.7 and beta 0.9, so that factor turns negative?"
    You: "Then the second eigenvalue is minus 0.6, so the gap still shrinks by 0.6 but flips sign each step: entry (0, 0) goes 0.3, 0.72, 0.468, 0.619, closing in on 0.5625 from both sides. At alpha = beta = 1 the eigenvalue is minus 1, the chain just alternates, and P to the n has no limit. The vector (1/2, 1/2) still solves pi P = pi, but then it means the long-run share of time in each state, not the distribution at a large fixed n."

L5. A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite

(a) Show that any covariance matrix Sigma is positive semidefinite. (b) Sigma = [4 2; 2 1]. Is it positive definite? What does that mean for X_1 and X_2?

Solution. (a) For any vector a: a^T Sigma a = Var(a^T X) >= 0. A variance is never negative. (b) det = 4 - 4 = 0, eigenvalues 5 and 0: semidefinite, not definite. Take a = (1, -2): a^T Sigma a = 4 - 8 + 4 = 0, so Var(X_1 - 2 X_2) = 0. X_1 = 2 X_2 + constant: one variable is a linear function of the other (correlation exactly 1).

Say it. "a transpose Sigma a is the variance of a transpose X, which can't be negative. A zero eigenvalue means some combination of the variables has zero variance, so they're exactly linearly related."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said the correlation is exactly 1. Show me that from the matrix."
    You: "Correlation is the covariance over the product of the standard deviations, so 2 over root 4 times root 1, which is 2 over 2, exactly 1. It matches the zero eigenvalue, whose eigenvector (1, minus 2) says that X1 minus 2 X2 doesn't vary at all."
  2. Prof: "So Sigma has no inverse. What breaks if you use it in a normal likelihood or a regression?"
    You: "The normal density needs Sigma inverse and divides by the square root of det Sigma, and both fail when the determinant is zero. In a regression with an intercept and X1 and X2 as predictors, X transpose X is singular, so the coefficients aren't identifiable: many different sets of coefficients give exactly the same fit. I'd drop one of them or combine them."
  3. Prof: "Real data are rarely exactly singular. How would you spot near-collinearity, and where could it hurt your plan?"
    You: "I'd compute the variance inflation factor, 1 over (1 minus R squared), from regressing one predictor on the others; above about 10 is a warning sign. In my field-data model each merged change gets L j times e to the beta transpose z j expected faults. I haven't fixed the covariates z yet, but size measures like files changed and commits per pull request would likely move together, so I'd check this before reading any single coefficient."

L6. Gradient, Hessian and a minimum

f(x, y) = x^2 + x y + 2 y^2 - 4x. Find the critical point and decide whether it is a minimum.

Solution.

grad f = (2x + y - 4, x + 4y) = (0, 0)
Second equation: x = -4y.  First: -8y + y = 4, so y = -4/7, x = 16/7.
Hessian H = [2 1; 1 4]: 2 > 0 and det = 8 - 1 = 7 > 0, so positive definite (eigenvalues 3 +- sqrt 2).
f(16/7, -4/7) = 256/49 - 64/49 + 32/49 - 448/49 = -224/49 = -32/7.

Minimum at (16/7, -4/7), value -32/7. Since H is constant and positive definite, it is the global minimum.

Link. Fitting a two-parameter model (GO, beta-binomial) by Newton-Raphson uses exactly this: the gradient of log L and its Hessian. A negative definite Hessian of log L means a maximum, and its inverse (with a minus sign) estimates the covariance of the MLE.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You linked this to Newton-Raphson. How many Newton steps would this f need?"
    You: "One step from any starting point. Newton minimises the quadratic Taylor model, and here f is itself quadratic, so the model is exact. From (0, 0) the gradient is (minus 4, 0), and minus H inverse times it is (16/7, minus 4/7)."
  2. Prof: "A Goel-Okumoto log-likelihood is not quadratic. What can go wrong with Newton there?"
    You: "Far from the maximum the Hessian may not be negative definite, so a step can head to a saddle point or overshoot, even to a negative b or omega. Good starting values and step halving help. pysrat fits NHPP models with the EM algorithm instead, which never lowers the likelihood from one step to the next, though it converges more slowly."
  3. Prof: "EM gives you no Hessian. How do you get standard errors then?"
    You: "I'd compute the Hessian of the log-likelihood at the EM estimate numerically, by finite differences, and invert its negative. Or I'd bootstrap. In my plan I bootstrap anyway, resampling runs, because the same runs meet every input, so the inputs are not independent observations."

L7. Lagrange multipliers: combining two estimates

Two independent unbiased estimates of the same quantity have variances sigma_1^2 = 1 and sigma_2^2 = 4. You combine them as w_1 X_1 + w_2 X_2 with w_1 + w_2 = 1. Which weights minimise the variance? Then, as a second use, find the MLE of multinomial probabilities.

Solution.

Minimise w_1^2 sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 sigma_2^2 subject to w_1 + w_2 = 1.
L = w_1^2 sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 sigma_2^2 - lam (w_1 + w_2 - 1)
dL/dw_i = 2 w_i sigma_i^2 - lam = 0  ->  w_i = lam / (2 sigma_i^2), proportional to 1/sigma_i^2
w = (1, 1/4) / (5/4) = (0.8, 0.2),  minimum variance = 0.64 (1) + 0.04 (4) = 0.8 = 1/(1/1 + 1/4)

Weights 0.8 and 0.2 (inverse-variance weighting), variance 0.8. Multinomial: maximise sum n_i log p_i subject to sum p_i = 1. n_i / p_i = lam, so p_i = n_i / lam, and the constraint gives lam = n. p_i-hat = n_i / n.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Inverse-variance weights assume the two estimates are independent. What if they are correlated, with correlation rho?"
    You: "Then the variance gains a term 2 w1 w2 rho sigma1 sigma2, and the best w1 becomes sigma2 times (sigma2 minus rho sigma1), over (sigma1 squared plus sigma2 squared minus 2 rho sigma1 sigma2). With our numbers and rho = 0.5, w1 is exactly 1 and the variance stays 1: the noisier estimate adds nothing. Above 0.5 its weight even turns negative."
  2. Prof: "So correlation limits what combining buys you. What does that mean for voting over many agent runs?"
    You: "If N estimates each have variance sigma squared and pairwise correlation rho, their average has variance sigma squared times (1 plus (N minus 1) rho), over N, which never falls below rho sigma squared. Voting has the same kind of floor: as N grows, a majority vote fails on roughly the share of inputs where theta, the chance a run fails on that input, is above one half."
  3. Prof: "Give me numbers. Mean failure probability 0.1 and rho 0.2."
    You: "With a Beta model, rho is 1 over (a plus b plus 1), so a plus b is 4 and theta is Beta(0.4, 3.6). Then E of theta squared is p squared plus rho p (1 minus p), 0.028, and E of theta cubed is 0.0112, so a 2-out-of-3 vote fails with 3 times 0.028 minus 2 times 0.0112, which is 0.0616. That's more than double the value for independent runs, which by chance is also 0.028. However many runs I add, it only falls toward about 0.023, the chance that theta is above one half."

L8. Integrating x e^(-ax), with and without a finite limit

(a) integral_0^infinity x e^(-ax) dx. (b) integral_0^T x e^(-ax) dx. Evaluate (b) for a = 0.5, T = 4. (NAIST C20(b) does part (a) in the form lambda x e^(-lambda x).)

Solution. By parts with u = x, dv = e^(-ax) dx, so du = dx and v = -e^(-ax)/a.

(a) [-x e^(-ax)/a]_0^infinity + (1/a) integral_0^infinity e^(-ax) dx = 0 + 1/a^2
(b) [-x e^(-ax)/a]_0^T + (1/a) [-e^(-ax)/a]_0^T = -T e^(-aT)/a + (1 - e^(-aT))/a^2
                                             = 1/a^2 - e^(-aT) (T/a + 1/a^2)
a = 0.5, T = 4:  4 - e^(-2) (8 + 4) = 4 - 12 e^(-2) = 2.376

(a) 1/a^2. (b) 2.376. With a = lambda, (a) times lambda is the exponential mean 1/lambda.

Check. Numerical integration gives 2.37598. At the boundary, x e^(-ax) tends to 0 as x tends to infinity, because the exponential wins.

Say it. "By parts, u equals x and dv equals e to the minus a x. The boundary term vanishes, and what is left is one over a times the integral of e to the minus a x, so one over a squared."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You gave part (a) a meaning. What does part (b) mean when a is a failure rate?"
    You: "Times lambda, part (b) is the expected value of X counted only when X is at most T, so the mean of X times the indicator that it failed by T. With lambda 0.5 and T = 4 that's 0.5 times 2.376, which is 1.188."
  2. Prof: "Then what is the mean failure time, given that the part failed before T?"
    You: "I divide by the chance of failing by T, 1 minus e to the minus 2, which is 0.865. So it's 1.188 over 0.865, about 1.374, well below the unconditional mean of 2, because conditioning cuts off the long lifetimes."
  3. Prof: "That's a truncated exponential. Where does its mean show up in software reliability?"
    You: "In fitting the Goel-Okumoto model to n failure times observed up to T. Setting the derivatives of the log-likelihood to zero gives omega hat equal to n over (1 minus e to the minus b T), and an equation saying the average failure time equals 1 over b, minus T e to the minus b T over (1 minus e to the minus b T). That right-hand side is exactly this truncated mean, so b hat is the rate whose truncated exponential matches the average of the data."

L9. Higher moments and the Gamma function

(a) integral_0^infinity x^2 e^(-ax) dx. (b) The general integral_0^infinity x^n e^(-ax) dx. (c) Var T for T ~ Exponential(lambda). (d) Gamma(5) and Gamma(3/2).

Solution. (a) By parts with u = x^2: the boundary term vanishes and you get (2/a) integral x e^(-ax) dx = (2/a)(1/a^2) = 2/a^3. (b) Substitute u = ax: integral_0^infinity x^n e^(-ax) dx = (1/a^(n+1)) integral_0^infinity u^n e^(-u) du = Gamma(n + 1)/a^(n+1) = n!/a^(n+1). (c) E[T^2] = lambda (2/lambda^3) = 2/lambda^2, so Var T = 2/lambda^2 - 1/lambda^2 = 1/lambda^2. (d) Gamma(5) = 4! = 24. Gamma(3/2) = (1/2) Gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi)/2 = 0.886 (used in the Weibull MTTF, file 04 R17 and R19; Gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi) comes from L14).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "So the standard deviation of an exponential equals its mean. How would you use that on real failure data?"
    You: "The coefficient of variation, standard deviation over mean, is exactly 1 for the exponential, so it's a quick check on the gaps between failures. Well above 1 suggests a mixture of exponentials, and well below 1 means the gaps are more regular than exponential. It only works if the failure rate isn't changing, though: during reliability growth the gaps get longer, and that alone pushes the CV up."
  2. Prof: "Which distribution gives a CV below 1? Use your Gamma integral."
    You: "The Gamma with shape k and rate lambda. From the integral, E of T is k over lambda and E of T squared is k times (k plus 1), over lambda squared, so the variance is k over lambda squared and the CV is 1 over root k. Shape 4 gives a CV of 0.5."
  3. Prof: "Where does the Gamma appear in our lab's software reliability models?"
    You: "As the fault-detection time distribution in the Gamma-type NHPP, where m of t is omega times the Gamma distribution function; pysrat has it as GammaNHPP. With shape 2 it is the delayed S-shaped model, omega times 1 minus (1 plus b t) e to the minus b t. Shape 2 means two exponential stages in a row, often read as detecting a failure and then isolating the fault, so the detection rate starts at zero and m of t has an S shape."

L10. y' = -a y, and constant-hazard reliability

(a) Solve y' = -a y with y(0) = y_0. (b) A part's reliability satisfies R'(t) = -lambda R(t), R(0) = 1, with lambda = 0.002 per hour. Find R(500) and the time at which R = 0.9.

Solution. (a) Separate: dy/y = -a dt, so ln|y| = -at + C and y = y_0 e^(-at). Half-life ln 2 / a. (b) R(t) = e^(-lambda t): R(500) = e^(-1) = 0.368. R = 0.9 when t = -ln(0.9)/0.002 = 52.7 h. The equation says the failure rate per surviving unit, -R'/R, is constant: that is the hazard, and constant hazard means exponential life.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "The part reaches 0.9 at 52.7 hours. Suppose the job needs 0.99 over those 52.7 hours. What are your options?"
    You: "With one part I'd need a failure rate of minus ln 0.99 over 52.7, about 0.00019 per hour, roughly a tenth of this one. Or I put two of these parts in parallel: each fails with probability 0.1, so if they fail independently both fail with 0.01 and the pair reaches 0.99."
  2. Prof: "And if 10 percent of each part's failure rate comes from a shock that hits both at once?"
    You: "Then each part has its own rate 0.0018 and they share a rate 0.0002. The pair survives if the shared shock doesn't come, e to the minus 0.0105, about 0.9895, and the two own clocks don't both fire, 1 minus 0.0905 squared, about 0.9918. The product is 0.981, so the chance of losing the pair nearly doubles, from 1 percent to 1.9 percent, and no number of extra parts can push it above 0.9895."
  3. Prof: "Where have you seen that model, and what does it have to do with your plan?"
    You: "It's the Marshall-Olkin model, which Prof. Dohi, Prof. Zheng and Prof. Okamura used in their 2021 note on a two-unit parallel system, where a shared clock moves both units down a deterioration level together. My plan asks the same question about agent runs, except the shared shock is a hard input rather than a moment in time. So instead of a shared rate I measure how much theta, the chance a run fails on an input, varies across inputs, and that sets how much extra runs can help."

L11. Goel-Okumoto from a differential equation

Assume faults are found at a rate proportional to the number still in the code: m'(t) = b (omega - m(t)), m(0) = 0. Solve it. What if the rate b(t) changes with time?

Solution.

Let r(t) = omega - m(t), the expected remaining faults. Then r' = -m' = -b r, r(0) = omega.
So r(t) = omega e^(-bt), and m(t) = omega (1 - e^(-bt)).        (Goel-Okumoto)
With b(t):  r' = -b(t) r  ->  r(t) = omega exp(-integral_0^t b(u) du)
            m(t) = omega [1 - exp(-integral_0^t b(u) du)] = omega F(t),
where F is the distribution whose hazard rate is b(t).

So every finite-failure NHPP m(t) = omega F(t) says "the detection rate per remaining fault is the hazard of F". For the delayed S-shaped model, b(t) = b^2 t / (1 + b t), which rises from 0 toward b.

Check. sympy's dsolve returns omega - omega e^(-bt).

Say it. "If each remaining fault is found at rate b, the remaining count decays exponentially, so the found count is omega times 1 minus e to the minus b t. That is the Goel-Okumoto model."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Your differential equation only gives the mean m of t. Why is the number of faults found by t Poisson?"
    You: "Suppose the initial number of faults is Poisson with mean omega, and each fault is found at an independent random time with distribution F. Keeping only the faults found by t is thinning a Poisson count, so the number found is Poisson with mean omega F of t. If the number of faults were a fixed N instead, the count would be binomial, as in the Jelinski-Moranda model."
  2. Prof: "Then what can you say about the faults still left after testing up to time T?"
    You: "They're Poisson too, with mean omega e to the minus b T, and independent of how many were found. So the chance that none is left is e to the minus omega e to the minus b T. With omega 100, b 0.05 per day and 100 days of testing, about 0.67 faults are expected to remain and the chance of none is about 0.51."
  3. Prof: "Isn't it strange that the number you found tells you nothing about how many remain?"
    You: "It is, and it comes from the Poisson count with omega treated as known; with a fixed N, by contrast, every fault found is one fewer left. In practice omega is estimated from the found count, so the data do inform the remaining count, and a Bayesian NHPP with a prior on omega makes that uncertainty explicit. Hirata, Okamura and Dohi described a Bayesian inference tool for NHPP models in 2009."

L12. A double integral: which happens first?

Failure time X ~ Exponential(lambda) and an independent repair-crew arrival Y ~ Exponential(mu). Find P(X < Y) as a double integral.

Solution.

P(X < Y) = integral_{y=0}^{infinity} integral_{x=0}^{y} lambda e^(-lambda x) mu e^(-mu y) dx dy
         = integral_0^infinity (1 - e^(-lambda y)) mu e^(-mu y) dy
         = 1 - mu/(lambda + mu) = lambda/(lambda + mu)

P(X < Y) = lambda/(lambda + mu). With lambda = 1, mu = 2: 1/3. This is the "race" between two exponential clocks that every CTMC uses (file 02, P14).

Check. Doing the double integral symbolically gives 1/3.

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "When the failure wins the race, how long did the race take on average?"
    You: "The race ends at the minimum of X and Y, which is exponential with rate lambda plus mu, so the mean is 1 over (lambda plus mu), a third with lambda 1 and mu 2. It's the same whether the failure or the crew wins, because the winner and the finishing time are independent."
  2. Prof: "Why are the winner and the time independent? Show it."
    You: "P of X less than Y with the minimum above t is the integral from t to infinity of lambda e to the minus lambda x, times e to the minus mu x. That is lambda over (lambda plus mu), times e to the minus (lambda plus mu) t, which is P of X less than Y times P of the minimum above t, so it factors. It's why a CTMC can be simulated by drawing the holding time first and then picking the next state with probabilities proportional to the rates."
  3. Prof: "That all needs exponential clocks. What if the crew always arrives after exactly half an hour?"
    You: "Then the failure wins with probability 1 minus e to the minus 0.5, about 0.39, higher than the one third from an exponential crew with the same mean. The function 1 minus e to the minus y is concave, so by Jensen's inequality averaging it over a random arrival time gives less than plugging in the mean. A fixed delay also breaks the Markov property, so in a full system model I'd use a Markov regenerative process, as Prof. Zheng does for software rejuvenation."

L13. Swapping the order of integration

Compute integral_0^1 integral_x^1 e^(y^2) dy dx.

Solution. e^(y^2) has no elementary antiderivative, so do not integrate in y first. The region is 0 <= x <= y <= 1. Swap the order: for each y in [0, 1], x runs from 0 to y.

integral_0^1 integral_0^y e^(y^2) dx dy = integral_0^1 y e^(y^2) dy = [ e^(y^2)/2 ]_0^1 = (e - 1)/2 = 0.859

Check. Numerical integration in the original order (y inside) gives 0.85914.

Say it. "The inner integral has no closed form, so I draw the region, a triangle under the line x = y, and swap the order. Then the inner integral is just y, and the outer one is a simple substitution."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "When are you allowed to swap the order of integration?"
    You: "By Tonelli's theorem whenever the integrand is non-negative, and by Fubini's whenever the integral of its absolute value is finite. Here e to the y squared is positive and bounded on a bounded triangle, so it's safe. A classic failure is (x squared minus y squared) over (x squared plus y squared) squared, on the unit square: one order gives pi over 4 and the other minus pi over 4."
  2. Prof: "Where do you use that swap in reliability?"
    You: "To show that the mean time to failure is the integral of the reliability function. I write T as the integral from 0 to infinity of the indicator that T is greater than t, take expectations, and swap the expectation with the integral, which Tonelli allows because everything is non-negative. That gives E of T equals the integral of P of T greater than t, which is R of t."
  3. Prof: "Use it: the MTTF of two identical units in parallel, each exponential with rate lambda."
    You: "The system survives if either unit does, so R of t is 2 e to the minus lambda t, minus e to the minus 2 lambda t. Integrating gives 2 over lambda minus 1 over 2 lambda, which is 3 over 2 lambda. With lambda 0.002 per hour that's 750 hours against 500 for one unit: the first failure comes after 250 hours on average, then the survivor lasts another 500."

L14. The Gaussian integral

Show integral_{-infinity}^{infinity} e^(-x^2) dx = sqrt(pi). Why does the normal density integrate to 1?

Solution.

I^2 = integral integral e^(-(x^2 + y^2)) dx dy        (two copies, independent variables)
    = integral_0^(2 pi) integral_0^infinity e^(-r^2) r dr d(angle)    (polar: dx dy = r dr d(angle))
    = 2 pi [ -e^(-r^2)/2 ]_0^infinity = 2 pi (1/2) = pi
I = sqrt(pi) = 1.7725

Substituting x = u / sqrt(2) gives integral e^(-u^2/2) du = sqrt(2 pi), so the N(0, 1) density e^(-u^2/2)/sqrt(2 pi) integrates to 1. Substituting x^2 = t gives Gamma(1/2) = sqrt(pi).

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "Why is there an extra r in dx dy = r dr d(angle)?"
    You: "It's the Jacobian determinant of x = r cos theta, y = r sin theta, which works out to r. Geometrically, a small polar patch has one side dr and the other r d theta, because an arc at radius r is r times the angle. That r is also what lets me integrate e to the minus r squared in closed form."
  2. Prof: "Use the same change of variables on two independent standard normals. What is the distribution of X squared plus Y squared?"
    You: "In polar form the joint density is 1 over 2 pi, times e to the minus r squared over 2, times r. Integrating out the angle, P of R greater than r is e to the minus r squared over 2, so R squared is exponential with mean 2, which is the chi-square with 2 degrees of freedom. Turned around, that's the Box-Muller method: R equal to the root of minus 2 ln U, with a uniform angle, gives two independent standard normals."
  3. Prof: "Would you generate the inputs for your plan with normal draws like that?"
    You: "Not as the main source. My plan builds inputs from the type hints and mixes random draws with the values in the pull request's tests. Any continuous draw, normal or uniform, almost never lands exactly on zero or on a limit in the code, so I also add boundary inputs as a stress test with the BCD method of Guo, Okamura and Dohi, taking the boundary points from branch conditions in the merged code."

L15. Limits and small-p approximations

(a) lim_{n -> infinity} (1 - lambda/n)^n. (b) lim_{x -> 0} (1 - e^(-bx))/x, and its meaning for the GO model. (c) Compare (1 - p)^n with e^(-np) for p = 0.001, n = 1000 and for p = 0.01, n = 100.

Solution. (a) ln[(1 - lambda/n)^n] = n ln(1 - lambda/n) = n (-lambda/n - lambda^2/(2n^2) - ...) -> -lambda. So the limit is e^(-lambda). (This is why the binomial tends to the Poisson.) (b) 0/0 form. L'Hopital: b e^(-bx)/1 -> b. Or Taylor: 1 - e^(-bx) = bx - (bx)^2/2 + ... So for small t, the GO m(t) = omega (1 - e^(-bt)) is about omega b t: at the start, failures come at rate omega b. (c) p = 0.001, n = 1000: 0.999^1000 = 0.36770, e^(-1) = 0.36788. p = 0.01, n = 100: 0.99^100 = 0.36603 against 0.36788. The approximation is better when p is smaller. Also 1 - (1 - p)^n is about np when np is small.

Say it. "ln of 1 minus p is about minus p for small p, so 1 minus p to the n is about e to the minus n p. That's the Poisson limit of the binomial, and it's how I'd estimate the chance that at least one of many inputs fails."

Follow-ups (each builds on your last answer)

  1. Prof: "You said 1 minus (1 minus p) to the n is about n p when n p is small. How small, and how big is the error?"
    You: "1 minus e to the minus n p is n p minus half the square of n p, plus smaller terms, so n p overestimates by a relative error of about n p over 2. At n p = 0.1, say p = 0.001 and n = 100, the exact value is 0.0952 against 0.1, about 5 percent too high. And n p is always an upper bound, by the union bound."
  2. Prof: "Does that union bound need the failures to be independent?"
    You: "No, it holds for any dependence, so n p is always safe. But the exact formula, 1 minus (1 minus p) to the n, needs independence. When failures are positively dependent, at least one failure is less likely than that formula says, because the failures pile up on the same cases."
  3. Prof: "Put numbers on that for three runs in your Beta model, with mean 0.1 and rho 0.2."
    You: "Theta is then Beta(0.4, 3.6). No run fails with E of (1 minus theta) cubed, which is 3.6 times 4.6 times 5.6 over 4 times 5 times 6, about 0.773, so at least one fails with 0.227 instead of 0.271. But all three fail with E of theta cubed, 0.0112, eleven times the independent 0.001. So shared hard inputs mean fewer inputs see any failure but many more see every run fail, and that's the case voting can't fix."
